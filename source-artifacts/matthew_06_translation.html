The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

Before Your Father Who Sees

ὁ πατήρ σου ὁ βλέπων ἐν τῷ κρυπτῷ — ho patēr sou ho blepōn en tō kryptō, “your Father, the one seeing in the hidden place”
Unit 6 · Matthew 6:1–34 · the middle panel of the Sermon on the Mount

The devotion panel — a concentric ring · 6:1–21

A heading, three matched case studies in hidden piety, and a closing bracket that answers the heading. The Lord’s Prayer sits at the dead center — not only of this ring, but of the whole Sermon.

A6:1
Don’t do y’all’s right-doing in front of people to be gazed at — or y’all have no wage with y’all’s Father — the heading: true right-doing, real wage
b6:2–4
Case 1 — Almsgiving. No trumpet · the actors have their wage in full · give in the hidden place — your Father who sees will pay you back
c6:5–15
Case 2 — Prayer. Not like the actors (5–6) · not like the nations (7–8) · THE PRAYER (9–13) · the release coda (14–15) — the panel swells; the pattern breaks; this is the pivot
b′6:16–18
Case 3 — Fasting. They un-appear their faces so as to appear · anoint and wash instead — your Father who sees will pay you back
A′6:19–21
Don’t treasure up treasures on earth where moth and eating un-appear them — where your treasure is, there your heart will be — true wealth, real wage

Why 6:19–21 closes the panel — one verb

The seam between the fasting case study and the treasure saying is usually read as a break. The Greek argues otherwise: the same rare verb does the work on both sides of it, and it is built out of the panel’s master word for “appear.”

VerseGreekWhat it says
6:16ἀφανίζουσιν τὰ πρόσωπα … ὅπως φανῶσινThey make their faces vanish so that they may appear. The alpha-privative ἀ- negates the very verb it stands beside: self-erasure performed for visibility.
6:19ὅπου σὴς καὶ βρῶσις ἀφανίζειWhere moth and eating make it vanish. The identical verb, now describing what decay does to hoarded wealth.
6:20ὅπου οὔτε σὴς οὔτε βρῶσις ἀφανίζειWhere neither moth nor eating makes it vanish. The treasury in the skies is the one place the verb has no purchase.

Movement One · 6:1–21 — Three practices before an audience of one

1“But watch out that y’all don’t do y’all’s right-doing1 in front of people, toward the end of being gazed at by them2; otherwise y’all have no wage alongside y’all’s Father, the one in the skies.

The opening word is a sentry’s word — προσέχετε (prosechete), “stand guard.” It returns at 7:15 to guard against false prophets. Note the direct collision with 5:16, where y’all’s light is to shine before people: same visibility, opposite terminus. Greek πρὸς τὸ θεαθῆναι αὐτοῖς (pros to theathēnai autois) Wooden “toward the end of being gazed at by them” NASB “to be noticed by them” Text-form Some manuscripts read ἐλεημοσύνην (eleēmosynēn, “almsgiving”) for δικαιοσύνην here — an ancient reader already hearing the Hebrew צְדָקָה (tsedaqah) behind the Greek, which means both “righteousness” and “charity.”

2“So whenever you do a mercy-deed3, don’t blow a trumpet ahead of yourself, the way the stage-actors do in the meeting-houses and in the alleys, so that they may be glorified by people. Amen I say to y’all: they have their wage in full4.

Notice the pronoun shift: verse 1 addressed y’all; verse 2 turns and addresses you, one person. The word for the villains, ὑποκριταί (hypokritai), is the ordinary Greek word for actors on a stage — men who speak from behind a mask. The modern moral sense of “hypocrite” came later.

3“But you, when you are doing a mercy-deed, don’t let your left hand know what your right hand is doing,

4so that your mercy-deed may be in the hidden place; and your Father, the one seeing in the hidden place, will pay you back5.”

Proverbial excess, not an instruction in anatomy. Chrysostom’s reading is the sane one: if it were somehow possible to hide it from yourself, do even that. The target is the private ledger you keep of your own generosity.

5“And whenever y’all pray, y’all will not be like the stage-actors, because they love to stand and pray in the meeting-houses and on the corners of the wide streets, so that they may appear to people. Amen I say to y’all: they have their wage in full.

Standing is the normal posture; the meeting-house is the normal place. The satire is in the corner — the spot chosen for its sightlines, so that the fixed hour of prayer and the maximum audience coincide by “accident.”

6“But you, whenever you pray, go into your storeroom6 and, having shut your door, pray to your Father, the one in the hidden place; and your Father, the one seeing in the hidden place, will pay you back.

Not a bedroom and not a “prayer closet.” ταμεῖον (tameion) is the inner storeroom where a household kept provisions and valuables — the one room in a Galilean house with a door that locked. The man who wants God to settle his account is sent into the strongroom.

7“And when praying, don’t babble-on7 the way the nations do, for they suppose that in their many-wordiness they will be listened to.

8So don’t be made like them, for y’all’s Father has known what things y’all have need of before y’all ask him.

The foil changes here from the actors to the nations, and the objection is to prayer as leverage — the theory that the deity must be worn down, that enough syllables compel a response. The nations return at 6:32 to close this frame.

9“So pray like this, y’all:

iOur Father, the one in the skies:
let your name be made holy8,

iilet your kingdom come9,

iiilet your will come to be —
as in sky, so also on earth.

ivOur bread, the epiousios one10, give to us today;

vand release for us our debts,
as we also have released our debtors;

viand don’t carry us into testing11,
but rescue us from the evil one12.

Two tablets of three. The first three end in σου (sou, “your”) and are third-person imperatives — a form English has no clean equivalent for, so the “let …” renderings are approximations of a real command directed at God. The last three are the “us” petitions: bread, debt, deliverance. Greek ὡς ἐν οὐρανῷ καὶ ἐπὶ γῆς (hōs en ouranō kai epi gēs) Wooden “as in sky, also on earth” NASB “On earth as it is in heaven” Note The Greek has no article and no verb — just the bare pairing. Grammatically the clause may govern the third petition only or all three; the ambiguity is probably deliberate. Greek ὁ ἐπιούσιος (ho epiousios) — a word with essentially no life outside this prayer NASB “our daily bread” Hart declines to domesticate it, rendering it along the lines of bread “for the coming day” rather than “daily” — a choice that keeps the word’s strangeness and its forward tilt visible. Text-form Jerome reports the Hebrew Gospel of the Nazoreans read maḥar, “tomorrow.”

14“For if y’all release for people their false steps, y’all’s heavenly Father will also release for y’all;

15but if y’all do not release for people, neither will y’all’s Father release y’all’s false steps.”

Six petitions are given and exactly one is expanded — which tells you which one Matthew expects to be the problem. Stated both ways, positive then negative, a Semitic device for absolute emphasis. This is the seed of 18:23–35.

16“And whenever y’all fast, don’t become gloomy-faced like the stage-actors, for they un-appear their faces so that they may appear to people as fasting13. Amen I say to y’all: they have their wage in full.

The wordplay is the whole point and English fights it. They erase themselves in order to be conspicuous.

17“But you, when fasting, oil your head and wash your face,

18so that you may not appear to people as fasting, but to your Father, the one in the hidden place; and your Father, the one seeing in the hidden place, will pay you back.

Not a command to conceal the fast so much as to dress for a feast while keeping it. Nothing here abolishes any of the three practices — the assumption throughout is when you give, when you pray, when you fast.

19“Don’t treasure up for yourselves treasures on the earth, where moth and eating make them vanish, and where thieves dig through and steal14;

20but treasure up for yourselves treasures in sky, where neither moth nor eating makes them vanish, and where thieves do not dig through nor steal.

21For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also15.”

The causation runs backwards from what we expect. Not: fix your heart and your money will follow. Rather: your heart follows your treasure — so relocate the assets and the self migrates after them. In Hebrew anthropology the heart is the seat of will and decision, not feeling. This is a claim about formation, not sentiment.

Movement Two · 6:22–34 — One eye, one master, no anxiety

22“The lamp of the body is the eye. So if your eye is single16, your whole body will be full of light;

23but if your eye is evil, your whole body will be full of darkness. If, then, the light in you is darkness — the darkness, how much!”

The last clause has no verb in the Greek. It is a broken exclamation, and English versions that smooth it into a complete sentence lose the fracture. Light dawned at 4:16, was handed to the disciples at 5:14, and can curdle back into darkness here — with money as the mechanism. Greek ὀφθαλμὸς ἁπλοῦς … ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός (ophthalmos haplous … ophthalmos ponēros) Wooden “single eye … evil eye” NASB “if your eye is clear … if your eye is bad” Idiom עַיִן רָעָה (ayin ra‘ah), “evil eye,” is a fixed Hebrew idiom for stinginess — see Deut 15:9, Prov 28:22, and Matthew’s own 20:15. Read idiomatically the saying is about open-handedness, which is why it sits between the treasure saying and the mammon saying rather than interrupting them.

24“No one is able to slave for two masters17; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will hold fast to one and look down on the other. Y’all are not able to slave for God and for Mammon.”

Not two employers — δουλεύω (douleuō) is slave-service, and a slave has one owner by definition. This is not a saying about difficulty but about impossibility. “Hate” and “love” here are Semitic idiom for rejection and preference, not emotion. Greek οὐ δύνασθε θεῷ δουλεύειν καὶ μαμωνᾷ (ou dynasthe theō douleuein kai mamōna) NASB “You cannot serve God and wealth.” Note Matthew leaves the Aramaic מָמוֹנָא (mamona, “property, assets”) untranslated when πλοῦτος (ploutos, “wealth”) was available. Leaving it foreign makes it sound like a name — and the parallelism makes it sound like a rival god’s name. Translating it “wealth” is accurate and drains the personification out.

25“Because of this I say to y’all: don’t be pulled apart with care18 for y’all’s life — what y’all will eat — nor for y’all’s body — what y’all will put on. Isn’t the life more than the food, and the body more than the clothing?

“Because of this” — the anxiety discourse is a consequence of the two-masters saying, not a new topic. If Mammon cannot be your master, the fear that keeps you serving it has to be dealt with.

26Look at the birds of the sky: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and y’all’s heavenly Father feeds them. Aren’t y’all worth more than they?

27And which of y’all, by being pulled apart with care, is able to add one forearm’s length to his span19?

28And about clothing — why are y’all pulled apart with care? Study the wildflowers of the field, how they grow: they do not labor nor spin.

The three verbs at v. 26 — sow, reap, gather into barns — are the male agricultural cycle; the two verbs here — labor, spin — are the female textile cycle. Both halves of the peasant economy, deliberately paired. The κρίνα (krina) are probably not lilies in our sense but the brilliant anemones of a Galilean spring.

29But I say to y’all that not even Solomon in all his glory was wrapped like one of these.

30And if God so clothes the grass of the field — here today and tomorrow thrown into an oven — will he not much more clothe y’all, y’all little-faiths20?

Three arguments running light-to-heavy, the rabbinic qal vaḥomer: if the greater gift is given, the lesser follows.

31So don’t be pulled apart with care, saying ‘What will we eat?’ or ‘What will we drink?’ or ‘What will we wrap ourselves in?’

32For all these things the nations seek after; for y’all’s heavenly Father has known that y’all need all of these.

33But seek first the kingdom and his right-doing21, and all these things will be added to y’all.

The chapter’s first word returns as its last argument. δικαιοσύνη opened at 6:1 — don’t perform it — and closes here — seek it first. That inclusio is the frame of Unit 6. Greek ζητεῖτε δὲ πρῶτον τὴν βασιλείαν καὶ τὴν δικαιοσύνην αὐτοῦ NASB “But seek first His kingdom and His righteousness” Text-form The SBLGNT reads simply “the kingdom,” with no τοῦ θεοῦ (“of God”) — so the “his” of “his right-doing” reaches back to the Father of v. 32. Many manuscripts supply “of God”; the shorter text is likely original and leaves the possessive doing double duty.

34So don’t be pulled apart with care for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be pulled apart with care about itself. Enough for the day is its own trouble22.”

The prayer asked for bread for one day; the chapter closes by telling you to live in one day. The two ends of Matthew 6 give the same instruction in two registers.

The ledger running under the chapter

Matthew 6 is built out of commercial vocabulary from its first sentence to its last. Reading it as a chapter about accounts, rather than about “spirituality,” is what makes the mammon saying land where it does.

GreekWhereWhat it actually means
μισθός (misthos)6:1, 2, 5, 16A day laborer’s wage. Not a vague “reward” — the coin handed over at sundown.
ἀπέχουσιν (apechousin)6:2, 5, 16The commercial receipt formula from the papyri: paid in full, nothing further owed. The account is closed.
ἀποδώσει (apodōsei)6:4, 6, 18“Will pay back” — a settlement verb, the same root as 5:26 and 18:25–34.
ὀφειλήματα (opheilēmata)6:12Actual monetary obligations; behind it Aramaic חוֹבָא (ḥoba), which serves for both “debt” and “sin.”
ταμεῖον (tameion)6:6The household strongroom. The place you go to pray is the place you keep the valuables.
θησαυρός (thēsauros)6:19–21Treasury or hoard. Two storehouses, one perishable, one not.
δουλεύειν (douleuein)6:24To slave for. Not employment — ownership. Hence: only one.

Treasure in the skies — a living idiom, not a new coinage

Jesus is speaking a phrase his hearers already knew. The interesting question is what he adds to it.

SourceWhat it says
Sirach 29:11–12Lay up your treasure according to the commandments; store almsgiving in your storehouse and it will rescue you from disaster.
Tobit 4:9Giving to the poor lays up a good treasure against the day of necessity.
King Monobazus (rabbinic tradition)Accused of squandering the family stores on Jerusalem’s poor, he answers that his fathers laid up treasure in a place a hand can reach, and he laid it up where no hand can.
Matthew 6:19–21Same idiom — plus the reversal at v. 21: the heart follows the treasure, not the other way around. That is the sentence with no parallel.

What the first three petitions are asking for

“Let your name be made holy” is not a compliment paid to God. It is a passive whose implied agent is God himself, and it has a specific prophetic address.

Ezekiel 36:22–23 name profaned among the nations→ “I will sanctify my great name” God acts→ Exile ends, people restored the mechanism→ Matt 6:9–10 do it now

Read this way the first three petitions are one petition asked three times: vindicate your name · establish your reign · let your will be done here. Behind them stands the Exodus frame — “Israel is my firstborn son”, a name made known so Egypt would know, bread given one day at a time, and rescue.

Notes

  1. δικαιοσύνη, “right-doing” (v. 1). In classical Greek this word means “justice” and never “charity.” But in the Septuagint it is the standard rendering of Hebrew צְדָקָה (tsedaqah), which by the Second Temple period meant both “righteousness” in general and, specifically, almsgiving. Tobit 12:9 sets ἐλεημοσύνη (eleēmosynē, “alms”) and δικαιοσύνη in synonymous parallelism, which shows the overlap was live in Greek-speaking Jewish usage. This is why 6:1 can head a section whose first case study is giving to the poor, and why some manuscripts simply substitute “almsgiving” here. I have rendered it “right-doing” throughout to keep the deed-sense audible against the more abstract “righteousness.” ↗
  2. θεαθῆναι and ὑποκριταί (vv. 1–2). Two theatre words back to back. θεάομαι (theaomai) is to behold as spectacle — the root of θέατρον, theatre. ὑποκριτής (hypokritēs) is the ordinary Greek word for a stage actor, one who answers from behind a mask; the moral sense of “hypocrite” is a later development driven largely by this very passage. Matthew is not primarily accusing anyone of inconsistency between word and deed. He is accusing them of performing for the wrong house.
  3. ἐλεημοσύνη, “mercy-deed” (v. 2). Built on ἔλεος (eleos), mercy — the LXX’s regular word for Hebrew חֶסֶד (chesed), covenant loyalty. So almsgiving in this vocabulary is not philanthropy but enacted covenant faithfulness, which is why the same word-family runs through the fifth beatitude (5:7) and the Hosea citation Jesus twice quotes at 9:13 and 12:7.
  4. ἀπέχουσιν τὸν μισθὸν αὐτῶν (vv. 2, 5, 16). The decisive evidence is documentary, not literary. Deissmann showed from the Egyptian papyri and ostraca that ἀπέχω is the standard formula stamped on a receipt certifying payment received in full and the obligation discharged; the cognate noun ἀποχή (apochē) simply means “receipt.” So the line is not “they already got a small reward” but “they can sign for it — the account is closed, nothing further is owed.” Alford cites a Plutarch parallel with the identical phrase. ↗
  5. “In secret … openly” (vv. 4, 6). The Textus Receptus adds ἐν τῷ φανερῷ (en tō phanerō, “openly”) at the end of vv. 4 and 6, whence the King James “shall reward thee openly.” Augustine already noted that the older Greek copies lacked it. The shorter text is almost certainly original, and losing the addition matters: the promise is not that hidden piety will eventually be publicized, only that it is seen.
  6. ταμεῖον, “storeroom” (v. 6). Not a bedroom. The ταμεῖον is the inner chamber where provisions and valuables were kept — in a modest Galilean house, the only room with a lockable door. There may be an LXX resonance with Isaiah 26:20, where the people are told to enter their chambers and shut their doors until the wrath passes; the vocabulary overlaps, though the allusion is not certain. Either way, the placement is pointed: the man seeking payment from God is sent into the strongroom. ↗
  7. βατταλογήσητε, “babble-on” (v. 7). A rare and probably onomatopoeic verb — batta-batta, stammering, or heaping up syllables — paired with πολυλογία (polylogia, “many-wordiness”). The target is prayer as technique. Surviving Greek magical papyri string long chains of divine names on precisely the theory being rejected here: that more names mean more leverage. Against that: the Father already knows. The implied question — then why ask at all? — is answered by the structure rather than argued: prayer here is not information transfer but the maintenance of a relation whose basic term is “Father.”
  8. ἁγιασθήτω τὸ ὄνομά σου (v. 9). A third-person aorist passive imperative — an imperative addressed, in effect, to God. In Jewish idiom the implied agent of such a passive is God himself. The address is Ezekiel 36:22–23, where God declares he will act not for Israel’s sake but for the sake of his holy name, profaned among the nations, and will sanctify that great name so the nations know he is the LORD — a sanctification accomplished by ending the exile. The petition therefore asks God to do that, now. The Aramaic Kaddish opens on the same two notes in the same order — magnify and sanctify the great name; establish the kingdom — which places this prayer squarely inside the synagogue’s own liturgical vocabulary rather than over against it.
  9. ἐλθέτω ἡ βασιλεία σου (v. 10) — the live disagreement. Constable reads this as necessarily and only future: the kingdom is the earthly millennial reign, presently in abeyance because Israel rejected it, so the petition asks for the second advent; he argues, citing Burrows approvingly, that the plain future force of ἐλθέτω should not be re-read through the single realized-eschatology saying at 12:28. That is a fair methodological objection. But it strains against Matthew’s own narrative: the man teaching this prayer opened his ministry announcing ἤγγικεν ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν (4:17) in the perfect tense — arrived and standing — and will shortly say that if he casts out demons by God’s Spirit, the kingdom has come upon his hearers. France and Wright read the petition as asking for the completion of something inaugurated rather than the initiation of something wholly absent, and Wright presses hard on “as in sky, so on earth” as the clause a purely future, purely otherworldly reading cannot absorb. The honest position: the aorist genuinely looks forward and the surrounding narrative genuinely says something has begun. The prayer belongs to people who have seen the start and are asking for the finish.
  10. ἐπιούσιος (v. 11). The most famous crux in New Testament lexicography — a word appearing here, in Luke’s parallel, and essentially nowhere else in surviving Greek. Origen said the evangelists coined it; Moulton and Milligan concluded the papyri shed no clear light. Three derivations compete: from ἐπὶ + οὐσία, “for subsistence,” hence “the bread we need”; from ἡ ἐπιοῦσα (ἡμέρα), “the coming day,” hence “tomorrow’s bread, today”; and from ἐπιέναι, “to come upon,” hence bread of the age to come. Each pulls the prayer somewhere different — a laborer’s wage-prayer, a manna prayer (Exodus 16:4–5, where gathering more than one day’s worth is forbidden and the surplus rots), or a request for a seat at the messianic banquet now. I have left the word untranslated in the text so the choice stays visible. Constable’s observation is worth keeping alongside the theology: Galilean day laborers were in fact paid daily, so “bread for the day, today” describes an actual economic rhythm.
  11. μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν (v. 13). πειρασμός (peirasmos) covers both “temptation” and “trial, testing.” In the LXX it is the wilderness word, and there God is explicitly the tester — Deut 8:2, “to test you, to know what was in your heart.” Matthew has already used the cognate of Jesus himself: at 4:1 the Spirit leads him up εἰς τὸ πειρασθῆναι, “to be tested.” So the petition cannot mean “God never tests.” It means something nearer “do not bring us to the trial we would fail” — and it is answered narratively at Gethsemane, where Jesus tells the disciples to pray exactly this and then prays the opposite for himself.
  12. ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ, “from the evil one” (v. 13). The genitive is formally ambiguous between neuter (“evil”) and masculine (“the evil one”). Matthew’s own usage decides it: at 13:19 and 13:38 ὁ πονηρός is unmistakably personal. Within the Sermon the word is a live keyword — 5:37, 5:39 (τῷ πονηρῷ), here, and again at 6:23 of the eye. The doxology (“for yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory”) is absent from the earliest manuscripts — Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Codex Dublinensis, the fragment 0170 — and the sheer variety of its forms where it does appear marks it as a liturgical growth, modelled on 1 Chronicles 29:11. Edersheim notes that in the temple the congregation answered prayers not with “Amen” but with a benediction blessing the name of the glory of God’s kingdom forever. What we have, then, is a fossil of very early worship rather than a corruption — and its absence from Matthew’s original is no reason to stop saying it.
  13. ἀφανίζουσιν … ὅπως φανῶσιν (v. 16) — the chapter’s best pun. φαίνω (phainō) means to appear, shine, be visible. ἀφανίζω (aphanizō) is that same root with an alpha-privative prefixed: to make un-appear, obliterate, render invisible. Set side by side the sentence reads: they make their faces vanish so that they may be seen. The practical referent is the customary disfigurement of a fast — unwashed face, ashes, disheveled hair. The structural payoff comes three verses later, where the identical verb describes what moth and vermin do to hoarded treasure (vv. 19–20). The self-erasure of the performer and the erosion of the treasury are, in Matthew’s Greek, the same process — which is a real argument that 6:19–21 belongs with the panel above it rather than starting a new one. ↗
  14. Moth, eating, and digging (v. 19). The three threats match the three forms first-century wealth actually took. σής (sēs) is the clothes-moth, because fine textiles were a primary store of value. βρῶσις (brōsis) is literally “eating” — usually rendered “rust,” but the wider sense of devouring (vermin, corrosion, whatever consumes) is closer. And διορύσσουσιν (dioryssousin) means to dig through: in a world of mud-brick walls you did not pick a lock, you tunnelled. The image is precise, not generic.
  15. “There your heart will be also” (v. 21). καρδία (kardia) renders Hebrew לֵב (lev), which in Israelite anthropology is the seat of will, thought, and decision — the executive centre of a person, not the seat of feeling. So the claim is not sentimental but formative: relocate your assets and your self will follow them there. This is the logic that makes the hidden-treasure and pearl parables work at 13:44–46, and that makes the rich man’s price at 19:21 — “you will have treasure in the skies” — an intelligible offer rather than a cruelty.
  16. ἁπλοῦς, “single” (v. 22). The word means “single, simple, unfolded,” and in Jewish Greek the ἁπλότης (haplotēs) word-group carries the settled sense of generosity, open-handedness — see Paul at Romans 12:8, 2 Corinthians 8:2 and 9:11. Its opposite here, “an evil eye,” is a fixed Hebrew idiom for stinginess and grudging. Read idiomatically, then, 6:22–23 says the generous person is full of light and the stingy person full of darkness — which explains what otherwise looks like a non sequitur wedged between two money sayings. Constable takes it instead as single-minded devotion, linking to 5:8, which is traditional and defensible; the best reading probably lets generosity carry the weight with undividedness as its overtone, since in this chapter’s logic those are the same posture. Ancient extramission optics — the eye as emitter rather than receiver of light — makes the lamp metaphor work without strain.
  17. Mammon (v. 24). μαμωνᾶς (mamōnas) transliterates Aramaic מָמוֹנָא (mamona), “wealth, property,” plausibly from the root behind “amen” — that in which one places trust. In the targums and in rabbinic usage the word is entirely neutral: assets. Jesus does not translate it and does not condemn it; he personifies it, setting it opposite God as the other κύριος and using slave-vocabulary for both. Sirach 31:8 blesses the rich man found blameless, so the tradition knew wealth as a test rather than a sin; some Qumran material associates wealth with Belial’s dominion. But the bare, unglossed antithesis — God, or Mammon — is Jesus’s own, and it is why the word entered English as a demon’s name.
  18. μεριμνάω, “be pulled apart with care” (vv. 25, 27, 28, 31, 34×2). Related to μερίζω (merizō), to divide — the sense is of a mind drawn in pieces. Six occurrences in ten verses, forming an inclusio: μὴ μεριμνᾶτε at v. 25, μὴ οὖν μεριμνήσητε at v. 34. The same verb strangles the seed at 13:22, where “the anxiety of the age and the deceit of wealth” choke the word — anxiety and money paired again, exactly as here. I have used the clumsy “pulled apart with care” to keep the division-sense visible; “worry” is smoother and loses it. ↗
  19. “One forearm’s length to his span” (v. 27). ἡλικία (hēlikia) means either bodily stature or length of life, and πῆχυς (pēchys) is a cubit — a measure of length, roughly forearm to fingertip. So the line is either “add eighteen inches to his height,” which is absurd and may be exactly the joke, or “add a cubit to his lifespan,” a mixed metaphor treating life as a length. The NASB takes the second (“a single hour to his life”); I have kept the literal measure and the ambiguity, since the comic reading is not out of place in a paragraph about birds and wildflowers.
  20. ὀλιγόπιστοι, “little-faiths” (v. 30). A compound essentially peculiar to Matthew, and always his epithet for disciples, never for outsiders — here, then 8:26 in the storm, 14:31 as Peter sinks, 16:8 over the bread. Worth noticing that its first appearance in the gospel is not attached to a failed miracle but to the household budget.
  21. “Seek first the kingdom and his right-doing” (v. 33). The SBLGNT reads τὴν βασιλείαν with no “of God,” so the possessive in “his right-doing” reaches back to the Father of v. 32; many manuscripts supply “of God,” and the shorter reading is likely original. ζητεῖτε is a present imperative — keep on seeking, an ongoing practice rather than a posture of waiting. Constable reads the verse as prioritizing a kingdom still wholly future; France and Wright read it as the summons to reorder present life around a reign already breaking in. The verb’s tense does not settle the question but leans toward a practice for now.
  22. “Enough for the day is its own trouble” (v. 34). κακία (kakia) here means hardship or badness of circumstance rather than moral evil. There is a close rabbinic parallel at b. Berakhot 9b warning against fretting over tomorrow’s trouble, since you cannot know what a day will bring — and the wider wisdom tradition says the same at Proverbs 27:1. Structurally the verse returns the chapter to ἐπιούσιος: the prayer asked for bread sufficient for one day, and the closing line commands a life lived in one day. Beginning and end of Matthew 6 give the same instruction in two keys.

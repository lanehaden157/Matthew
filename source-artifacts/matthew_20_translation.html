The Last Shall Be First

hoi eschatoi prōtoi kai hoi prōtoi eschatoi — the last first and the first last
Unit 20 · Matthew 20:1–34 · the vineyard wage, the cup, and two blind men who see

    The master of the house and the workers · 20:1–16

    1 For the kingdom of the skies is like a man, a master of the house, who went out at first light to hire workers into his vineyard.1

    For ties the story to 19:30. Hire (misthoomai) is the verb of wage (misthos): hiring and wage are one word-family.
    Isa 5:1–7; Jer 12:10 — Isaiah sings of a beloved’s vineyard that yields wild grapes, and says outright that “the vineyard of Yahweh of Armies is the house of Israel”; in Jeremiah many shepherds ruin God’s vineyard. The Greek of both uses Matthew’s word, ampelōn. A vineyard story about God already has Israel in it.

    2 And having agreed with the workers on a denarius for the day, he sent them into his vineyard.2

    Denarius (dēnarion): a silver coin, an ordinary day’s pay for a labourer, neither generous nor stingy.

    3 And going out about the third hour, he saw others standing workless in the marketplace;

    The third hour is about nine in the morning. Workless (argos, “without work”): not lazy, unhired.

    4 and to those he said, “Go, y’all too, into the vineyard, and whatever is right I will give y’all.”3

    No price is named for them, only whatever is right (dikaion): the reader is left wondering what “right” will come to.

    5 And they went off. Again, going out about the sixth and the ninth hour, he did likewise.

    Noon and three in the afternoon. The hirings shrink in the telling; only the first and the last will matter.

    6 And about the eleventh, going out, he found others standing, and he says to them, “Why have y’all stood here the whole day workless?”

    The eleventh hour, about five, an hour before sundown, breaks the three-hour pattern and draws the eye.

    7 They say to him, “Because no one hired us.” He says to them, “Go, y’all too, into the vineyard.”

    A day labourer unhired was a family unfed. These last are promised nothing at all.

    8 And when evening came, the master of the vineyard says to his steward, “Call the workers and pay them the wage, beginning from the last up to the first.”

    Master (kyrios), the word used of Jesus; steward (epitropos), only here in Matthew. Paying the last first lets the first watch.
    Lev 19:13; Deut 24:14–15 — the hired worker’s wage must not stay with you until morning; pay it the same day, before the sun goes down, for he is poor and sets his heart on it. Evening pay is the Law kept.

    9 And when those of about the eleventh hour came, they received a denarius each.

    10 And when the first came, they supposed that they would receive more; and they too received the denarius each.

    11 And having received it, they grumbled against the master of the house,4

    Grumbled (gongyzō), an imperfect: they kept on grumbling. The word sounds like what it names.
    Exod 16:7–8 — Israel grumbles for food in the wilderness, and Moses says “your grumbling is not against us but against God.” The Greek noun there (gongysmos) is from Matthew’s verb: a complaint against the one who provides.

    12 saying, “These last did one hour, and you made them equal to us, who carried the weight of the day and the scorching heat.”

    Scorching heat (kausōn) is also the word for a burning wind. Their charge is equal: the story will not deny it.
    Jonah 4:8–11 — God sends a scorching east wind (Greek kausōn) on Jonah, who is angry that God spared Nineveh; God asks whether he should not pity the great city. One who bore the heat resents mercy shown to latecomers. Tentative: a rare shared word and a shared situation, and Matthew gives no signal.

    David’s equal share 1 Sam 30:21–25

    David recovers the plunder taken from Ziklag. Two hundred of his men were too exhausted to cross the brook and stayed behind with the baggage. The worthless men among those who fought want to give them nothing but their wives and children. David refuses: what Yahweh has given is not theirs to hoard, and “as his share is who goes down to the battle, so shall his share be who stays with the baggage; they shall share alike.” It became a statute in Israel.

    • Those who did the hard part object to an equal share for those who did less.
    • The one who gives overrules them: the share is a gift, not earnings.

    No word is shared: the Greek of David’s ruling has no wage, no worker and no “equal”. David’s men had fought for the plunder; the master’s wage is his own money, freely given.

    An equal share by gift, not by work, was already part of Israel’s story and law. The grumblers stand where David’s worthless men stood. Tentative: Matthew never points to the story.

    13 But he answered one of them and said, “Friend, I am not wronging you. Did you not agree with me on a denarius?

    Friend (hetairos): civil, cool, perhaps because no name is known. Matthew uses it only for people in the wrong.

    The elder brother Luke 15:25–32

    In Luke’s parable the elder son comes in from the field, hears music for his returned brother, and is angry. He reminds his father of years of slaving and obedience, with never a young goat for a party, while “this son of yours” gets the fattened calf. The father answers, “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours,” and says it was right to celebrate.

    • A resentful early arrival complains about generosity to someone later and less deserving.
    • The one who gives answers him with a speech about his own goodness and what is rightly his to give.
    • The listener is drawn into the complainer’s seat.

    The elder son is family, and his father goes out and pleads with him; here a hired hand is told “take what is yours and go.” Luke’s story ends on joy at a lost son found; Matthew’s ends on the evil eye.

    Both stories aim at the person tempted to grumble, not at the latecomer. Matthew’s answer is sharper: the complaint is turned back on the eye that sees it.

    14 Take what is yours and go. But I want to give to this last one as also to you.

    Go (hypagō) was the invitation of 20:4 and 20:7; now it is a dismissal. I want (thelō): the master’s will settles it.

    15 Is it not permitted me to do what I want with what is mine? Or is your eye evil because I am good?”

    An evil eye is a grudging, envious eye that hates another’s good fortune. I am good answers 19:17: “One there is who is the good.”
    Greek (translit.)ē ho ophthalmos sou ponēros estin hoti egō agathos eimi
    This translation“Or is your eye evil because I am good?”
    NASB“Or is your eye envious because I am generous?”
    Note“Envious” and “generous” give the sense but lose the words. Kept wooden, the line sets “evil” against “good” and reaches back to the Sermon’s evil eye (6:23) and to “One there is who is the good” (19:17).
    Matt 6:22–23; Deut 15:9 — Jesus has said that if your eye is evil your whole body is dark. Deuteronomy warns against the eye turned evil toward a poor brother as the release year nears, “and you do not give to him” (Greek ponēreuomai, ophthalmos). The master gives; the evil eye begrudges the giving.

    16 So the last will be first and the first last.5

    The saying of 19:30 returns with its order turned, to fit a story in which the last were paid first.

    Mark 10:31; Luke 13:30 — which comes first

    Mark 10:31“But many first will be last, and the last first”: the order Matthew kept at 19:30.
    Luke 13:30“There are last who will be first, and there are first who will be last,” of those shut out at the door.
    SameThe reversal of first and last.
    DifferentOnly Matthew has the saying twice, around a parable; at 20:16 the order matches Luke’s, not Mark’s.

    Matthew wraps the vineyard parable in the saying and turns it round at the close, so the frame ends where the pay-line began, with the last.

    The third time · 20:17–19

    17 And as Jesus was going up to Jerusalem, he took the twelve learners aside privately, and on the road he said to them,

    Going up (anabainō), the pilgrim’s word for the climb to Jerusalem; the road is the private classroom.

    18 “Look, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of Man will be handed over to the chief priests and scribes, and they will judge against him for death,6

    We are going up: the learners are not spectators. Judge against (katakrinō), the condemning verb.

    19 and they will hand him over to the nations to mock and to scourge and to crucify, and on the third day he will be raised.”7

    Crucify (stauroō), the verb of the cross the follower takes up (10:38; 16:24), named in a prediction for the first time.

    Mark 10:32–34; Luke 18:31–34 — the third prediction

    Mark 10:32–34Jesus walks ahead; they are amazed and those following are afraid. He will be mocked, spat on, scourged and killed, and “after three days” he will rise.
    Luke 18:31–34“All the things written through the prophets” will be completed; he will be mocked, insulted, spat on, scourged and killed. “They understood none of these things.”
    SameGoing up to Jerusalem, handed over, the Gentiles, mocking and scourging, rising.
    DifferentMatthew drops the amazement and fear and the spitting, says “crucify” for “kill” and “on the third day” for Mark’s “after three days,” and has no line about their not understanding.

    Matthew’s prediction is the shortest and the closest to what will happen. He does not say the learners failed to understand; the next scene shows it.

    The mother, the sons, and the ten · 20:20–28

    20 Then the mother of the sons of Zebedee came to him with her sons, kneeling low and asking something from him.8

    Then (tote): straight after the prediction. Her sons are James and John (4:21; 10:2), two of the three at the transfiguration.
    Matt 15:22–25 — another mother has knelt low before Jesus and asked, the Canaanite woman for her daughter. She asked for mercy and was heard; this mother asks for rank and is refused.

    Bathsheba before David 1 Kgs 1:11–40

    With Adonijah claiming the throne, Nathan sends Bathsheba in to the old king David. She bows low, the king asks what she wants, and she reminds him of his oath that her son Solomon would reign and sit on his throne. David swears it again, and that day Solomon is anointed king.

    • A mother comes before a king, bows low and asks for her son’s place on the throne.
    • The king asks her what she wants.
    • The Greek has the same bowing verb (proskyneō, 1 Kgs 1:16) and the verb for sitting on the throne (kathizō, 1:13).

    Bathsheba’s request is granted at once. This mother’s is refused: the seats are not Jesus’ to give, and he offers a cup instead.

    The scene plays as a court petition before a king, and the king answers with a cup, not a throne. Tentative: Matthew does not cite the story.

    21 And he said to her, “What do you want?” She says to him, “Say that these two sons of mine may sit, one at your right and one at your left, in your kingdom.”

    Say: speak the word, as a king decrees. Left (euōnymos, “well-named”), a polite word that avoids the unlucky side.

    Mark 10:35–37 — who asks

    Mark 10:35–37James and John ask for themselves: “Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we ask … grant that we may sit, one at your right and one at your left, in your glory.”
    SameRight and left; “what do you want?”
    DifferentMatthew has the mother ask, kneeling; drops the blank-cheque opening; says “say” for “grant” and “in your kingdom” for “in your glory.”

    The request becomes a court petition for seats in a kingdom, fed by the twelve thrones of 19:28. The mother does not shield the sons: Jesus answers them, and the ten are angry at them.

    Matt 27:38 — “one at the right and one at the left” returns in the same Greek words at the cross, where two bandits are crucified with him. The places the mother asks for are given, to others, in Jerusalem.

    22 But Jesus answered and said, “Y’all do not know what y’all are asking. Are y’all able to drink the cup that I am about to drink?” They say to him, “We are able.”9

    He answers the sons, plural, not the mother. The cup returns at Gethsemane (26:39), where these two will be asleep.
    Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17 — a cup is in Yahweh’s hand, of foaming wine, and all the wicked of the earth must drain it; Jerusalem has drunk from Yahweh’s hand “the cup of his fury.” In the prophets the cup God hands over is mostly judgment, falling first on his own people.

    23 He says to them, “My cup y’all will drink; but to sit at my right and at my left is not mine to give, but it is for those for whom it has been prepared by my Father.”10

    Not “y’all are able” but “y’all will drink.” By my Father is Matthew’s addition.

    Mark 10:38–41 — the cup without the baptism

    Mark 10:38–39“Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, and to be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with?” They will do both.
    Mark 10:40–41“Not mine to give, but for whom it has been prepared.” The ten are indignant “toward James and John.”
    SameThe cup, “we are able,” the seats not his to give.
    DifferentMatthew drops the baptism and keeps only the cup; adds “by my Father”; has “the two brothers” for Mark’s names.

    With the baptism gone, everything rests on the cup, the word that returns at the supper and in Gethsemane. The added “Father” places the seats in the Father’s hands, as the day and hour are (24:36).

    24 And when the ten heard, they were indignant about the two brothers.

    Indignant (aganakteō): not righteous anger; the two had got in first.

    25 But Jesus, summoning them, said, “Y’all know that the rulers of the nations master it down over them, and the great ones wield authority down over them.

    Both verbs begin with kata, “down”: katakyrieuō and katexousiazō. Power bearing down on those below.
    Gen 1:28; Ps 10:10 — in the Greek of Genesis God tells humankind to master the earth (katakyrieuō); in the Greek psalm (9:31) the wicked use the same verb on the poor. Jesus applies it to rulers over people. Tentative: a rare shared word, and no one draws the line.

    26 It will not be so among y’all; but whoever wants to become great among y’all will be y’all’s servant,

    Servant (diakonos), one who waits on others, the one who serves at table.

    27 and whoever wants to be first among y’all will be y’all’s slave;

    Slave (doulos), stronger than servant: one who belongs to another. First again: the vineyard’s word, now as ambition.

    28 just as the Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom in place of many.”11

    Life (psychē); ransom (lytron), the price that frees a slave or captive; in place of (anti).
    Greek (translit.)kai dounai tēn psychēn autou lytron anti pollōn
    This translation“and to give his life as a ransom in place of many”
    NASB“and to give His life as a ransom for many”
    Noteanti is “in place of,” not just “for the benefit of.” Readers divide on whether the stress falls on the price paid or on the freedom it buys, and on whether “many” means all or the elect; the saying itself does not settle either.
    Dan 7:13–14 — one like a son of man is given dominion and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations and languages should serve him (the Old Greek: latreuō, give worship). Here the Son of Man comes to give service, not to receive it. Matthew’s verb (diakoneō) is not Daniel’s.
    Isa 53:10–12 — the servant’s life is made an offering; in the Greek “his life was handed over to death,” he “serves many well” and “bore the sins of many.” Life, handing over, serving and many are all there; the word ransom is not. The idea sounds through more than the wording.

    Two blind men at Jericho · 20:29–34

    29 And as they were going out from Jericho, a large crowd followed him.

    Jericho is the last town before the long climb to Jerusalem; the crowd are largely pilgrims going up for the feast.

    30 And look, two blind men sitting beside the road, hearing that Jesus was passing by, cried out, saying, “Master, show us loyal-love, Son of David!”12

    They sit while everyone else walks. Their cry is almost word for word the cry of the two blind men at 9:27 and of the Canaanite mother (15:22).

    Mark 10:46–52; Luke 18:35–43 — one blind man or two

    Mark 10:46–52One blind beggar, Bartimaeus son of Timaeus. Bystanders tell him “take heart, get up, he is calling you”; he throws off his cloak, calls Jesus “Rabbouni,” and hears “your faith has saved you.”
    Luke 18:35–43One blind man, as Jesus nears Jericho; those in front rebuke him; “your faith has saved you”; he follows, glorifying God.
    SameThe cry to the Son of David, the rebuke, the louder cry, “what do you want me to do,” sight, following.
    DifferentMatthew has two men, unnamed; no cloak, no “take heart,” no word about faith; “Master” three times; Jesus is wrenched in the gut and touches their eyes.

    Matthew tells it in the shape of his own earlier healing (9:27–31): two blind men, the same cry, a touch on the eyes. The two sight stories bracket the ministry; between them the leaders have turned against him, and his loyal-love has not changed.

    31 But the crowd charged them sharply, so that they would be silent; but they cried out more, saying, “Master, show us loyal-love, Son of David!”

    The crowd does what the learners did to the children (19:13). In the next chapter this crowd will itself shout “Son of David” (21:9).

    32 And Jesus stood still, called out to them and said, “What do y’all want me to do for y’all?”

    Called out (phōneō, “voice”), not the call-verb of 20:8.
    Matt 20:21 — the question he put to the mother of Zebedee’s sons, now to two blind men. Two privileged learners asked for future honour through someone else; two outsiders ask directly, with titles, for sight now.

    33 They say to him, “Master, that our eyes may be opened.”

    Isa 35:5 — “then the eyes of the blind will be opened”: the Greek has the same three words, blind (typhlos), eyes (ophthalmos) and opened (anoigō). The blind men ask for what Isaiah promised for the day God comes.

    34 And wrenched in the gut, Jesus touched their eyes, and at once they saw again, and they followed him.13

    Eyes here is omma, a rarer word than in 20:33. Followed: the scene opens and closes on the verb.

    Notes

    1. For the kingdom (v. 1). The parable is Matthew’s alone, and the for makes it the answer to Peter’s “what then will there be for us?” (19:27) and the saying of 19:30. Older Christian readers made the five hirings ages of the world, from Adam to Christ, or stages of a life; others have seen Israel and the Gentiles in the first and last hired. Most recent readers warn against matching groups to the workers: the vineyard may sound Israel, but the story turns on the master’s goodness and the grumbler’s eye. A man who keeps hiring workers he does not need through the day is a poor businessman and a good neighbour to the unemployed.
    2. agreed (v. 2). symphōneō, “sound together,” as at 18:19. The agreement on a denarius frames the whole quarrel: it is made here and invoked in the master’s answer (20:13). The first are paid exactly what they agreed to.
    3. whatever is right (v. 4). dikaion, the neuter of the righteous word, standing alone as a noun; the only other neuter use in Matthew is of righteous blood (23:35). The promise leaves the fair amount unnamed, and the story fills it with generosity rather than calculation.
    4. grumbled (v. 11). The verb group belongs to Israel in the wilderness, which has led some to identify the first-hired with Israel. Many readers resist that: the word shows the shape of the fault, a complaint against the one who provides, and does not decide who the workers are.
    5. the last will be first (v. 16). The story itself shows no first becoming last: everyone gets the same. One reading takes the saying as reversal, a warning that those who presume on their early start lose their place; another takes it as equality, that no one gets more for being first. The parable shows equality; the frame around it speaks of reversal, and both are heard. Many later manuscripts add “for many are called, but few chosen,” from 22:14; the earliest do not have it here.
    6. the chief priests and scribes (v. 18). The pair first appeared together when Herod asked them where the Anointed One was to be born (2:4); now they gather round the same king to condemn him. Judge against him for death anticipates the council’s verdict, “he is liable to death” (26:66).
    7. on the third day (v. 19). Mark’s Greek has “after three days”; Matthew has “on the third day,” as at 16:21 and 17:23. The sign of Jonah (12:40) set the three days before them. The flat, short ending after mocking, scourging and crucifying is jarring, and readers have long noticed it.
    8. the mother (v. 20). Why the mother asks, when in Mark the sons ask, has no single answer. Some think Matthew spares the apostles, but he does not spare Peter elsewhere, and the ten are angry at the brothers, not at her. Others think the sons were ashamed to ask, or that the mother is meant to recall Bathsheba, or to stand beside the Canaanite mother of 15:22. She appears again at the cross (27:56).
    9. the cup (v. 22). In the psalms a cup can be blessing (Ps 16:5; 23:5; 116:13), but in the prophets it is mostly the cup of God’s wrath. Here it means suffering at least; whether it also carries a judgment Jesus alone will bear is read both ways. Many later manuscripts add Mark’s “or to be baptized with the baptism that I am baptized with” here and in 20:23; the earliest do not.
    10. not mine to give (v. 23). One old reading takes it as condescension, Jesus speaking to their level; others take it as a real limit the Son accepts, like the day and hour only the Father knows (24:36). Prepared returns for the kingdom prepared for the blessed and the fire prepared for the slanderer (25:34, 41). The sons did drink: James was killed by the sword (Acts 12:2); John’s end is less certain, and the later stories of his suffering are not secure.
    11. ransom in place of many (v. 28). The first time in Matthew that Jesus says why he will die; “for many” returns at the supper (26:28). The noun lytron is the price that frees a slave or a forfeited life (Exod 21:30; Lev 25:51–52; Num 18:15). Isaiah 53 supplies the shape (a life handed over, the many), though not the noun; a further text, God giving nations as a ransom for Israel (Isa 43:3–4), is attractive but cannot be shown. “Many” is a Semitic way of saying a great number, not a limit set against “all”; later, “a ransom for all” (1 Tim 2:6).
    12. two blind men (v. 30). Why Matthew has two where Mark and Luke have one is unresolved. Suggestions: two witnesses, as the Law requires; a pairing with the two sons of Zebedee; or assimilation to the earlier two blind men (9:27–31). Luke sets the healing on the way into Jericho, Mark and Matthew on the way out. One early reader held that “Son of David” named only Jesus’ human side; Matthew lets the title be shouted twice and heard.
    13. they followed him (v. 34). It may make them a picture of disciples who see, against learners who did not; or it may only mean they joined the pilgrim crowd going up. Matthew does not say. He gives no word about their faith, and no order to keep silent, as there was at 9:30.

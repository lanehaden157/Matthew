The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

Life in the Community

hena tōn mikrōn toutōn — one of these little ones
Unit 18 · Matthew 18:1–35 · the fourth discourse, closed at 19:1 ("when Jesus finished these words")

    Who is greater in the kingdom · 18:1–5

    1In that hour the learners came to Jesus, saying, "Who then is greater in the kingdom of the skies?"1

    mathētai, "learners"; basileia tōn ouranōn, "the kingdom of the skies"; meizōn, "greater", the comparative of megas, "great".

    2And having summoned a child, he stood it in their midst

    proskaleomai, "summon, call to oneself", a separate word from kaleō, "call"; paidion, "child, little child".

    3and said, "Amen I say to y'all, unless y'all turn around and become like the children, y'all will never come into the kingdom of the skies.

    strephō, "turn, turn around", a different verb from metanoeō, "turn" (the change of mind John and Jesus proclaimed).

    4Whoever, then, will lower himself like this child, this one is the greater in the kingdom of the skies.2

    tapeinoō, "lower, bring low", the verb of tapeinos, "lowly", Jesus' word for his own heart at 11:29.

    5And whoever receives one such child in my name receives me.3

    dechomai, "receive, welcome" as a guest; epi tō onomati mou, literally "on my name", on the strength of it.

    Mark 9:33–37 · Luke 9:46–48 — who is greatest

    Mark 9:33–34In the house at Capernaum Jesus asks what they were discussing on the road; they keep silent, because on the road they had argued who was greater.
    Luke 9:46–47An argument comes in among them over which of them is greater; Jesus, knowing the reasoning of their heart, takes a child and stands it beside him.
    Mark 9:37"Whoever receives one of such children in my name receives me."
    John 13:20At the foot-washing: whoever receives the one Jesus sends receives him. A later reuse of the saying, with a different verb for receiving.

    In Mark and Luke the learners argue among themselves and Jesus finds them out; Matthew has them bring the question to him and set it inside the kingdom of the skies. Then he adds what neither parallel has: "unless y'all turn around and become like the children, y'all will never come in." Rank becomes entry, and the entry condition is the lowliness Jesus claimed for himself at 11:29.

    Trip-ups and the little ones · 18:6–9

    6"But whoever trips up one of these little ones who trust in me, it would be better for him that a donkey's millstone be hung around his neck and that he be sunk in the open sea.4

    skandalizō, "trip up", from the trap's trigger-stick; mikros, "little"; pisteuō, "trust". mylos onikos, "donkey-millstone", the upper stone a donkey turned; katapontizō, "sink, drown"; pelagos tēs thalassēs, "the open water of the sea".

    7Woe to the world because of the trip-ups! For the trip-ups must come, but woe to the man through whom the trip-up comes.

    skandalon, "trip-up", the noun of the same root; anankē, "it is necessary, it must be".

    8And if your hand or your foot trips you up, cut it off and throw it from you; it is better for you to come into life maimed or lame than, having two hands or two feet, to be thrown into the fire of the age.

    to pyr to aiōnion, "the fire of the age": aiōnios belongs to aiōn, "age", the coming age's fire.

    9And if your eye trips you up, pluck it out and throw it from you; it is better for you to come into life one-eyed than, having two eyes, to be thrown into the Gehenna of fire.5

    geenna, "Gehenna", the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, made the name of judgment; monophthalmos, "one-eyed".

    Mark 9:42–50 · Luke 17:1–2 — trip-ups and the millstone

    Mark 9:42Better for him if a donkey-millstone is put around his neck and he is thrown into the sea.
    Mark 9:48"…where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched" (Isa 66:24), after the hand, foot and eye.
    Mark 9:49–50Everyone salted with fire; salt is good; have salt in yourselves and be at peace with one another.
    Luke 17:1–2Trip-ups cannot fail to come, but woe to the one through whom they come; a millstone-stone around his neck would profit him.

    Matthew keeps the millstone, the woe and the amputation sayings, but drops Isaiah's worm and fire, the salt saying, and the outsider who cast out demons in Jesus' name (Mark 9:38–41), whose cup of cold water Matthew had already placed at 10:42. What remains is the warning of Gehenna aimed squarely at harm done to the little ones, with the hand-and-eye saying repeated from the Sermon (5:29–30) as the thread's first telling.

    The Father's will for the wanderer · 18:10–14

    10"See that y'all do not look down on one of these little ones, for I say to y'all that their angels in the skies always see the face of my Father in the skies.67

    kataphroneō, literally "think down on", treat as beneath notice.

    12What does it seem to y'all? If a certain man comes to have a hundred sheep and one of them wanders off, will he not leave the ninety-nine on the mountains and go and seek the one wandering?

    planaō, "wander, go astray" (hence "planet"). "Leave" is aphiēmi, later "release": possibly a wordplay.

    13And if he comes to find it, amen I say to y'all, he rejoices over it more than over the ninety-nine that have not wandered off.

    14So it is not the will before y'all's Father in the skies that one of these little ones be lost.

    apollymi, "lose, perish, be lost"; thelēma, "will, what is wanted".

    Luke 15:3–7 — the lost sheep

    Luke 15:2–3Told to Pharisees and scribes grumbling that he welcomes sinners and eats with them.
    Luke 15:4The man has lost one sheep, leaves the ninety-nine in the wilderness, and goes after the lost one until he finds it.
    Luke 15:7There will be more joy in the sky over one sinner who turns than over ninety-nine righteous who need no turning.

    Luke aims the parable outward, at critics of Jesus' table, and the sheep is "lost" and then found among outsiders. Matthew tells it to the learners, the sheep "wanders off" from inside the flock, and the close is the Father's will that not one little one be lost. Luke's story is about outsiders found; Matthew's is about insiders kept.

    God against the shepherds who did not seek Ezek 34:4–16

    In exile, Ezekiel indicts Israel's shepherds, its rulers, for feeding themselves and not the flock: the strayed they did not bring back, the lost they did not seek, and the sheep wandered over all the mountains with no one searching. So God says he will search for his sheep himself, seek the lost, bring back the strayed, and bind up the broken.

    • A sheep that has strayed and a search for it
    • The mountains as the place of wandering
    • Seeking and losing as the governing verbs

    No shepherds are condemned in Matthew. The man who leaves the ninety-nine is the model, and the learners are put in the shepherd's place, answerable for one little one.

    The community's care for its wanderers becomes God's own promised shepherding, carried on through them; it ties into the shepherd thread of 9:36, 10:6 and 15:24.

    When a brother sins · 18:15–20

    15"But if your brother sins against you, go, expose his fault between you and him alone. If he hears you, you have gained your brother.89

    hamartanō, "sin, miss the mark"; elenchō, "bring to light, show someone his fault"; kerdainō, "gain", a merchant's word.
    Lev 19:17 forbids hating a brother in the heart and commands reproving one's neighbor instead; Jesus makes that command the first, private step.

    16But if he does not hear, take along with you one or two more, so that on the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may stand. (Deut 19:15)

    "On the mouth of … every word may stand" keeps the Torah's own idiom: testimony needs more than one voice.

    17And if he refuses to hear them, tell it to the assembly; and if he refuses to hear even the assembly, let him be to you as the Gentile and the tax collector.1011

    parakouō, "hear past, refuse to hear"; ekklēsia, "assembly", only here and 16:18 in the Gospels; ethnikos, "someone of the nations".

    18Amen I say to y'all, whatever y'all bind on the earth will have been bound in the sky, and whatever y'all loose on the earth will have been loosed in the sky.12

    deō, "bind"; lyō, "loose". Said to Peter alone at 16:19; here "y'all", the whole assembly.

    Luke 17:3 · John 20:23 — reproof and release

    Luke 17:3"If your brother sins, charge him sharply, and if he turns, release him."
    John 20:23The risen Jesus breathes the Spirit on them: whoever's sins y'all release are released; whoever's y'all hold fast are held fast.

    Luke has one move, rebuke and release. Matthew builds a three-step procedure, private, then with witnesses, then before the assembly, and closes it with binding and loosing in the plural. The authority given to Peter at 16:19 now belongs to the gathered community, much as John gives the authority to release or hold sins to the group the risen Jesus sends.

    19Again, amen I say to y'all that if two of y'all agree on the earth about any matter they may ask, it will happen for them from my Father in the skies.

    symphōneō, "sound together", as voices in tune; the word behind "symphony".

    20For where two or three are gathered into my name, there I am in their midst."13

    synagō, "gather", the harvest verb; "into my name", eis to emon onoma, gathered toward and belonging to it.
    Exod 25:8, "let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them": the presence once housed in a tent now stands among two or three, as "God with us" (1:23) promised and 28:20 will repeat.

    Seventy-seven times · 18:21–22

    21Then Peter came and said to him, "Master, how many times will my brother sin against me and I release him? Up to seven times?"

    aphiēmi, "release, let go", the verb for letting go debts and sins alike; kyrie, "Master".

    22Jesus says to him, "I do not say to you up to seven times, but up to seventy-seven times.14

    hebdomēkontakis hepta, "seventy-times seven": seventy-seven times, the number of Lamech's vengeance.

    Luke 17:4 — seven times a day

    Luke 17:4"And if seven times in the day he sins against you, and seven times turns back to you, saying, 'I turn,' you will release him."

    Luke states a rule for repeated, repented wrongs. Matthew turns it into Peter's question with a generous ceiling of seven and answers with Lamech's number, which breaks the ceiling and sets up the parable that follows.

    Lamech's song of vengeance Gen 4:23–24

    Cain's descendant Lamech sings to his two wives that he has killed a man for wounding him and a boy for striking him. If Cain is avenged seven times, he boasts, then Lamech seventy-seven times. It is the last word of the spreading violence before the flood story.

    • The same number, in the Greek Old Testament's same phrase, hebdomēkontakis hepta
    • Seven set against seventy-seven
    • Wrongs done by a brother, as Cain's was

    Lamech counts revenges; Jesus counts releases.

    The boast of unlimited vengeance from humanity's first spiral of violence is reversed inside a new family: the measure of payback becomes the measure of release, with no ceiling.

    The slave released and the slave throttled · 18:23–35

    23Because of this, the kingdom of the skies has been likened to a man, a king, who wanted to settle accounts with his slaves.1516

    basileus, "king"; doulos, "slave"; synairō logon, "settle accounts". Only Matthew tells this parable.

    24When he began to settle them, one debtor of ten thousand talents was brought to him.

    opheiletēs, "debtor"; talanton, "talent", the largest unit of reckoning; ten thousand (myrioi) is the largest number word.

    25And since he had nothing to repay with, the Master ordered him to be sold, and his wife and the children and all that he has, and repayment to be made.

    26So the slave fell and kept bowing low before him, saying, 'Be long-tempered with me, and I will repay you everything.'17

    proskyneō, "bow low, worship"; makrothymeō, "be long-tempered", slow to boil over.

    27And the Master of that slave, wrenched in the gut, released him, and the loan he let go for him.

    splanchnizomai, "be wrenched in the gut"; apolyō, "release, send away"; daneion, "loan"; aphiēmi, "let go, release".

    28But that slave went out and found one of his fellow slaves who owed him a hundred denarii, and seizing him he began to throttle him, saying, 'Repay, if you owe anything!'

    dēnarion, "denarius", a day's wage (20:2); opheilō, "owe"; pnigō, "choke, throttle".

    29So his fellow slave fell and kept urging him, saying, 'Be long-tempered with me, and I will repay you.'

    The plea is the first slave's own, word for word, short only of "everything". parakaleō, "urge, call alongside".

    30But he would not; instead he went off and threw him into prison until he should repay what was owed.

    31So his fellow slaves, seeing what had happened, were grieved exceedingly, and they came and made plain to their Master all that had happened.

    lypeō, "grieve"; diasapheō, "make thoroughly clear".

    32Then, having summoned him, his Master says to him, 'Evil slave! All that debt I released to you, since you urged me.

    ponēros, "evil"; opheilē, "debt": the Master's word changes from "loan" to "debt".

    33Was it not necessary for you, too, to show loyal-love to your fellow slave, as I also showed loyal-love to you?'

    eleeō, "show loyal-love", the verb of eleos in Hosea 6:6, which Jesus quoted at 9:13 and 12:7.

    34And in anger his Master handed him over to the tormentors until he should repay all that was owed.18

    paradidōmi, "hand over"; basanistēs, "tormentor", of basanizō, "torment". "Until" has no end: the sum is unpayable.

    35So also my Father in the skies will do to y'all, if y'all do not release, each one his brother, from y'all's hearts."19

    kardia, "heart"; adelphos, "brother". The discourse ends where 18:14 began, with the Father in the skies.

    Notes

    1. The shape of the discourse (v. 1). This is the fourth of Matthew's five discourses; its closing formula, "when Jesus finished these words", comes at 19:1. It runs in two panels: 18:1–14 on the little ones and 18:15–35 on the brother who sins. No concentric ring survives checking here. What holds the chapter together is a set of repeated phrases: "one of these little ones" (18:6, 10, 14), "my Father in the skies" (18:10, 14, 19, 35) and "brother" (18:15, 21, 35). The question of who is greater "in the kingdom of the skies" (18:1) and the parable that likens "the kingdom of the skies" to a king (18:23) also bracket the whole. The scene just before, the temple tax, ended with Jesus paying "so that we do not trip them up" (17:27), and tripping up is exactly where this discourse turns at 18:6.
    2. Lowering (v. 4). The word moves through Matthew in three steps. Jesus calls himself "gentle and lowly (tapeinos) in heart" (11:29); here the one who lowers (tapeinoō) himself like a child is the greater in the kingdom; and at 23:12 the same saying turns against the scribes and Pharisees: whoever lifts himself up will be lowered, and whoever lowers himself will be lifted up. The entry condition for the kingdom is Jesus' own posture.
    3. Receiving (v. 5). The mission discourse already said it: whoever receives the learners receives Jesus, and whoever gives a cup of cold water to one of these little ones will not lose his wage (10:40–42). The child here stands for the same people.
    4. The millstone and who the little ones are (v. 6). A donkey-millstone was the heavy upper stone of a mill turned by an animal, and a millstone was a household's livelihood: the law forbids taking one in pledge (Deut 24:6; cf. the upper millstone a woman drops on Abimelech, Judg 9:53). The image may echo that law, though the link is possible rather than shown. A dispensational reading takes the little ones as humble believers and tripping them up as withholding encouragement from weaker believers, with the millstone punishing the active causing of a fall. A patristic reading sees in them the poor and the despised. Both treat "little ones" as a category of discipleship rather than of age, and the frame at 10:42 points the same way.
    5. A repeated saying (vv. 8–9). These verses nearly repeat 5:29–30, where the Sermon applied the eye and the hand to lust; the same clause, "pluck it out and throw it from you", stands at 5:29 and 18:9. The Sermon aimed the saying at one's own desire; the discourse aims it at the harm one's life does to the little ones.
    6. The face of the Father (v. 10). "Those who see the king's face" is a phrase for the king's closest counselors (Esth 1:14), and the line may borrow that court picture: the least of the community have advocates with immediate access to the Father. The verbs likely differ, so the link is conceptual rather than verbal. Nothing here builds a doctrine of guardian angels; the point is the little ones' standing before God.
    7. The missing verse (18:11). Some later manuscripts add "For the Son of Man came to save the lost." The best manuscripts and the base text used here lack it, so the verse number is skipped. The line looks borrowed from Luke 19:10, the end of the Zacchaeus story, though its theme does sit in the parable that follows.
    8. The reproof tradition (v. 15). Matthew's procedure joins two Torah texts, the command to reprove a neighbor (Lev 19:17) and the rule of two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15). The Qumran community ran a similar ladder of private reproof, then witnesses, then the congregation (Damascus Document 9:2–8; Community Rule 5:24–6:1); this rests on secondary studies, not a reading of the scrolls themselves. A rabbinic sidelight, Rabbi Tarfon in the Babylonian Talmud (Arakhin 16b), wonders whether anyone in his generation can still accept reproof at all. Jesus is joining an existing conversation about how to keep Torah's commands toward a brother. None of these texts has anything like Matthew's last step, "as the Gentile and the tax collector".
    9. Gaining (v. 15). kerdainō is a merchant's word. At 16:26 it weighs gaining the whole world against losing one's life; at 25:16–22 the faithful slaves "gain" more talents. Here the profit is a brother.
    10. Hear, refuse to hear (vv. 15–17). The steps turn on hearing: "if he hears you" (akouō), then "if he refuses to hear" (parakouō, literally "hear past"). The prophets use the language of a people who did not hear when God called (e.g. Isa 65:12); whether the Greek Old Testament uses this exact verb there is unconfirmed.
    11. "As the Gentile and the tax collector" (v. 17). Three readings stand. (1) A dispensational reading sees church discipline ending in exclusion from the community. (2) Another reading holds that the break is between the brother and the one he wronged, not a formal expulsion. (3) A third asks how Jesus himself treats Gentiles and tax collectors: he called Matthew from the tax booth, ate with tax collectors (9:9–13), and healed the Canaanite woman's daughter (15:21–28), so the line would mean "start over with him as an outsider to be won". The binding and loosing that follows sounds judicial, which favors (1) or (2); (3) keeps the line inside the chapter's search for the wanderer. The text can carry all three.
    12. Binding and loosing in the plural (v. 18). At 16:19 the keys and the authority to bind and loose were given to Peter alone ("you", singular). Here the same words go to "y'all", the assembly acting together. The verb form is a future perfect: what they bind "will have been bound" in the sky. The usual reading takes this to mean that the sky's verdict stands before the community acts and the community recognizes it, though this is debated and nothing here depends on it. A dispensational reading ties the pair to the keys and to the rabbinic sense of "forbid" and "permit", with a further sense of "punish" and "absolve" found in Josephus. In a setting of reproof, the nearer sense may be holding a sin or releasing it, as in John 20:23. The text does not force a choice.
    13. Witnesses become presence (vv. 16, 20). The "two or three witnesses" of Deut 19:15 (18:16) become the "two or three gathered" whom Jesus joins (18:20). A rabbinic saying of the same era in kind (Mishnah Avot 3:2–3) holds that when two or more sit together over Torah, the divine presence rests among them; it is a parallel, not a source. Matthew puts Jesus himself in the midst and ties the gathering to his name.
    14. Seventy-seven or seventy times seven (v. 22). hebdomēkontakis hepta can be read "seventy-seven times" or "seventy times seven". It is the same phrase the Greek Old Testament uses for Lamech's seventy-sevenfold vengeance (Gen 4:24), where the Hebrew reads seventy-seven (shivʿim veshivʿah), so "seventy-seven" is preferred here. Either way it is not a new ceiling: a dispensational reading also takes it to mean no limit on the number of releases.
    15. Whose kingdom, when (v. 23). The king stands for God, as kings commonly do in rabbinic parables. A dispensational reading takes the scene to show kingdom conditions that should mark learners now, between the two comings, and that will prevail when Jesus returns to reign on the earth. That is one reading of "has been likened"; the text itself only says that the kingdom of the skies resembles this reckoning.
    16. Settling accounts and release (vv. 23–27). A pattern noticed in this study, not drawn from a source: a reckoning of accounts that ends in a debt "let go" may echo the seventh-year release of debts (Deut 15:1–2) and the Jubilee (Lev 25), Torah's own institutions of release. It is offered as a possibility only.
    17. Long-tempered (vv. 26, 29). makrothymeō is literally "to be long of temper". God names himself "long-tempered", slow to anger, at Sinai (Exod 34:6), possibly in this word's adjective in the Greek Old Testament. The two slaves ask a fellow creature for the patience God shows; the king gives far more, and the released slave gives nothing.
    18. The tormentors (v. 34). A dispensational reading lists the main ways the punishment has been taken: (a) loss of salvation, which it rejects because salvation would then depend on works; (b) a warning built on an impossible situation; (c) evidence that the man was never truly a believer, like Judas; (d) God's discipline in this life; (e) loss of reward; (f) hyperbole. It notes that Jesus does not say eternal punishment. A plainer reading lets the threat stand as real loss for whoever refuses release. The text does not settle which is meant.
    19. Released, so release (v. 35). The Lord's Prayer asks, "release us our debts as we also have released our debtors" (6:12), and Jesus adds that the Father will not release those who do not release (6:14–15). The parable stages that prayer. A dispensational reading stresses that this does not teach earning release by releasing: the order in the story runs from the king's release to the slave's duty. The verse does not soften the threat either.

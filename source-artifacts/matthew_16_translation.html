The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

On This Rock

epi tautē tē petra — "on this rock"
Unit 16 · Matthew 16:1–20 · a sign refused, bread mistaken for leaven, and the rock Simon is named for

Recurring roots — colour key

    The movement of the unit · 16:1–20

    A test at the lakeside 16:1–4→ the other side, and no bread 16:5–12→ the parts of Caesarea Philippi 16:13–20→ a silence ordered 16:20

    The sign they asked for · 16:1–4

    1And the Pharisees and Sadducees, coming up, testing him, asked him to show them a sign from the sky.

    Massah: the test at the rock Exod 17:1–7

    Out of Egypt, Israel camps at Rephidim and finds no water. The people quarrel with Moses and demand water, and he asks them, "Why do y'all test Yahweh?" At Yahweh's word Moses strikes the rock at Horeb and water comes out. He names the place Massah, "Test," and Meribah, "Quarrel," because they tested Yahweh by asking, "Is Yahweh among us or not?"

    • a demand for proof of God's presence
    • the verb "test"
    • a rock in the story

    At Massah the whole people test God and the test is about water; here the leaders test Jesus and the demand is for a sign from the sky. There the rock gives what is asked, and here the answer is a refusal.

    It lets the demand for a sign be heard as the old question, "Is Yahweh among us or not?", put to the one whom Matthew named "God with us" at the start (1:23). The unit that opens with a test closes with a rock.

    Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:16, "You shall not test Yahweh your God," which looks back to Massah, when the tester takes him up on the temple (4:7).

    2[But he, answering, said to them, "When evening has come, y'all say, 'Fair weather, for the sky is fiery red.'

    3And at dawn, 'Today, a winter storm, for the sky is fiery red and gloomy.' The face of the sky y'all know how to discern, but the signs of the times y'all cannot."]1

    pyrrazō, "be fiery red": from pyr, "fire." stygnazō, "look gloomy," of a sky that turns dark and sullen. kairoi, "the times": the set moments, the right seasons, not the passing of clock time.

    Luke 12:54–56 — the same weather sayings, to a different audience

    Luke 12:54–56Jesus speaks to the crowds. A cloud rising in the west, and they say a shower is coming; a south wind, and they say there will be scorching heat. "Stage-actors," he says, they can test the face of earth and sky, but how is it they cannot test "this time"? There is no sign demanded and no Sadducee.
    Matthew 16:2–3The same shape, with a red sky at evening and again at dawn, said to the Pharisees and Sadducees right after they ask for a sign.

    Luke aims the saying at a crowd that can read weather and will not read the moment. Matthew turns it into the answer to a sign demand: they can discern the face of the sky, but not "the signs of the times." The whole saying is missing in some of the oldest manuscripts (note 1).

    4"An evil and adulterous generation seeks a sign, and a sign will not be given to it, except the sign of Jonah." And leaving them behind, he went away.

    The prophets picture Israel as a wife who breaks the marriage with her God (Hos 3:1; Ezek 16:32), so "adulterous" is a charge of faithlessness to the covenant. It is the same pair of words as 12:39.

    Mark 8:11–13 · Luke 11:16, 29–30 — the sign refused

    Mark 8:11–13The Pharisees alone come out, argue with him and ask for a sign from the sky, testing him. Jesus sighs deeply in his spirit and asks why this generation seeks a sign. His answer is the form "if a sign shall be given to this generation," a Semitic oath that means "certainly not." There is no Jonah, and he gets back into the boat.
    Luke 11:16, 29–30Some test him by seeking a sign from the sky. As the crowds gather he calls this an evil generation that seeks a sign, and says only the sign of Jonah will be given, as Jonah was a sign to the Ninevites.
    Matthew 16:1–4The Pharisees and Sadducees come together. There is no sigh. The generation is "evil and adulterous," and Jonah is the one exception, left unexplained.

    Matthew has the sign scene of 12:38–42 and tells it again almost word for word. He does not need to explain Jonah, because he did so at 12:40 (three days and three nights), and he adds the Sadducees, who will come back in the very next warning about leaven.

    The leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees · 16:5–12

    5And the learners, going to the other side, forgot to take bread.

    6But Jesus said to them, "Look, and watch out for the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees."2

    zymē, "leaven": the bit of fermented dough kept back from one baking to start the next. It works through the whole lump out of sight, which is why the image can carry a spreading influence.

    7But they were reasoning among themselves, saying, "Because we took no bread."

    8But Jesus, knowing it, said, "Why are y'all reasoning among yourselves, little-faiths, because y'all took no bread?

    oligopistoi, "little-faiths": a one-word nickname, "people of small trust." Jesus uses it of worry over food and clothing (6:30), of fear in a storm (8:26) and of Peter sinking (14:31); here it is bread again.

    9Do y'all not yet perceive, nor remember the five loaves of the five thousand, and how many baskets y'all took up?

    10Nor the seven loaves of the four thousand, and how many hampers y'all took up?3

    11How is it y'all do not perceive that I did not speak to y'all about bread? But watch out for the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees."

    12Then they understood that he did not say to watch out for the leaven of the loaves, but for the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees.

    noeō, "perceive" (16:9, 11), is to take something in with the mind; syniēmi, "understand" (16:12), is to "throw together" the pieces. Matthew keeps the two apart, and the second is the verb of the parable chapter.

    Mark 8:14–21 · Luke 12:1 — the leaven, and what the learners do with it

    Mark 8:14–21They have one loaf in the boat. The warning is about "the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod." Jesus asks a run of questions: do you not yet perceive, is your heart hardened, having eyes do you not see, having ears do you not hear? He recalls both feedings with their numbers and ends, "Do you not yet understand?" Mark gives no answer.
    Mark 8:22–26Next in Mark comes the blind man of Bethsaida, healed in two stages. Matthew has no counterpart here.
    Luke 12:1"Watch out for the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy," said to the crowds, with no bread and no boat.
    Matthew 16:5–12No single loaf. The leaven is of the Pharisees and Sadducees. The hard-heart language is gone, "little-faiths" stands in for it, and the passage ends with an answer: "Then they understood," with the leaven named as "the teaching."

    Matthew turns Mark's unanswered question into a success and names the leaven himself. Where Mark leaves the learners blind, Matthew has them understand just before the confession, and swaps Herod for the Sadducees so that the warning matches the opposition of the first scene.

    Who do y'all say I am? · 16:13–16

    13And Jesus, coming into the parts of Caesarea Philippi, was asking his learners, saying, "Who do people say the Son of Man is?"4

    Mark 8:27–28 · Luke 9:18–19 — where, and what the question is

    Mark 8:27–28"To the villages of Caesarea Philippi," and "on the way" he asks, "Who do people say that I am?" The answers: John the Immerser, Elijah, one of the prophets.
    Luke 9:18–19While Jesus is praying alone, the learners with him, he asks, "Who do the crowds say that I am?" The answers are John, Elijah, and one of the ancient prophets risen.
    Matthew 16:13–14"The parts of Caesarea Philippi," no road and no prayer. The question is "Who do people say the Son of Man is?" The answers add Jeremiah by name.

    Matthew alone puts Jesus' own title into the question, so the title he has used of himself since 8:20 is the thing people are asked to identify. The list of answers is the longest of the three.

    14And they said, "Some say John the Immerser; others, Elijah; and others, Jeremiah or one of the prophets."5

    Matthew is the only New Testament writer who names Jeremiah, here, at the weeping Rachel of 2:17 (Jer 31:15) and at the potter's field of 27:9, so the prophet of the exile stands at the start, middle and end of the book.

    15He says to them, "But y'all, who do y'all say I am?"

    16And Simon Peter, answering, said, "You are the Anointed One, the Son of the living God."

    The high priest asks for these same two titles under oath at 26:63, "by the living God," and "the living God" is Israel's name for the God who acts, as in Joshua's word at the Jordan: "by this you will know that the living God is among you" (Josh 3:10; see also Deut 5:26).

    The rock and the keys · 16:17–20

    17And Jesus, answering, said to him, "Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah, because flesh and blood did not reveal it to you, but my Father in the skies.6

    Bar-Iōna, "Bar-Jonah": Aramaic for "son of Jonah." Jesus names Simon by his father's name before he gives him the new one.

    John 6:67–71 · John 1:42 — Simon's great words, placed elsewhere

    John 6:67–71After many learners leave, Jesus asks the Twelve whether they too want to go. Simon Peter answers, "Master, to whom shall we go? You have words of eternal life, and we have trusted and come to know that you are the Holy One of God." Jesus replies that he chose the Twelve, and one of them is a slanderer.
    John 1:42At their first meeting Jesus looks at him and says, "You are Simon son of John; you will be called Cephas," which John says is Peter.
    Matthew 16:16–18The confession comes at Caesarea Philippi, the blessing follows it, and the name Peter is explained only here, after Matthew has used it twice without comment (4:18; 10:2).

    John places Peter's confession and Jesus' naming at different moments, and John's confession ends with a shadow over one of the Twelve. Matthew gathers confession, blessing and naming into one scene, and the shadow falls next, at 16:23.

    18And I also say to you that you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it.789

    Petros, "Peter," is a masculine name made from petra, "rock," a bedrock shelf or crag; in Aramaic it is one word, Kēphas. pylai hadou, "gates of Hades": Hades is the place of the dead, and its gates are what a dead person goes through.
    Greek (translit.)su ei Petros, kai epi tautē tē petra oikodomēsō mou tēn ekklēsian
    This translation"you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly"
    WEB"you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly"
    NoteThe wording matches. The pair Petros / petra is a play on two forms of one word, and "rock" is feminine, so the name and the rock are not the same word even if they point at one another. Note 7 sets out the readings.

    The renaming of Abram and Jacob Gen 17:5; 32:28

    God renames Abram "Abraham," "father of a multitude of nations," when he makes the covenant with him (Gen 17:5), and Sarai becomes Sarah (17:15). After the night at the Jabbok, Jacob is renamed "Israel" because he has struggled with God and with people and prevailed (Gen 32:28).

    • God changes a person's name
    • the new name says what he will be for the people
    • the change comes at the turn of a promise

    In Genesis the new name arrives with its meaning in the same breath. Matthew has already used "Peter" twice (4:18; 10:2) as if it were a nickname already in place, and here Jesus says what it was for. The name is also a pun in Greek, where Genesis gives a reason.

    It makes the name Peter a commission: the man called "rock" is what the assembly is built on, as the father of a multitude was the starting point of Israel.

    Isaiah 51:1–2 tells Israel to look to the rock she was cut from, and names Abraham and Sarah; Matthew 3:9 already had God raising up children to Abraham from stones.
    "Gates of Hades" is the Greek Old Testament's phrase for death's door (Isa 38:10; Wis 16:13); Capernaum is told it "will go down to Hades" at 11:23.
    Peter himself later writes of the Anointed as a living stone and of believers as living stones being built into a house (1 Pet 2:4–5).

    19I will give you the keys of the kingdom of the skies, and whatever you bind on the earth will have been bound in the skies, and whatever you loose on the earth will have been loosed in the skies."10

    kleis, "key": a key opens and shuts, and carries the sense of authority over a house.
    Greek (translit.)ho ean dēsēs epi tēs gēs estai dedemenon en tois ouranois
    This translation"whatever you bind on the earth will have been bound in the skies"
    WEB"whatever you bind on earth will have been bound in heaven"
    NoteThe Greek is the verb "to be" plus a past participle, "will be having-been-bound," a state already in place. Many English versions smooth it to "will be bound." Note 10 sets out why it matters.

    Eliakim installed over the house of David Isa 22:15–25

    Yahweh sends Isaiah to Shebna, the steward over the king's house, who has cut himself a tomb on the height. Yahweh will throw him out and call his servant Eliakim son of Hilkiah, clothe him in Shebna's robe and sash, and make him "a father" to Jerusalem and Judah. In the Hebrew, "the key of the house of David" is laid on his shoulder: "he opens and no one shuts, he shuts and no one opens" (22:22). The oracle ends with the peg fixed in a firm place being cut down (22:25).

    • a steward over the house of David, given a key
    • opening and shutting that none can overrule
    • a "father" over the people, and a man chosen to replace another

    The Greek Old Testament does not have the word for "key" in this verse, so the link runs through the Hebrew, and through Revelation's "key of David" (Rev 3:7). Eliakim replaces a faithless steward and in the end the peg gives way. Simon replaces no one in this scene, and the keys are plural and of the kingdom of the skies.

    It shows what "keys" mean in David's house: the authority of the steward who admits and shuts out in the king's name, a trust the king gives and can withdraw.

    At 23:13 the scribes and Pharisees "shut the kingdom of the skies in people's faces," the opposite use of the same authority.

    Sky has run through the whole unit: a sign "from the sky" (16:1), the "face of the sky" (16:2–3), the Father "in the skies" (16:17), the kingdom "of the skies" and things bound "in the skies" here.

    20Then he gave strict orders to the learners that they tell no one that he is the Anointed One.11

    diastellomai, "give strict orders": to lay out an instruction with firm limits, a stronger word than "tell."

    Mark 8:29–30 · Luke 9:20–21 — the confession, and what follows it

    Mark 8:29–30Peter answers, "You are the Anointed One." Jesus charges them sharply to tell no one about him. Nothing else follows between the confession and the order.
    Luke 9:20–21Peter answers, "The Anointed One of God." Jesus charges them sharply and orders them to tell this to no one.
    Matthew 16:16–20"The Anointed One, the Son of the living God," then the blessing, the rock, the assembly and the keys. The order, with a different verb, names its content: that he is the Anointed One.

    Everything between Peter's words and the order to be silent, the blessing, the rock, the assembly and the keys, is in Matthew only. Mark and Luke have the shorter title and then the silence, with no word for Peter.

    Notes

    1. The weather saying (vv. 2–3). The Greek text this study follows puts the saying in brackets. Some of the oldest manuscripts leave it out, and many later ones have it. Either it was brought in from Luke 12:54–56 or it dropped out of the earliest copies; scholars are divided. It is kept here, bracketed, and nothing in the unit rests on it: verse 4 follows verse 1 without it.
    2. Pharisees and Sadducees (v. 6). Matthew pairs them under one article at 3:7, when they come to John's immersing, and in this scene (16:1, 6, 11, 12), and nowhere else; the Sadducees come alone at 22:23 and the Pharisees alone at 22:34. They were rivals on much, the Sadducees denying the resurrection and the Pharisees keeping the elders' traditions, and the pairing makes the leaven one thing they share. The leaven of 13:33 is the same word used of the kingdom, and that does not make the Pharisees' leaven good; it shows the image is about spreading.
    3. Two baskets (vv. 9–10). Matthew keeps the two basket words from the feedings apart: kophinoi, "baskets" (14:20), after the five thousand, and spyrides, "hampers" (15:37), after the four thousand. Mark keeps them apart as well (8:19–20). A common guess is that the two words point to two different peoples; the text does not say so. What Jesus' questions stress is that on both occasions there was more left than was needed.
    4. Caesarea Philippi (v. 13). The place was a Gentile city about twenty-five miles north of the Sea of Galilee, at the foot of Mount Hermon, where a spring comes out of a cave under a cliff. The god Pan was worshipped there; Herod built a temple to Augustus nearby, and Philip the tetrarch rebuilt the city and named it for Caesar and for himself. The cult history here comes from a secondary summary, not from the ancient writers. One scholarly proposal reads the scene against the Book of Enoch, where the watchers came down on Hermon and Enoch sat "by the waters of Dan, south of west of Hermon": a commissioning at Hermon, with rock, gates and binding in the background. This study has not read the article itself, and none of the Greek words can be compared, so it holds the proposal open as a minority view and builds nothing on it.
    5. The answers (v. 14). Each name is a figure who returns. John is whom Herod feared (14:1–2); Elijah is promised at Malachi 4:5 and identified with John at Matthew 11:14 (see also 17:10–13); Jeremiah appears only here; and "one of the prophets" fits the prophet-like-Moses hope of Deuteronomy 18:15 and the crowds' "the prophet" at 21:11. None says "Anointed One," and none says "Son."
    6. Bar-Jonah, "flesh and blood," and "blessed" (v. 17). John 1:42 has "son of John," which may be a short form of the same name. The name Jonah sits in this scene twice: in the only sign given (16:4) and as Simon's father. The text does not join them, and this study only notes it. "Flesh and blood" is the old idiom for a mere human being. The blessing is the kind pronounced on eyes that see (13:16), and the thing revealed is what Jesus thanked the Father for hiding from the wise and showing to little ones (11:25–27).
    7. The rock (v. 18). The texts first, and the readings briefly. (1) The rock is Simon himself: the pun is plain, "you are Peter, and on this rock," and "I also say to you" sets the naming beside his confession. (2) The rock is his confession, or the trust it expresses: one ancient Christian reading takes it so, pointing to the shift from "you" to "this rock" and to the shrewd builder of 7:24–25 who builds on what is heard and done. (3) The rock is Jesus: the Hebrew Scriptures call God "Rock" (tsur) often, as at Deuteronomy 32 and 2 Samuel 22, although the Greek Old Testament seldom puts petra there, and 1 Corinthians 10:4 calls the rock in the wilderness the Anointed One. Isaiah 51:1–2 makes Abraham the rock Israel was cut from, and a rabbinic story in a later collection (the Yalkut Shimoni, about the thirteenth century, with older material inside) has a king who builds on bedrock and finds it in Abraham. Across the seam: by 16:23 Simon is called a stumbling block (skandalon, the word of Isa 8:14's stone of stumbling), and unit 17 takes up how the rock and the stumbling block belong to one man.
    8. Assembly (v. 18). ekklēsia, "a gathering called out," is the Greek Old Testament's word for Israel gathered before Yahweh, "the day of the assembly" at Horeb (Deut 4:10; 9:10; 18:16). In the gospels it appears only here and at 18:17, both times in Matthew. A dispensational reading takes it as a new body distinct from the kingdom. Another reading hears Israel's own assembly, renewed round the Anointed One, and notes that "my assembly" sets it beside the older "assembly of Yahweh" (Deut 23:2–9).
    9. Gates of Hades (v. 18). Matthew's "will not prevail against it" is his own pairing; no Old Testament verse joins "prevail" to the gates. Two readings: the gates are defensive, a fortress the assembly storms; or the gates stand for the grip of death, which cannot hold what Jesus builds. Both hear a promise that death does not have the last word.
    10. Keys, binding and loosing (v. 19). "Bind" and "loose" were verbs of the Jewish world for ruling what is forbidden and what is permitted, and perhaps for putting under a ban and lifting it. Matthew uses the loosing verb of relaxing a commandment at 5:19 and repeats the whole saying to the community at 18:18; John 20:23 has forgiving and holding sins. The Greek says "will have been bound": a state already in place. One reading hears the skies ratifying what the earth does; the other hears the earth catching up with what the skies have already decided. This study renders it literally and rests nothing on the choice.
    11. The silence (v. 20). The order to tell no one runs through the book: to the cleansed leper (8:4), to the healed blind men (9:30), to the crowds, with Isaiah 42's "he will not quarrel or cry out" (12:16), and after the mountain, "until the Son of Man is raised from the dead" (17:9). The silence has an end date, and this verse is the middle of it.

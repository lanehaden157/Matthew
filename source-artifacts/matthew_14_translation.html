The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

Rejection, a Beheading, Bread, and the Sea

Tharseite, egō eimi · mē phobeisthe — take heart: I am — do not fear
Unit 14 · Matthew 13:54–14:36 · a hometown trips, a king feasts, a wilderness is fed, and the sea is walked

Recurring roots — colour key

    Five answers to who he is · 13:54–14:36

    The order of the unit, not a ring. Each scene gives a different answer: offence, guilty fear, hunger, terror turned to worship, and the reach of a hand.

    Nazareth 13:54–58→ Herod's hall 14:1–12→ the wilderness 14:13–21→ the sea 14:22–33→ Gennesaret 14:34–36

    "From where?" — a frame around the family · 13:54–56

    A simple frame, not a chiasm: the town asks the same question twice, pothen toutō, "from where, to this man?", and puts his family in between as if that settled it. The reader already has the other answer: "from the Holy Spirit" (1:18, 20).

    Two tables — Herod's feast and the wilderness meal · 14:6–21

    A diptych: two meals set side by side, joined by "heard" (14:1, 14:13) and by verbs that really repeat in the Greek. The reclining words differ (synanakeimai, "lie at table with"; anaklinō, "lean back"), so the pairing rests on the scene and on these.

    A prophet in his hometown · 13:54–58

    54And coming into his hometown, he was teaching them in their gathering-place, so that they were struck out of themselves and said, "From where does this man get this wisdom and the acts of power?

    Ekplēssomai, "be struck out of oneself," is the crowds' astonishment at the Sermon (7:28); here it sours. Dynamis, "power," in the plural is "acts of power," the study's word for what English calls miracles.

    55Isn't this the builder's son? Isn't his mother called Mary, and his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?1

    Tektōn is a craftsman in wood or stone, a builder. Mark has Jesus himself as "the builder"; Matthew moves the trade to the father.2

    56And his sisters, aren't they all with us? From where, then, does this man get all these things?"

    57And they were tripped up in him. But Jesus said to them, "A prophet is not without honor except in the hometown and in his own house."

    Skandalizō, "trip up," from skandalon, the trigger-stick of a trap; the form says it kept happening. Atimos, "without honor," is literally "without worth."
    Jer 11:21; 12:6 — the men of Jeremiah's own town seek his life, and of his brothers and his father's house he is told, "do not trust them"; and Mic 7:6, which Jesus applied at 10:36: "a son dishonors his father … a man's enemies are the men of his house," where "dishonors" shares the stem of "without honor" here.

    Luke 4:16–30 — a launching scene, or one more refusal

    Luke 4:22–24"Isn't this Joseph's son?" … "Surely y'all will tell me this parable, 'Physician, heal yourself' … Amen I say to y'all, no prophet is dektos, acceptable, in his hometown."
    Luke 4:29–30"And rising up they threw him out of the town and led him to the brow of the hill … to throw him down. But he, passing through the middle of them, went on his way."
    Matt 13:57"And they were tripped up in him … 'A prophet is not without honor except in the hometown and in his own house.'"

    Luke puts Nazareth at the very start of the ministry, with the reading from Isaiah, the examples of Elijah and Elisha helping foreigners, and a mob at the cliff. Matthew keeps Mark's later place and gives no reading, no violence, and no stated reason beyond the family. In Matthew it is not a manifesto but one more refusal in a run of them (11:16–24; 12:24, 38–45).

    58And he did not do many acts of power there, because of their not-trusting.3

    Apistia is the negative of pistis, "trust": not a wrong opinion but reliance withheld.

    Mark 6:3–6 — "could not" becomes "did not"

    Mark 6:3"Isn't this the builder, the son of Mary, and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon?"
    Mark 6:4"A prophet is not without honor except in his hometown and among his relatives and in his house."
    Mark 6:5–6"And he could not do any act of power there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them. And he marveled because of their not-trusting."
    Matt 13:58"And he did not do many acts of power there, because of their not-trusting."

    Matthew turns inability into restraint and drops Jesus' own amazement, but keeps the cause, so the limit sits on Nazareth's side, not on his power. He also moves the trade off Jesus and onto his father, and drops "among his relatives," which leaves "his own house," the household word of the thread.

    Herod hears, and how John died · 14:1–12

    1At that season Herod the quarter-ruler heard the report of Jesus,4

    Tetraarchēs, "ruler of a quarter" of a divided kingdom: Herod Antipas, son of the Herod who hunted the child in chapter 2. He literally "heard the hearing."

    2and he said to his servants, "This is John the Immerser. He has been raised from the dead, and because of this the acts of power are at work in him."

    "Has been raised" is in the passive, the form that usually leaves God as the one acting. Mark and Luke give the crowds' guesses (Elijah, a prophet); Matthew gives only Herod's own certainty.

    3For Herod, having seized John, bound him and put him away in prison because of Herodias, the wife of Philip his brother;5

    Phylakē is a guard, a prison, or a watch of the night; the same word comes back at 14:10 and 14:25.

    4for John had been saying to him, "It is not permitted for you to have her."6

    "Had been saying" renders an ongoing past: he said it again and again. Exestin, "it is permitted," is the legal-question word the Pharisees threw at the learners (12:2).
    Lev 18:16; 20:21 — the Torah forbids uncovering a brother's wife and calls a man who takes her "uncleanness."

    5And wanting to kill him, he feared the crowd, because they held him as a prophet.

    Matt 21:26, 46 — the temple leaders will fear the crowd because "all hold John as a prophet," and then fear the crowds over Jesus, "because they held him as a prophet."

    Mark 6:19–26 · Luke 9:7–9 — who wanted John dead

    Mark 6:19–20"Herodias held it against him and wanted to kill him, and could not; for Herod feared John, knowing him a righteous and holy man, and kept him safe; and hearing him he was much at a loss, and he heard him gladly."
    Mark 6:23"Whatever you ask me, I will give you, up to half of my kingdom."
    Luke 9:9"John I beheaded; but who is this about whom I hear such things?" And he was seeking to see him.
    Matt 14:5"And wanting to kill him, he [Herod] feared the crowd."

    Matthew moves the wish to kill from Herodias onto Herod, and swaps Herod's awe of John for fear of the crowd: a simpler and darker king. He also drops Mark's guest list and the "half my kingdom" offer, the line that sounds like the Persian king in Esther. Luke tells no banquet at all and leaves Herod curious; Matthew's Herod is already sure.

    6But when Herod's birthday came, the daughter of Herodias danced in the middle and pleased Herod,7

    Pharaoh's birthday feast Gen 40:1–3, 19–22

    Pharaoh's chief cupbearer and chief baker offend him and are put in the royal prison, where Joseph is held. Joseph reads their dreams: in three days Pharaoh will lift up the cupbearer's head and restore him, and will take the baker's head off him and hang him on a tree. On the third day, Pharaoh's birthday, he makes a feast for all his servants, restores the cupbearer, and hangs the baker, as Joseph had said.

    • a ruler's birthday feast
    • a man held in the ruler's prison (the same Greek word, phylakē)
    • a head taken off at the feast's decision

    Pharaoh's sentence is his own judgment on his own servant; Herod's is a girl's request that he is ashamed to refuse. Joseph, the interpreter in Pharaoh's prison, is later raised to rule; the prophet in Herod's prison dies there. The words for "birthday" are related (genesis there, genesia here), not the same: the link is the shape of the scene, not a shared phrase.

    It sets Herod's hall in the pattern of a foreign court where a ruler's party decides who keeps his head, and makes "the king" of 14:9 the Pharaoh of the scene.

    7so that with an oath he declared he would give her whatever she might ask.

    Matt 5:33–37 — "do not swear at all": Jesus' ban on oaths, against which Herod's oaths (14:7, 9) bind him to a killing.

    8And she, coached by her mother, says, "Give me here, on a platter, the head of John the Immerser."

    Probibazō, "coach, put forward," is rare. In the Greek of Deut 6:7 it is the word for parents teaching their children the commands: here a mother teaches a killing.

    9And the king, grieved, because of the oaths and those reclining at table with him, commanded it to be given,8

    Called "quarter-ruler" in 14:1, he is "the king" now, inside his own court. The gospel's true king was asked after by the magi (2:2). He fears the crowd (14:5), then his guests: he is ruled by both.

    10and he sent and beheaded John in the prison.

    11And his head was brought on a platter and given to the little girl, and she brought it to her mother.

    Korasion, "little girl," is used elsewhere in Matthew only for Jairus's daughter (9:24–25), the girl who was raised.

    12And his learners came and took up the corpse and buried him, and they went and reported to Jesus.

    Matt 27:57–60 — "when evening came" a learner of Jesus asks for his body, Pilate "commanded it to be given back," and he takes the body and lays it in a tomb; Herod had "commanded it to be given" (14:9), and John's learners take up his corpse and bury him.

    Elijah, Ahab and Jezebel 1 Kgs 18:17–18; 19:1–6; 21:17–29

    King Ahab of Israel has married Jezebel, a foreign princess who backs Baal's prophets and kills Yahweh's. When Ahab calls Elijah "the troubler of Israel," Elijah answers to his face that the troubler is Ahab and his father's house. After the contest on Carmel, Jezebel sends word that by this time tomorrow she will have Elijah's life; he is afraid and runs into the wilderness, where bread is set by his head. Later, when Jezebel has Naboth killed so that Ahab can take his vineyard, Elijah meets the king there with God's sentence on him and on the queen; Ahab tears his clothes and humbles himself, and the judgment is put off.

    • a prophet who tells a king to his face that he is in the wrong
    • a queen who wants the prophet dead
    • a king who goes along with her

    Elijah escapes into the wilderness and is fed there; John stays in the prison and is killed. Ahab is shaken into humbling himself; Herod is grieved and keeps his oath anyway. And here the queen gets what Jezebel never did.

    Jesus has already said that John "is Elijah who is to come" (11:14), so the old story runs again with its ending changed for the worse. Right after it, Jesus goes into a wilderness where bread is given.

    Matt 17:10–13: "Elijah has already come … they did to him whatever they wished; so also the Son of Man is about to suffer at their hands."

    Bread in the wilderness · 14:13–21

    13Now Jesus, having heard, withdrew from there in a boat to a wilderness place by himself; and the crowds, having heard, followed him on foot from the towns.9

    Two hearings hinge the chapter: Herod heard and feared (14:1); Jesus hears and withdraws. Anachōreō, "withdraw," is Matthew's standing verb for getting out of a killer's way (2:12–22; 4:12; 12:15).

    14And coming out, he saw a large crowd, and he was wrenched in the gut over them, and he healed their sick.

    Splanchnizomai, from splanchna, the inward organs: pity felt as a blow to the gut (as at 9:36).

    Mark 6:30–34 — why he withdrew, and what he did there

    Mark 6:30–31"And the emissaries gather to Jesus and reported to him all they had done and taught. And he says to them, 'Come here, y'all yourselves, privately, into a wilderness place, and rest a little.'"
    Mark 6:34"He was wrenched in the gut over them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd, and he began to teach them many things."
    Matt 14:13–14"Having heard, he withdrew … he was wrenched in the gut over them, and he healed their sick."

    In Mark the cause is the Twelve's return and their need for rest; in Matthew it is the news of John's death, so the feeding follows straight on from Herod's feast. Matthew has already used the shepherdless-sheep line (9:36) and does not repeat it; and where Mark's Jesus teaches the crowd, Matthew's heals them.

    15When evening came, the learners came to him saying, "The place is a wilderness, and the hour has already gone by. Send away the crowds, so that they may go off into the villages and buy themselves food."

    Apolyō, "send away, release," is also the word for releasing a prisoner and for divorce.

    16But Jesus said to them, "They have no need to go off. Y'all yourselves give them something to eat."

    "Y'all yourselves" renders an emphatic hymeis: the learners' own hands are named before the loaves are.

    17They say to him, "We have nothing here except five loaves and two fish."

    18And he said, "Bring them here to me."

    19And commanding the crowds to lean back on the grass, taking the five loaves and the two fish, looking up into the sky, he spoke the blessing, and breaking, he gave the loaves to the learners, and the learners to the crowds.10

    Took, spoke the blessing, broke, gave: the four actions of the last meal (26:26), in the same order.

    20And they all ate and were fed full, and they took up what was left over of the broken pieces, twelve baskets full.11

    Chortazō, "feed full," is built on chortos, the grass they are sitting on: they are pastured. "Took up" is the verb used for John's corpse (14:12).

    Elisha's twenty loaves 2 Kgs 4:38–44

    There is famine in the land, and Elisha is at Gilgal with the company of prophets. A man from Baal-shalishah brings him twenty barley loaves from the first of the harvest. Elisha says, "Give to the people, and let them eat." His servant protests, "What, shall I set this before a hundred men?" Elisha says it again: "Give to the people and let them eat, for thus says Yahweh: they will eat and leave some over." They eat, and some is left over, as Yahweh had said.

    • "give … to eat," said to the one who must hand it out
    • a protest that the supply is far too small
    • everyone eats, and some is left over

    Twenty loaves for a hundred men is one loaf for every five; five loaves for five thousand men is one for every thousand, before the women and children are counted. Elisha passes on Yahweh's word ("thus says Yahweh"); Jesus speaks no oracle, says "bring them here to me," and does the breaking himself.

    It puts the learners in the servant's place, protesting, and Jesus in the prophet's, so the scene reads as a prophet's sign on a vastly greater scale.

    John 6:9 keeps Elisha's barley loaves, which Matthew leaves out.

    21And those eating were about five thousand men, apart from women and children.12

    Bread from the sky in the wilderness Exod 16:4, 13–21; Num 11:13, 21

    After the sea, Israel is hungry in the wilderness and complains. Yahweh tells Moses, "Look, I am raining bread from the sky for y'all," and each morning the manna lies on the ground; each gathers what he needs, and what is kept overnight breeds worms and stinks. Later, when the people crave meat, Moses asks, "From where am I to get meat to give all this people?" and counts them: "six hundred thousand men on foot."

    • a wilderness place and a crowd with no food
    • a crowd counted by its men, "on foot" (related words: pezos there, pezē at 14:13)
    • bread looked for from the sky
    • a leader faced with too many mouths

    Israel grumbles; this crowd has just been healed. Manna falls and cannot be kept; here the bread is broken from five loaves, and twelve baskets of pieces are taken up afterward. Matthew never says "manna" or "Moses."

    It lets a reader who knows the wilderness story hear the one who looks up to the sky and gives bread in the place Yahweh held there, without Matthew saying so.

    Matt 15:33 asks Moses' "from where?" outright; John 6:31–35 names the manna.

    John 6:1–15 — Passover, a boy, and a crowd that wants a king

    John 6:4, 9"Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was near." … "There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two small fish; but what are these for so many?"
    John 6:14–15"This is truly the prophet who is coming into the world." … Knowing that they were about to come and seize him to make him king, he withdrew again to the mountain, himself alone.
    Matt 14:21–23"Apart from women and children." … He compelled the learners into the boat, sent the crowds away, and went up into the mountain by himself to pray.

    John spells out what Matthew leaves for the reader: the Passover, Elisha's barley, the crowd's "the prophet," and the push to make him king that explains why he sends everyone away. Matthew keeps the mountain and the solitude, says none of it, and adds the women and children to the count.

    Walking on the sea · 14:22–33

    22And at once he compelled the learners to get into a boat and go ahead of him to the other side, until he should send away the crowds.

    Anankazō, "compel, force": a strong word. Matthew gives no reason for it.

    23And having sent away the crowds, he went up into the mountain by himself to pray. When evening came, he was there alone.

    24But the boat was already many stadia away from the land, tormented by the waves, for the wind was against it.

    A stadion is about 185 meters. "Tormented" is basanizō, the word for the paralyzed servant (8:6) and the demons' dread (8:29).

    25And in the fourth watch of the night he came toward them, walking on the sea.

    The fourth watch is the last stretch before dawn, roughly 3 to 6 a.m. Phylakē, "watch," is the same word as John's "prison" (14:3, 10).

    The night at the sea Exod 14:19–31; 15:8

    Israel is trapped between Pharaoh's army and the sea. Moses stretches out his hand over the sea, and Yahweh drives it back with a strong wind all night; Israel goes into the middle of the sea on dry ground, the water a wall on either side. In the morning watch Yahweh looks down on the Egyptian camp from the pillar of fire and cloud and throws it into confusion. The sea comes back, and the Egyptians sink.

    • a night at the sea and a strong wind
    • rescue in the last watch before dawn (the same word, phylakē)
    • a stretched-out hand (ekteinō … cheir, as at 14:31)
    • terror at the water, and someone going under

    Israel goes through the sea between its walls; Jesus walks on top of it, and the learners do not cross at all until he is in the boat. Some readers doubt the link for exactly that reason. In Exodus the sea comes first and the bread after; here the bread comes first.

    It frames the night as a rescue at sea by the God who made a road through it. Peter, sinking, is pulled out where Pharaoh's army was not.

    Ps 77:19 — "your way was in the sea, your paths in the great waters, and your footprints were not known"; Isa 43:16, "who makes a way in the sea."

    26But the learners, seeing him walking on the sea, were thrown into turmoil, saying, "It is an apparition!" and from fear they cried out.13

    27But at once Jesus spoke to them, saying, "Take heart: I am. Do not fear."14

    Greek (translit.)Tharseite, egō eimi; mē phobeisthe.
    NASB"Take courage, it is I; do not be afraid."
    This study"Take heart: I am. Do not fear." — the bare words, so both hearings stay open: "it's me," and Isaiah's "I am he."
    ParallelsMark 6:50, the same three words; John 6:20, "I am; do not fear."
    Isa 41:10; 43:2, 5 — in the Greek, God says "do not fear, for I am with you … I am your God," and "if you pass through water, I am with you."

    28And Peter answered him and said, "Master, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters."15

    The "if" grants the point more than it doubts it: "since it is you." Peter asks the one who commanded the crowds (14:19) for a command of his own.

    29And he said, "Come." And going down from the boat, Peter walked on the waters and came toward Jesus.

    30But seeing the wind, strong, he was afraid, and beginning to sink into the deep, he cried out, saying, "Master, save me!"16

    Ps 69:1–2 (68:2–3 in the Greek) — "Save me, O God, for the waters have come in up to my life … I came into the depths of the sea, and a storm sank me," where "sank" is katapontizō, the rare verb of Peter's "sink into the deep."

    31And at once Jesus, stretching out his hand, took hold of him, and says to him, "Little-faith, why did you waver?"17

    Oligopiste, a one-word address, "little-trust one": the nickname of 6:30 and 8:26, now for one learner only. Distazō, "waver," is built on dis, "in two."
    Ps 144:7 (143:7 in the Greek) — "send out your hand from on high; rescue me and deliver me out of many waters," and Ps 18:16 (17:17 in the Greek), "he sent from on high and took me; he drew me out of many waters."

    32And when they had gone up into the boat, the wind dropped.

    Kopazō, "grow tired, drop": the wind is not charged this time; it simply gives out.

    The first storm Matt 8:23–27

    Earlier, Jesus gets into a boat and his learners follow him. A great shaking comes up on the sea, so that the boat is covered by the waves, and he is asleep. They wake him: "Master, save — we are being destroyed!" He says, "Why are y'all cowards, little-faiths?", gets up, charges the winds and the sea sharply, and there is a great stillness. The men marvel: "What sort of man is this, that even the winds and the sea obey him?"

    • a boat beaten "by the waves" (the same phrase, hypo tōn kymatōn)
    • "Master, save" (Kyrie, sōson)
    • "little-faith"
    • the wind stops when he acts

    There Jesus is in the boat, asleep, and stills the storm with a word; here he is absent, comes across the water, and the wind simply drops once he is aboard. There the whole boat cries "save" and is called "little-faiths"; here one man cries it and is called "Little-faith."

    The first boat asks, "What sort of man is this?" The second answers, bowing low: "Truly you are God's Son."

    Matt 16:8 — "little-faiths" once more, crossing the lake, over forgotten bread.

    33And those in the boat worshiped him, saying, "Truly you are God's Son."18

    Matt 27:54 — the centurion and the guard at the cross: "Truly this was God's Son," the boat's own words.

    Mark 6:45–52 · John 6:16–21 — hardened hearts, or worship

    Mark 6:48"He comes toward them walking on the sea, and he wanted to pass them by."
    Mark 6:51–52"And they were utterly beside themselves, for they had not understood about the loaves; their heart was hardened."
    John 6:20–21"I am; do not fear." They wanted to take him into the boat, and at once the boat was at the land they were heading for.
    Matt 14:28–33Peter: "command me to come to you on the waters" … "Little-faith, why did you waver?" … "And those in the boat worshiped him: 'Truly you are God's Son.'"

    Only Matthew has Peter on the water. Where Mark ends in hardened hearts and John in a sudden landing, Matthew ends in worship and confession, the answer to the question of the first boat (8:27). He also drops Mark's "he wanted to pass them by," the phrase that recalls God passing by Moses, and saves the learners' failure to understand the loaves for a later boat (16:5–12).

    Gennesaret: the fringe of his cloak · 14:34–36

    34And crossing over, they came onto the land at Gennesaret.

    Gennesaret is the fertile plain on the northwest shore of the lake, south of Capernaum.

    35And the men of that place, recognizing him, sent into all that surrounding region and brought to him all who were in a bad way,

    36and they kept urging him that they might only touch the fringe of his cloak; and as many as touched were brought safely through.19

    Kraspedon is the tassel Israel was told to wear (Num 15:38–39). Diesōthēsan, "brought safely through," is the compound of sōzō, the word Peter cried at 14:30. His hometown would not trust him (13:58); this district asks only to touch him.
    Zech 8:23 — ten men from all the nations take hold of the fringe of a Jew's cloak, saying, "let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you."

    Notes

    1. The brothers and sisters (vv. 55–56). Four brothers are named and the sisters are not. Three old readings stand: children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus; children of Joseph by an earlier marriage; or cousins, a reading that points to "Mary the mother of James and Joseph" at the cross (27:56). Matthew does not settle it. James later leads the Jerusalem assembly (Gal 1:19), and the brothers seem not to have trusted him before the resurrection (John 7:5). The same family stood outside at 12:46–50. Compare Mark 6:3 ↗.
    2. "The builder's son" (v. 55). Tektōn covers craftsmen and builders in wood and stone ↗. One reading hears a put-down in it: in that world a son was expected to follow his father's trade, and a builder's son who teaches like this has stepped out of his place. Calling a man "the son of" someone, rather than by his own name, could itself diminish him.
    3. Not-trusting (v. 58). Apistia is tagged with the trust thread as its negative form. A dispensational reading takes the scene as a step in Jesus' turn from a nation that will not trust him to training his learners, and notes that people who did not trust him did not ask; readings of the kingdom as already breaking in hear him regathering Israel around himself rather than turning from it. Both read the same sequence and disagree about what it means for Israel.
    4. Quarter-ruler and king (vv. 1, 9). Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea under Rome from 4 BC to AD 39, with the title tetrarch, never king. Mark calls him "King Herod" throughout (Mark 6:14). Matthew's own narration says "the quarter-ruler" (14:1), and he becomes "the king" only inside the story (14:9), the title his court gives him. Luke also says tetrarch (Luke 3:1; 9:7).
    5. Josephus on John (vv. 3–5). Josephus (Antiquities 18.109–119) says Antipas divorced the daughter of the Nabatean king Aretas to marry Herodias, which led to a war Antipas lost. Some Jews took that defeat as God's punishment for killing John. Josephus gives a political motive: Antipas feared that John's hold on the crowds might turn to revolt, so he held him at the fortress of Machaerus and killed him there. The gospels give a moral one, and both can be true. ↗
    6. Why the marriage was barred (v. 4). The Torah forbids taking a brother's wife (Lev 18:16; 20:21), with the levirate exception when the brother has died childless (Deut 25:5). A patristic reading points out that Philip was alive and Herodias had a daughter by him, so the exception could not apply. ↗
    7. The dancer (v. 6). Matthew does not name her; she is "the daughter of Herodias" and "the little girl." Josephus elsewhere gives her name as Salome. That the dance was sensual is often assumed from the Herods' reputation, but the text does not say so.
    8. Banquet and decree (v. 9). Third Maccabees 5 has a Second Temple version of the same shape: King Ptolemy turns to drinking, commands those present to recline opposite him, and orders drunken elephants loosed on the Jews, while "those reclining with him" look on. The Greek shares both of Matthew's reclining words (synanakeimai, 14:9; anaklinō, 14:19) and his "command" (keleuō), but not the plot: no prophet, no oath, and in 3 Maccabees the Jews are delivered.
    9. Withdrawal (v. 13). In the Greek of Exod 2:15, Moses "withdrew from the face of Pharaoh" when Pharaoh sought to kill him: the same verb, a deliverer getting out of a killing king's reach. Matthew used it already for the magi and Joseph escaping Herod the Great (2:12–22), and for Jesus when John was handed over (4:12). ↗
    10. "Spoke the blessing" (v. 19). Eulogeō ↗, "speak well of": the Jewish blessing said over bread, which praises God as its giver, rather than a blessing put on the bread. Rendered "spoke the blessing" rather than the flattened "blessed," which also blurs it with makarios, the beatitude word. Flagged for Lane. The four actions return at the last meal (26:26), and at the second feeding "gave thanks" stands in for "spoke the blessing" (15:36).
    11. Fed full, and twelve baskets (v. 20). Chortazō ↗ began as a farming word, "feed with fodder," from chortos, "grass, pasture." It is the verb of Ps 132:15 (131:15 in the Greek), "her poor I will feed full with bread," and of the beatitude "they shall be fed full" (5:6). A dispensational reading sees no symbolism in the five and the two and takes the twelve baskets as one for each learner; others hear Israel's twelve tribes in them. The text leaves it unsaid. Matthew keeps this basket word, kophinos, apart from the "hampers" of the four thousand (spyris, 15:37), and Jesus asks about both by name (16:9–10).
    12. Counting the men (v. 21). Mark, Luke and John count "men" too (Mark 6:44; Luke 9:14; John 6:10); only Matthew adds "apart from women and children," and he repeats it at the four thousand (15:38). Counting the men has precedent in the exodus: "six hundred thousand men on foot, besides the baggage" (Exod 12:37 in the Greek). The whole crowd here would have been far larger than five thousand.
    13. "An apparition" (v. 26). Phantasma ↗ is a thing that appears: an apparition or a phantom, not specifically the spirit of a dead person, which "ghost" suggests. Its only use in the Greek Old Testament is Wis 17:14, in the plague of darkness on Egypt, where the Egyptians are driven by apparitions and "sudden and unexpected fear" pours over them. Here too it comes with "from fear they cried out."
    14. Egō eimi, "I am" (v. 27). The words are ordinary self-identification, "it's me," and in the dark that is enough. But the Greek Isaiah uses egō eimi for God's own self-declaration, "that I am he" (Isa 43:10; also 41:4; 51:12) ↗, and Isaiah 41 and 43 set it beside "do not fear" and the waters, the same cluster found here. The Greek of Exod 3:14 reads "I am the one who is" (egō eimi ho ōn), so the closer tie is to Isaiah. A dispensational reading takes the words as a claim to deity that the learners grasped only later; others hear first the reassurance and the presence promise, "God with us" (1:23), which Jesus speaks in his own voice at 18:20 and 28:20. Matthew's own usage is mixed. He keeps a bare egō eimi here, but where Mark's Jesus answers the high priest "I am" (Mark 14:62), Matthew's says "you have said" (26:64). The readings are not exclusive, and Matthew leaves it for the reader to hear.
    15. Peter on the water (vv. 28–31). Only Matthew tells this. A patristic reading praises his zeal and love, since he asks to be commanded and not just allowed, and says the sinking came from weak trust, not from the wind. A Reformation reading calls the request rash and the rescue a lesson in humility. A dispensational reading makes him both a good example and a bad one, and the first of three scenes where Peter is singled out (14:28; 16:13–23; 17:24–27). The text says he sank when he saw the wind; it does not say whether he should have asked.
    16. "Sink into the deep" (v. 30). Katapontizomai ↗, "be sunk in the open sea," occurs in the New Testament only here and at 18:6, where it is the fate fit for whoever trips up one of the little ones. In the Greek Psalms it is the drowning man's word: "a storm sank me" (Ps 69:2, 68:3 in the Greek).
    17. "Waver" (v. 31). Distazō ↗ is built on dis, "twice, in two": to hesitate between two ways, not to disbelieve outright. Its only other New Testament use is 28:17, where the learners bow low and some waver, the same pairing as this scene. "Master" renders kyrios as an address, as elsewhere in this study.
    18. "Truly you are God's Son" (v. 33). This is the first time the learners say it together. The voice at the baptism said "my Son" (3:17), the tester said "if you are God's Son" (4:3, 6), and the demons said it (8:29). Peter's fuller confession, "the Son of the living God," comes at 16:16. A dispensational reading takes the title here as messianic, true but not yet mature, given their later lapses; others hear the gospel's high view of Jesus arriving early, sealed by the bowing low.
    19. "Brought safely through" (v. 36). Diesōthēsan is a compound of sōzō, "save," tagged with that thread as a form sharing the stem. The fringe joins this summary to the woman of 9:20–22, who touched it and was "saved," and sets it against the leaders' enlarged tassels at 23:5. A dispensational reading also notes that Jesus is touched by crowds of the sick and is not made unclean; he makes them whole instead, which sets up the argument about clean and unclean in 15:1–20.

The Two Ways

ἡ ὁδὸς ἡ ἀπάγουσα εἰς τὴν ζωήν — hē hodos hē apagousa eis tēn zōēn, "the road that leads off to life"
Unit 7 · Matthew 7:1–29 · the close of the Sermon on the Mount

Two further words are marked in gold italic rather than tracked, because each occurs once here but detonates later in the gospel: ἀνομία (anomia, "lawlessness," v. 23) and ἐξουσία (exousia, "authority," v. 29).

The diptych — 6:19–34 answered by 7:1–11

Chapter 7 does not begin a new movement. Its first eleven verses are the second wing of a two-panel structure that opened at 6:19, each panel running the same three beats in the same order.

Panel one · 6:19–34 — God and wealthPanel two · 7:1–11 — God and neighbor
6:19–21 Allegiance: treasure on earth or treasure in the skies7:1–2 Allegiance: the judgment you deal out is dealt back
6:22–24 Two small parables: the good and the evil ὀφθαλμός (ophthalmos, eye) · two masters who cannot be mixed7:3–6 Two small parables: the rafter in the ὀφθαλμός (ophthalmos, eye) · holy things and dogs, which cannot be mixed
6:25–34 Anxiety — and the Father who feeds and clothes, so seek first7:7–11 Asking — and the Father who gives good things to those who seek
7:12 — capstone over both panels: "this is the Law and the Prophets"

The Law and the Prophets — an inclusio spanning three chapters · 5:17 → 7:12

The same four-word phrase opens and seals the body of the Sermon. Everything between the two is offered as an exposition of what Israel's scriptures were driving at.

A5:17
"Don't think I came to tear down τὸν νόμον ἢ τοὺς προφήτας (ton nomon ē tous prophētas), the Law or the Prophets" — the frame opens
B5:21–48
Six case studies: "y'all heard it said… but I say to y'all" — Torah read to its root
C6:1–18
The three practices — giving, praying, fasting — with the Father's Prayer at the exact center — the pivot of the whole Sermon
B′6:19–7:11
Six examples of loving God and neighbor, in two matching panels — see the diptych above
A′7:12
"For this is ὁ νόμος καὶ οἱ προφῆται (ho nomos kai hoi prophētai), the Law and the Prophets" — the frame seals

Matthew uses this exact phrase in a summarizing position only one other time, at 22:40, where the whole Law and the Prophets hang on love of God and love of neighbor. Both summaries are, underneath, Leviticus 19:18.

The holy and the dogs — a four-cornered turn · 7:6

The Greek pronoun in the second half is masculine plural, which pins the trampling to the pearls — so the two animals do not share the two crimes. Pigs waste; dogs attack. Read A B B′ A′.

B6b

B′6c
They stomp them underfoot — the pigs, wasting the pearls
A′6d

Three images of the choice · 7:13–27

The Sermon ends the way Deuteronomy ends — not with more instruction but with a fork, blessing and curse, and a demand for decision. Three paired images, not four: the "Master, Master" scene belongs to the fruit-test, not beside it.

ImageThe pairWhat it tests
1 · vv. 13–14Two gates, two roads — roomy and squeezedWhere you are going
2 · vv. 15–23Two prophets, two trees — and the fruit each makes, up to and including miracles done in his nameWhat you are producing
3 · vv. 24–27Two builders, two houses — identical storm, opposite outcomeWhat is underneath

The last discourse ends the same way: three paired parables at 25:1–46 — wise and foolish women, faithful and wicked slave, sheep and goats. The adjectives that sort the two builders here, φρόνιμος (phronimos, shrewd) and μωρός (mōros, stupid), are the same two that sort the ten women at 25:2. Jesus opens and closes his public teaching on one note.

Ezekiel 13 under the last two images

False prophets → a storm → a structure down with its footing laid bare. Matthew runs the prophet's sequence in the prophet's order.

Ezekiel 13:8–16Matthew 7:15–27
Prophets who see false visions and say peace where there is no peaceFalse prophets in sheep-clothes, wolves inside (v. 15)
They build a wall and slather it with whitewash — it looks soundTwo houses that look identical until the weather comes (vv. 24, 26)
Flooding rain, hailstones, a storm-wind sent to tear it downThe rain came down, the rivers came, the winds blew (vv. 25, 27)
The wall falls; its foundation is stripped nakedIt fell — and its fall was great (v. 27)

Read against Ezekiel 13, the two builders stop being a lesson about personal resilience and start sounding like a judgment oracle over a people trusting a false peace — which, for a gospel written within a few years of AD 70, has a city and a temple attached to it.

The formula that closes all five discourses

καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς (kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iēsous) — "and it happened when Jesus finished." Matthew uses this construction five times and nowhere else. Unit 7 supplies the first.

7:28 Sermon→ 11:1 Mission→ 13:53 Parables→ 19:1 Community→ 26:1 Olivet

Image of the eye · 7:1–5

1Don't y'all judge, so that y'all not be judged.1

Plural, aimed at the whole community. The unnamed judge in the second half is God — a reverent Jewish way of saying "God will judge you" without saying God.

2For with whatever verdict y'all judge, y'all will be judged; and with whatever measure y'all measure out, it will be measured back to y'all.2

Five forms of one root, then three of another, stacked in a single line. It is a jingle in Greek and probably a proverb already in circulation.

3And why do you stare at the wood-chip in your brother's eye, but the roof-beam in your own eye — you don't even take it in?

The Greek switches here from plural to singular and stays singular through v. 5: the rule went to everybody, the joke turns and points at one man. κάρφος (karphos) is a dry twig or chip off a threshing floor; δοκός (dokos) is a load-bearing rafter. Builder's-shop humor.

4Or how will you say to your brother, "Let me throw out the wood-chip from your eye" — and look, the roof-beam is in your own eye!

5Mask-wearer! Throw out the roof-beam from your own eye first, and then you'll see clear through to throw out the wood-chip from your brother's eye.3

Greek ὑποκριτά (hypokrita) NASB "hypocrite" Wooden stage-actor, one who speaks from behind a mask Text-form In the Greek Old Testament this word renders Hebrew חָנֵף (ḥanef) — not "pretender" but "godless, polluted." Both charges ride along. The speck still comes out. What is forbidden is operating blind, not operating.

Image of the holy thing · 7:6

6Don't y'all give the holy thing to the dogs, and don't throw y'all's pearls out in front of the pigs — or else they'll stomp them down with their feet, and wheel around and rip y'all open.4

Greek τὸ ἅγιον… τοὺς μαργαρίτας (to hagion… tous margaritas) NASB "what is holy" Wooden the consecrated portion — the altar-meat only priests may eat Text-form Exodus 22:31 fixes the opposite rule: torn meat is thrown to dogs. Dogs get the profane; the holy never leaves the sanctuary. The dog here is κύων (kyōn), the scavenging street dog — not κυνάριον (kynarion), the household pup Jesus uses at 15:26, which the Canaanite woman will seize on and turn into an argument. Matthew has the harsh word here and the gentle word there on purpose.

Image of the asking child · 7:7–11

7Ask — and it will be given to y'all. Seek — and y'all will find. Knock — and it will be opened to y'all.5

Three commands, three promises, two of them again with God kept offstage in the passive. "Seek" is the same verb as 6:33, seek first the kingdom, and it carries exile freight: Deut 4:29, Jer 29:13, Isa 55:6 all promise a scattered people that God is still findable.

8For everyone asking receives, and the one seeking finds, and to the one knocking it will be opened.

"Everyone" — no qualifying clause, no worthiness test.

9Or what person is there among y'all whose son will ask him for a loaf — he won't hand him a stone, will he?

10Or he'll ask for a fish — he won't hand him a snake, will he?

Both pairs work by resemblance: a flat loaf and a smooth stone, an eel-like fish and a serpent. The bad father in view isn't cruel, he's a passer of convincing counterfeits.

11So if y'all, being evil, know how to give good gifts to y'all's children, how much more will y'all's Father — the one in the skies — give good things to those asking him!6

A light-to-heavy argument, what the rabbis call qal vaḥomer. "Being evil" is dropped in as a shared premise, no argument offered — same root as the evil eye of 6:23 and the evil one of 6:13.

The capstone · 7:12

12So then — everything, however much y'all want people to do to y'all, that's what y'all do to them. For this is the Law and the Prophets.7

Greek οὗτος γάρ ἐστιν ὁ νόμος καὶ οἱ προφῆται (houtos gar estin ho nomos kai hoi prophētai) NASB "this is the Law and the Prophets" Wooden this — this single sentence — is the Law and the Prophets Note The "so then" reaches back past vv. 7–11 all the way to 5:17. It concludes the whole body of the Sermon, not the paragraph.

Image one: two gates, two roads · 7:13–14

13Come in through the narrow gate. Because wide is the gate and roomy is the road that leads off into ruin — and many are the ones coming in through it.8

14Because narrow is the gate and squeezed is the road that leads off into life — and few are the ones who find it.

Greek τεθλιμμένη ἡ ὁδός (tethlimmenē hē hodos) NASB "narrow" Wooden pressed, squeezed, crushed-in — from θλίβω (thlibō), the root of θλῖψις (thlipsis), "tribulation" Text-form SBLGNT reads ὅτι στενή (hoti stenē), "because narrow"; a well-attested variant reads τί στενή (ti stenē), "how narrow!" — an exclamation rather than a reason. "Finds" is the same verb promised in v. 8. The seeking that was guaranteed a finding four verses ago is now something few manage. The two sayings are not in contradiction; they are the two ways arriving early.

Image two: two prophets, two trees · 7:15–23

15Keep yourselves clear of the sham-prophets, who come at y'all dressed in sheep's wool but underneath are wolves — wolves out to prey.9

Greek ψευδοπροφητῶν… λύκοι ἅρπαγες (pseudoprophētōn… lykoi harpages) NASB "false prophets… ravenous wolves" Wooden ψευδοπροφήτης is one compound word, the LXX's fixed term for a whole class of person — not a real prophet who lies, but a category, closer to "sham-prophet" or "quack" than "prophet, false one." ἅρπαγες is from ἁρπάζω, "seize, snatch, carry off" — the same verb John uses for what the wolf does to the sheep at 10:12, and elsewhere in the NT the word for a swindler or extortioner (1 Cor 5:10–11; 6:10; Luke 18:11). No doctrine is specified — only an outside and an inside. And the adjective is already an accusation: these are wolves in the sense of predators for gain, which is exactly what vv. 21–23 will show them doing.

16From their fruits y'all will recognize them. They don't gather grapes off thornbushes, or figs off thistles, do they?

17So every good tree makes fine fruit, but the rotten tree makes evil fruit.

18A good tree cannot make evil fruits, and a rotten tree cannot make fine fruits.

Deliberately close to tautology. A tree doesn't try to bear; it discloses, in its fruit, what it already is.

19Every tree not making fine fruit is chopped down and thrown into fire.10

Word for word, this is John the Baptist at 3:10. Matthew doesn't flag the quotation. He simply lets Jesus speak the forerunner's sentence.

20So then — from their fruits y'all will recognize them.

Verses 16 and 20 are the same sentence, bracketing the tree logic between them like a chorus.

21Not everyone saying to me "Master! Master!" will come into the kingdom of the skies — but the one doing the will of my Father, the one in the skies.11

Greek Κύριε κύριε (kyrie kyrie) NASB "Lord, Lord" Wooden "Master! Master!" — the doubled vocative is Semitic intensity, the register of "Eli, Eli" Note The same noun stood at 6:24, where no one can serve two masters. The people crying out here have picked a master; the question is whether they served him.

22Many will say to me on that Day: "Master! Master! Didn't we prophesy by your name — and by your name throw out demons — and by your name make many acts of power?"

Their evidence is the most impressive fruit imaginable, and it is exactly the ministry Jesus will authorize for the twelve at 10:1, 8. "Throw out" is the same verb used of the wood-chip in vv. 4–5 — these are people expert at extraction.

23And then I'll declare to them: "I never knew y'all. Get away from me, y'all workers of lawlessness."12

Greek ἀποχωρεῖτε ἀπ' ἐμοῦ οἱ ἐργαζόμενοι τὴν ἀνομίαν (apochōreite ap' emou hoi ergazomenoi tēn anomian) NASB "practice lawlessness" Wooden those whose trade is the un-Torah-ing of life Text-form Nearly verbatim Psalm 6:9 in the Greek Old Testament — where the words belong to the vindicated sufferer dismissing his enemies. Jesus takes that seat. "I never knew you" turns Amos 3:2 inside out — "you only have I known of all the families of the earth." This is covenant language withdrawn, not a claim about missing information.

Image three: two builders, two houses · 7:24–27

24So everyone — whoever hears these words of mine and does them — will be likened to a shrewd man, who built his house on the rock.13

At v. 21 the object of obedience was the will of the Father. Here it is "these words of mine." Matthew makes no attempt to soften the equation.

25And the rain came down, and the rivers came, and the winds blew and hurled themselves at that house — and it did not fall, for it had been founded on the rock.

26And everyone hearing these words of mine and not doing them will be likened to a stupid man, who built his house on the sand.

Judean wadis floor out beautifully level and are dry eleven months of the year. Building there is not laziness; it is a perfectly reasonable-looking decision that fails once.

27And the rain came down, and the rivers came, and the winds blew and slammed into that house — and it fell. And its fall was enormous.

Greek καὶ ἦν ἡ πτῶσις αὐτῆς μεγάλη (kai ēn hē ptōsis autēs megalē) NASB "great was its fall" Wooden and its collapse was great — the noun, not the verb, gets the last word Note Both storms are described in identical words. The difference was never visible above ground.

The crowds · 7:28–29

28And it happened, when Jesus finished these words, the crowds were knocked clean out of themselves at his teaching.14

ἐξεπλήσσοντο (exeplēssonto) is literally "were struck out of" — the same startled violence English keeps in "thunderstruck."

29For he was teaching them as one having authority — and not like their scribes.15

A scribe's authority was borrowed: you taught by citing a chain of earlier names. Jesus has just spent three chapters saying "but I say to y'all," citing no one. The crowd registers the missing footnotes before anyone works out what their absence means.

Notes

  1. μὴ κρίνετε (mē krinete), v. 1. A present imperative with the negative, which in Greek often means stop an action already underway rather than never begin one. The rabbinic tradition runs alongside rather than against this: Avot 1:6 ("judge every person toward the scale of merit"), Avot 2:5 ("do not judge your fellow until you have stood in his place"), and Shabbat 127b (whoever judges his neighbor favorably is himself judged favorably). Lexicon: κρίνω.
  2. Measure for measure, v. 2. Mishnah Sotah 1:7 states the principle almost word for word as the working logic of divine justice — middah k'neged middah, measure against measure. μέτρον (metron) is a market word before it is a courtroom word: the grain scoop you fill and level off. The same reciprocity governed the debt-clause of the Father's Prayer at 6:12, 14–15, and it will be dramatized at length in the unforgiving slave, 18:23–35.
  3. Chip and rafter, vv. 3–5. A close rabbinic parallel stands at Arakhin 16b (doublet at Bava Batra 15b), where Rabbi Tarfon doubts anyone in his generation can accept rebuke: say "take the splinter from between your eyes" and the answer comes back "take the beam from between yours." Tarfon lived through AD 70, so the direction of borrowing is genuinely open. Note the difference of use: in Tarfon the deflection is defiance; in Matthew it is legitimate. Chrysostom takes v. 1 as aimed not at all judgment but at people loaded with their own faults trampling others over trifles — Homily 23 on Matthew ↗.
  4. The holy and the pearls, v. 6. The hardest verse in the Sermon, with at least five serious readings and no consensus: (i) sacramental — Didache 9:5 already quotes it to restrict the eucharist to the baptized (text ↗); (ii) Torah — rabbinic idiom could call the Law a golden ring and its rulings its pearls, and Matthew Black proposed an Aramaic qedasha ("ring") misread as qudsha ("holy"), which would line the couplet up with the gold ring in a pig's snout at Proverbs 11:22; (iii) mission — a temporary restriction like 10:5–6, overturned at 28:19; (iv) apostasy — dogs and pigs as insiders who turned, the pairing 2 Peter 2:22 uses of false teachers; (v) incompatibility — its position in the diptych answers the two-masters saying of 6:24, making the point that some things cannot be mixed. Reading (v) is the one the structure supports; the saying also circulates free-standing and uninterpreted in Gospel of Thomas 93, which suggests Jesus left it on the table as a riddle.
  5. Ask, seek, knock, v. 7. All three imperatives are present-tense, which can carry ongoing or repeated action — the old "keep on asking" is defensible, though the present is also simply the normal tense for a general command. The door that opens here is shut at 25:11–12, where the same doubled cry "Master, Master, open to us" receives "I do not know you" — almost exactly the verdict of v. 23 below.
  6. Good things, v. 11. Luke's version of this saying ends with the Father giving the Holy Spirit (Luke 11:13); Matthew ends with an unspecified plural, ἀγαθά (agatha). Luke points the promise at Pentecost; Matthew leaves it open to everything asked for since 6:9. Neither is an improvement on the other. Note also that the bread-and-stone pair last appeared in the wilderness at 4:3, with the tempter proposing the transaction in reverse.
  7. The golden rule, v. 12. Widely paralleled: Tobit 4:15, Letter of Aristeas 207, and Hillel's famous reply in Shabbat 31a — what is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow; that is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary, go and learn. The old apologetic claim that the negative form is mere restraint while the positive form requires grace should be resisted: Hillel's "go and learn" opens onto a Torah of relentless positive obligation — leave the gleanings, lend without interest, pay the wage before sundown, love the stranger. What is distinctive in Matthew is not the polarity but the closing clause, and the authority implied in saying it.
  8. Two ways, vv. 13–14. Covenant-ending form: Deuteronomy 30:15,19, Jeremiah 21:8, Psalm 1, Sirach 15:17; developed into cosmic dualism at Qumran in 1QS III–IV, and opening the Didache ("two ways, one of life and one of death, and a great difference between the two"). ἀπώλεια (apōleia) in the Greek Old Testament regularly names historical catastrophe — a city's ruin, a nation's collapse — as often as final judgment, which is why the reading splits. Constable takes it as eternal destruction and few-versus-many as a soteriological ratio; France and Wright hear Jeremiah's fork, a nation on a trajectory toward a dated disaster. Both edges can be live at once.
  9. Sham-prophets, ravenous wolves, v. 15. ψευδοπροφήτης is a single compound — the Septuagint's standing term for נָבִיא שֶׁקֶר (navi sheqer) — naming a class of person rather than qualifying an otherwise-real prophet as dishonest. ἅρπαγες shares its root with ἁρπάζω, "snatch, seize by force": the same verb describes the wolf's action against the sheep at John 10:12, and the adjectival form elsewhere in the NT names swindlers and extortioners in vice-lists (1 Cor 5:10–11; 6:10; Luke 18:11) — predation specifically for gain, which anticipates the profit-shaped false discipleship of vv. 21–23. The prophetic backdrop for the sheep's-clothing figure itself is Deuteronomy 13 and 18, Jeremiah 6, 8, 23, and Ezekiel 13. The harder question is where these figures stand: Constable assumes scribes and Pharisees; Davies & Allison and Luz take seriously that vv. 21–23 describe people prophesying, exorcising, and working miracles in Jesus' name — insiders to Matthew's own community. If that is right, the sharpest warning in the Sermon points inward at the church reading it.
  10. Verbatim John, v. 19. Compare 3:10. See also 12:33–37, where Matthew brings the tree-and-fruit test back and narrows "fruit" specifically to speech.
  11. Doing the Father's will, v. 21. Constable, defending a faith-alone frame, redefines "doing the will of the Father" as believing in Jesus. The move is worth weighing against the text: ποιέω (poieō) has been drumming since v. 12 and runs through vv. 17–19, 21, 22, 24, 26 — the Sermon's ending is built on the verb do, and reading it as a synonym for believe requires importing the equation rather than finding it. Compare 21:28–31, where Matthew stages the same distinction as a parable about two sons and settles it on action.
  12. ἀνομία (anomia), v. 23. Matthew's word and no one else's among the gospel writers — here, 13:41, 23:28, 24:12. In the Greek Old Testament it renders the Hebrew vocabulary of iniquity and covenant treachery, so the charge is not rule-breaking but living as though Torah, as though covenant, did not apply. Chrysostom on "I never knew you": God knows all people by nature but seems not to know these, because he does not love them — Homily 24 ↗. LXX text: NETS ↗.
  13. Build and rock, vv. 24–27. οἰκοδομέω + πέτρα is planted here and detonates at 16:18 — "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build." Isaiah 28:16's foundation stone stands behind both. Two rabbinic parallels make the same deeds-over-learning point: Hanina ben Dosa at Avot 3:9 (the one whose deeds exceed his wisdom — his wisdom endures), and Elisha ben Abuyah in Avot de Rabbi Natan 24, where the man with deeds builds stone first and brick after, so rising water cannot dislodge him.
  14. The discourse formula, v. 28. καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν recurs at 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1 and nowhere else — the clearest hard evidence that Matthew is building on a five-discourse plan rather than reporting as events arrived. Chrysostom on the crowds' reaction: Homily 25 ↗.
  15. ἐξουσία (exousia), v. 29. First occurrence in the gospel, and it runs: 8:9, 9:6, 9:8, 10:1, 21:23–27, and finally 28:18 — "all authority in sky and on earth has been given to me." The word quietly planted at the end of the first discourse is the word that ends the book.

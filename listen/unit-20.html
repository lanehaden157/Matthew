All units

Matthew, unit 20

Briefing

This unit sits at the very end of Jesus' long road from the north to Jerusalem, and it is the last stretch before the city. Since the moment Peter named him the Messiah, Matthew has told one story over and over: Jesus is going to Jerusalem to suffer, and the people closest to him keep not hearing it. Three times now he has said so, and each time something goes wrong right after. After the first, Peter rebukes him. After the second, the disciples argue about who is greatest. After the third, which falls in this unit, two of them, with their mother, ask for the best seats in the kingdom. The unit closes with two blind men outside Jericho, the last stop before the climb up to Jerusalem, who see more clearly than the Twelve do.

The first scene is a parable that belongs to Matthew alone. It answers a question Peter asked a few verses earlier, when he pointed out that the disciples had left everything and wanted to know what they would get. Jesus' reply surrounds the parable. Before it he says that many who are first will be last and the last first, and after it he says almost the same words again, with the order turned around. In between, a landowner hires workers all day long and pays everyone the same. The story is built to feel unfair, and readers have argued for two thousand years about whether it is about reversal, as the frame says, or about equality, as the story itself shows. Listen for both.

The setting would have been familiar. Day labourers waited in the town square, were paid at evening, and had nothing to fall back on if no one hired them. The vineyard also carries a lot of weight in Israel's Scriptures. The prophets use it as a picture of Israel itself, most famously in the book of Isaiah, where God plants a vineyard and looks for grapes and finds only a cry. Matthew will use the vineyard again in the next chapter, in two more parables aimed at Jerusalem's leaders.

The second and third scenes hang together. Jesus gives his most detailed prediction yet: the chief priests and scribes will condemn him, hand him over to the Gentiles, and the Gentiles will mock, flog and crucify him, and on the third day he will be raised. Then comes the mother of James and John, bowing, and asking. Three words to watch. The first is the cup, a familiar image in Israel's Scriptures for what God hands to someone to drink, sometimes blessing and more often judgment. The second is the pair of terms for service, one meaning a servant who waits on others and a stronger one meaning a slave who belongs to someone. The third is ransom, a word for a price paid to free a person. Jesus uses it of himself in the last line of the scene, and scholars have long debated how much of the book of Isaiah's suffering servant lies behind it.

The last scene is two blind men who will not be quiet. Mark and Luke tell the story with one man, and Mark gives him a name. Matthew has two, and he has told a very similar story before, much earlier in the Gospel. Why he does this is an open question, and the commentators we read, including Constable, France, and Davies and Allison, each admit they have no certain answer. Also watch the question Jesus asks. He asks the mother of James and John, and then the two blind men, the same thing: what do you want?

A word about voices. Constable reads the passage through a coming earthly kingdom and keeps the parable about grace in rewards. France argues the point is equal reward, which is simply how God rules. Davies and Allison read it as a warning to the faithful as much as an encouragement. And Chrysostom, preaching in the late fourth century, warns his hearers not to press every detail of a parable and reads this one as a comfort to people who come to faith late and a caution to people who are proud of an early start.

Hold three tensions as you listen. Does the parable show the first becoming last, or only the last being made equal? Does the cup Jesus offers mean suffering alone, or also something he alone will bear? And are the two blind men a picture of discipleship, or just two people who needed help and got it? The text will not settle all of these for us.

Verse by verse

The master of the house and his workers

The parable opens with a "for". The kingdom of the skies is like a man who is a master of the house, an oikodespotēs, which means the head of a household. That little word "for" ties the story to the saying just before it, that many who are first will be last and the last first. So the parable is meant to explain that saying, and to answer Peter's question about what the disciples will get. Matthew has used this figure before. In the book of Matthew Jesus himself is called a master of the house, and later parables use the same word for the figure who stands in for God. Here the kingdom is not compared to a thing but to a person, and what the person does.

He goes out at first light to hire workers, ergatai, into his vineyard, an ampelōn. The vineyard is one of the Bible's favourite pictures of Israel. In the book of Isaiah, God sings a song about his beloved's vineyard on a fertile hill. He fences it, clears it, plants the best vine, builds a tower and a winepress, and looks for grapes, and gets thorns. The Greek translation of Isaiah uses this same word, ampelōn, in that song. The prophet then says outright that the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel. Jeremiah, in the Greek, has God complain that many shepherds have destroyed my vineyard. So when Jesus' listeners heard "vineyard", they heard Israel, and they heard a story about God and his people. Notice also what Matthew does with this in the next chapter: two more vineyard parables come directly after, both aimed at the leaders in Jerusalem.

The wage matters. He agrees with these first workers for a denarius for the day, and the verb here, symphōneō, means to agree or to be of one voice. A denarius was an ordinary day's pay for a labourer. Davies and Allison say it was neither generous nor stingy. That agreement will come back, because the same verb appears again in the argument at the end. Chrysostom has one more thing to say. In his sermon on this passage he says it is not right to inquire curiously into every word of a parable. He tells his hearers to find what the parable is for and take that.

At the third hour, about nine in the morning, the master goes out and sees others standing idle in the marketplace, the agora. The word for idle, argos, literally means "not working", and France points out that the traditional "idle" sounds lazy. These are people no one has hired. They are not told a price. They are told to go in, and that he will give them "what is right", or fair. This is the only place in Matthew where that word stands alone as a neuter, "whatever is right" (the one other neuter use, in 23:35, describes blood), and Davies and Allison say that the reader is drawn in by wondering what the fair wage will be. Later in the story the answer will be a surprise.

He goes out again at the sixth and the ninth hour, noon and three, and does the same. Then at the eleventh hour, about five, an hour before sundown, he finds still others standing there and asks why they have stood all day with nothing to do. They say no one has hired them. He tells them to go into the vineyard too. Davies and Allison say the eleventh hour interrupts the pattern of every three hours and draws attention to itself. France adds a point about the economy. A day labourer had no security, not even the small security of a slave who belongs to a household. If no one hired you, your family did not eat. So a man who keeps hiring workers he does not obviously need is better read as a man looking after the unemployed than as a bad planner. France says that commercially he is a fool, and so is God.

Now evening comes. The "lord of the vineyard", a kyrios, is the word the Gospel uses for Jesus and for God. He tells his steward, an epitropos, to call the workers and pay them their wage, beginning from the last and going on to the first. The Law told employers to pay a hired worker before the sun went down. Leviticus says the wages of a hired worker must not stay with you until morning, and Deuteronomy says to pay the poor man the same day, before sunset, because he depends on it. So the evening payment is good manners and good law. But the order is a trap. By paying the last ones first, the master makes sure that the first can see what happens. Constable wonders whether it shows the master's pleasure in the last. France says the order already tells us that things will not go the conventional way.

The eleventh-hour workers each receive a denarius. The first group, watching, assume they will get more. They receive a denarius each too. And then comes the verb: they "murmured", egongyzon, against the master of the house. That is wilderness vocabulary. In Exodus, when Israel complains about food, the Greek uses the noun gongysmos, murmuring, and a related verb, and Moses says that the complaining is not against us but against God. Davies and Allison note that this vocabulary has led many to identify the first workers with Israel, but they warn against that, and so do France and Constable. The vocabulary shows the shape of the sin, a complaint against the one who provides. It does not decide who the workers are.

Their complaint has two parts. These last ones worked one hour, and you have made them equal to us who carried the weight of the day and the scorching heat. The word for scorching heat, kausōn, also means a burning wind. It is a rare word. It shows up in the book of Jonah, in one of the strangest scenes in the Bible. Jonah is furious that God has had mercy on Nineveh, God sends a burning east wind and the sun beats on Jonah's head, and Jonah says it is better to die than live. God asks him whether he has the right to be angry, and ends by asking whether God should not spare the great city. It is the same setup as the parable: someone who has borne the heat complains about God's generosity to people who did not earn it. No commentator we read draws this connection, so take it as a possible echo, not a firm one. But the shared rare word and the shared situation are real.

The master answers one of them. He calls him "friend", hetairos, a word that in Matthew is used to someone in a tense moment, and it is not exactly warm. He says, I am not wronging you, did you not agree with me for a denarius? Then a command, take what is yours and go. The word "go", hypagō, was used earlier in the story as an invitation, go into the vineyard, and now it is a dismissal, go out. Davies and Allison put it well: the scene becomes a court in which the accuser becomes the accused.

The master then says he wants to give to this last man as he gave to you. He asks, is it not permitted for me to do what I want with what is mine? The word is exestin, the same word used in the Sabbath quarrels earlier in the Gospel, where the question was what is permitted. Now the question is turned on God's own rights. And then the last line, the one that cuts. Is your eye evil because I am good? An "evil eye" in Jewish speech meant a stingy or envious eye, one that looks at someone else's good fortune and hates it. Earlier in the Sermon on the Mount Jesus said that the eye is the lamp of the body, and a good eye fills you with light. Constable also points to the Old Testament phrase for an evil eye toward a poor brother in Deuteronomy and the jealousy in the story of Saul and David. And "I am good" reaches back to the rich man, who was told that only one is good. The master of the house, in the Gospel's logic, is that one.

Then Jesus sums up. So the last will be first and the first last. Notice the order has been reversed from the earlier saying, to fit the story. And notice a problem that readers have noticed forever. The story does not show the first becoming last. Everyone gets the same. Chrysostom says this plainly: the beginning of the parable does not harmonize with its end. He notes that Jesus says the opposite both before and after the parable, and reads the saying as a warning that those who do not keep their place at the front become last. The parable itself, he says, is aimed at the proud and at late converts. Davies and Allison say the last become first through no merit of their own and the first become last by opposing grace, and that the teaching works as much as a warning as an encouragement. France and Constable think the sense is equality, that nobody gets a higher reward by being first. Many early Christian readers, such as Gregory the Great, read the five hirings as ages of the world, from Adam to Christ. Davies and Allison call that missing the point. They also report a later rabbinic parable about a king who hired workers and paid them all the same because one had done more in two hours than the rest in a whole day, and they note that in the rabbinic version pay is still according to merit. Jesus' story has no such merit. Whoever Jesus' original audience was, the sources suggest the story is set against a mechanical idea of reward. Davies and Allison also point to a story in the book of First Samuel. After a battle, David's soldiers want to keep the plunder from those who stayed behind with the baggage. David says no, because the one who goes down to the battle and the one who stays with the baggage will share alike. It became a law in Israel. The vocabulary is not the same, so we cannot say Jesus is thinking of it, but it shows that an equal share by gift, not by work, was already a part of Israel's story.

One more voice is worth keeping. Davies and Allison, and many before them, notice that this parable runs closely alongside the story of the older brother in Luke's Gospel, where a resentful elder son complains about the father's generosity to the prodigal. In both, a resentful early arrival is told that the generosity is a matter of goodness, and the point is aimed at the person tempted to be the grumbler. Luke makes the grumbler family. Here he is a hired hand dismissed with a word.

The third time

Now the story turns. Jesus is going up to Jerusalem. The words matter. He is going up, anabainō, as pilgrims did, and he takes the Twelve aside privately, on the road. Matthew has cut Mark's picture of Jesus striding ahead while the followers are afraid. What he keeps is the privacy and the destination. And then Jesus uses a plural. We are going up to Jerusalem. The disciples are not spectators. They are going where he is going. That draws on earlier calls to take up the cross and follow.

Then the details. The Son of Man will be handed over, paradidōmi, a word that Matthew has used for John the Baptist's fate, for the disciples' fate, and that will describe Judas's betrayal and Pilate's handing over, to the chief priests and scribes. The chief priests and scribes appeared together once before, in the story of the wise men, when Herod asked where the king was to be born. Davies and Allison say the end will be like the beginning: the same leaders gather around another king, and this time they do not point to Bethlehem. They will condemn him to death. Then they will hand him to the Gentiles, the ethnē, the nations. France stresses that this is the new part: the Jewish Messiah, shamed by Gentile mockery, and also the historical point that the Sanhedrin could not carry out an execution under Rome. Then three verbs in a row: to mock, to scourge, to crucify. The word crucify, stauroō, appears here for the first time in a prediction. Matthew has spoken of the cross before, when he said that anyone who would follow must take up his cross, so the careful reader is not surprised. Davies and Allison note that the order matches the passion story in the end of the Gospel, except that there the whipping comes before the mocking. Matthew leaves out Mark's and Luke's detail of spitting, so it is a run of three.

Two small changes from Mark are worth knowing. Mark has the Gentiles kill him, and Matthew has them crucify him, which is more exact. And Mark says after three days, where Matthew says on the third day, which is more exact again. Davies and Allison think Matthew is bringing the prediction closer to what happened. Luke's version adds that they understood none of it, and that it was hidden from them. Matthew has no such line, but he lets the next scene show it.

Chrysostom is helpful here. He says Jesus tells them in stages, more each time, so that when the events came they would not be shaken. He also says they did not really understand, and that this is why their grief and confusion look the way they do. The last words, raised on the third day, come last in the sentence, and France calls this almost matter-of-fact ending incongruous, set after such brutal events. That is what the disciples hear, or do not hear.

The mother, the sons, and the Ten

Then. That small word is the whole scene. Right after the prediction, the mother of the sons of Zebedee comes to him with her sons, bowing low, proskyneō, and asking something of him. Davies and Allison say the word "then" connects the two scenes closely. After the first prediction Peter rebuked him. After the second the disciples quarrelled over who was greatest. Now the third prediction is followed by an expression of personal hope.

Why the mother? In Mark the brothers ask directly. Davies and Allison list explanations. Some have said Matthew protects the brothers, as if a mother's love excuses them. But Matthew is not shy about showing apostles badly, and he does nothing to soften the anger of the Ten at the brothers, not at her. France thinks it is simply how the request was made, and says that we can only guess whether she took the initiative or was sent. Chrysostom says the sons were ashamed and put their mother forward. Davies and Allison offer two other ideas worth holding. One is the story of Bathsheba in the first book of Kings, who goes in to the old king David, bows, and the king asks what she wants. She asks for the throne for her son Solomon, and the Greek uses the same verb for her bowing and the same verb for sitting on the throne. In that story the request is granted. Here it is not. The other is the Canaanite woman earlier in Matthew, another mother kneeling for a child. There the mother asks mercy for her daughter and is heard. Davies and Allison ask whether setting the two side by side is meant to teach which sort of petition is proper.

Jesus asks, what do you want? The same question, in almost the same words, will come back at the end of the unit. She says: command that these my two sons may sit, one on your right and one on your left, in your kingdom. Matthew has changed Mark here. In Mark they ask for glory. In Matthew it is explicitly a kingdom, and the verb "command" treats Jesus as a king who gives orders. It comes straight out of what Jesus said earlier about twelve thrones, and France adds that it may be a bid against Peter. If James and John are at Jesus' right and left, where does that leave Peter?

Here is the sharpest irony in the unit. The phrase one on the right and one on the left appears again in the Gospel in the same words, in the Greek, at the crucifixion, where two bandits are crucified with Jesus, one on the right and one on the left. The word for left, euōnymos, literally means "well-named", a polite way to avoid the unlucky word. So the brothers ask for something they will see given to others, in a place they did not picture. France and Davies and Allison both note it, and Matthew has made the wording identical, which Mark does not quite do.

Jesus answers them, plural, you do not know what you are asking. Can you drink the cup that I am about to drink? The cup is one of the Bible's strongest images. In the Psalms the cup can be a cup of blessing. But far more often it is a cup that God holds, full of mixed wine, from which all the wicked of the earth must drink. In the book of Isaiah, Jerusalem has drunk from the Lord's hand the cup of his fury, and drained it. The cup is also used in the prophets for a judgment that falls first on God's own people. Constable reads it as divine judgment to be borne, and also suffering. France says that for these two sons it must mean suffering, not the punishment of the wicked, though later readers of Matthew, remembering Gethsemane, might hear more. Davies and Allison say that the cup that Jesus will drink is the cup of eschatological sorrow, poured out first on God's people. At Gethsemane, the sons of Zebedee will be asleep when Jesus wrestles with this cup. Matthew has dropped Mark's second image, baptism, leaving only the cup. Some think he wanted to avoid a sacramental reading.

They say, we can. It sounds quick. Jesus takes them at their word and says: you will drink my cup. James will be the first of the apostles killed, in the book of Acts. John's fate is less clear. A later tradition says he was exiled to Patmos, and Davies and Allison are inclined to accept that he lived to old age, while Jerome, drawing on church history, reports a story of boiling oil that Davies and Allison do not rely on. Whatever the end, Jesus does not mock them. Origen noticed that he does not say you are able, he says you will drink.

But to sit at his right and left, he says, is not mine to give, but it is for those for whom it has been prepared by my Father. Matthew adds those last words, of my Father. Chrysostom reads this as condescension to their understanding: the highest throne is not a place anyone but the Son holds, so the words are spoken at their level. Others, like France and Davies and Allison, treat it as a real limit on the Son's authority, with the same sort of limit as the day and hour no one knows but the Father. The word prepared reappears later in the Gospel for those who inherit the kingdom prepared for them. France says the effect is to rule the request out, and the slogan about the first and last warns that the choice will not follow human expectations.

The ten hear it and are indignant about the two brothers. France argues that it is not righteous anger. They are angry because James and John got in first. Davies and Allison say it is as if the whole teaching on greatness earlier had no effect. So Jesus calls them all to him, which matters, since the two had gone aside. He says: you know that the rulers of the nations lord it over them and the great ones exercise authority over them. Both of the verbs begin with the Greek word for "down", kata, and France says they "bear down" on people below. The first of those verbs, katakyrieuō, is a rare word. In the Greek of the first chapter of Genesis, it is what God tells humans to do over the living creatures, to rule them. The same verb appears in the Greek Psalms for the wicked who lord it over the poor. In the Greek of Genesis the word is about the order of creation. In Jesus' mouth it describes what pagan rulers do to people. Neither France nor Davies and Allison draw this out, so it is only a possible echo, but it is a real shared word. Later in the New Testament, the first letter of Peter tells elders not to lord it over those entrusted to them, using the same verb.

Then the turn. It will not be so among you. Whoever wants to become great among you will be your servant, diakonos, a servant who waits on others, the one who serves a meal. And whoever wants to be first will be your slave, doulos, someone who belongs to another. The same word for wanting, thelō, runs through the unit. The mother asked, what do you want, and James and John want thrones. The master of the vineyard says, I want to give to this last man as to you. Now Jesus says whoever wants to be great must serve. France puts the point: if there is ambition in the kingdom, it must be ambition to serve. Davies and Allison set this beside the later speech against the scribes and Pharisees, which has the same shape: two bad models, then "not so among you", then the rule.

And finally the Son of Man himself. Just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom, lytron, for many. The title Son of Man would have pointed his listeners to a vision in the book of Daniel, in which one like a son of man comes to the Ancient of Days and is given dominion, glory and a kingdom, so that all peoples, nations and languages should serve him. The Greek translations of Daniel, in two forms, use verbs for worship and service. So Davies and Allison say Jesus is turning Scripture upside down. The one to whom all nations are to give service has come to give it. They also say that Daniel is not rejected, but joined with a second text.

That second text is the suffering servant in the book of Isaiah. Let me be careful about how close it is. The Greek of Isaiah does not use the word lytron in that chapter, so there is no word-for-word quotation. But the Greek of Isaiah does say that the servant "was delivered to death", that he serves many well, and that he bore the sins of many. The words for life or soul, for handing over, and for many are all there. France says most readers find the idea more than the wording, and that it would be hard to write a shorter summary of that chapter than to give his life as a ransom in place of many. Davies and Allison add that if the saying was first spoken in Hebrew or Aramaic, the case for Isaiah is stronger, and they also mention another possible text in Isaiah about God giving nations as a ransom for Israel, which they call attractive but not demonstrable. Constable reads the many as either the elect or everyone, and says the passage does not speak to that. France says that limited atonement is far from the intention here.

The word lytron itself had a plain meaning. It was a price paid to buy someone free, a slave or a captive. In Exodus, if someone is liable to death, he may pay a ransom for his life, and in the Greek that sentence uses the same three words as Matthew's, give, ransom, and soul or life. So the commentators who say this is a payment are working with a real word, and the word fits an Old Testament legal sense even if it is not Isaiah's. The preposition anti means in place of. France thinks the main idea is the freedom that comes, and Constable and Ellicott stress the price. It is also the first time in Matthew that Jesus gives a reason for his death. France says it stands alone until the last supper, where the phrase for many comes back.

Two sets of eyes

They leave Jericho, and a great crowd follows him. Matthew uses the word follow, akoloutheō, as he has for the first disciples, and it is the word that will frame this scene. It appears at the start, for the crowd, and at the end, for the two men. France reminds us that Jericho was the last town before the long climb to Jerusalem, and that the crowd is largely Galilean pilgrims who will soon cry out at the city gates.

Then, behold, two blind men sitting by the road. Davies and Allison note that they sit while everyone else is on pilgrimage, so they are outsiders. They hear that Jesus is passing by and cry out: Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David. That cry is a repeat. Earlier in Matthew two blind men in Galilee had cried out in nearly the same words, and a Canaanite mother had cried, have mercy on me, Lord, Son of David. The word for mercy, eleaō, belongs to the thread that Matthew keeps tying to a line from Hosea about loyal love. Jesus is called Son of David by the blind and the outcast again and again. Meanwhile the leaders in Jerusalem will not see it. France says the contrast is worth reflecting on.

The crowd rebukes them to make them be quiet. The verb, epitimaō, is the same one the disciples used earlier when they rebuked the children brought to Jesus, and Jesus overruled them there too. So the crowd takes the disciples' part from the earlier scene. The blind men cry out all the more, and Davies and Allison draw out the irony. A few verses later, in the next chapter, the crowd will itself cry out, Son of David, at Jesus' entry into the city. The crowd does exactly what it tried to silence.

Jesus stands still. He calls them. He asks, what do you want me to do for you? This is the question he asked the mother. Davies and Allison quote the point that two bystanders, privileged disciples with a request about future honour, are followed by two outsiders with a request about sight. The brothers asked through someone else, with no title, about the future, for themselves. The blind men ask directly, with titles, about now, for something needed. And they say what they want. Lord, that our eyes may be opened. The verb is anoigō, to open. It is the same verb the Greek of Isaiah uses when it promises that the eyes of the blind shall be opened. Mark's version may hint at that promise too, but Matthew keeps it quiet and only uses the plain words.

Then Jesus is wrenched in the gut, splanchnizomai. That is a bodily word for compassion that Matthew uses of Jesus again and again, for the crowds like sheep without a shepherd, for the hungry. And he touches their eyes. Mark does not say that. Matthew borrows the touch from the earlier healing. At once they see again and follow him. France says that this may make them model disciples, or may only mean that they joined the pilgrim crowd. Constable reads them as believers who became disciples. Davies and Allison say a symbolic reading is not impossible but declare themselves undecided. It is Matthew's last healing story, and it follows a chapter in which disciples with sight misunderstood. In Mark, Jesus says "your faith has saved you." Matthew leaves that out. He tells us nothing about their faith except what they say and do.

There are some open questions to carry. Why two blind men, when Mark has one? Constable suggests two witnesses, as the Law required. France says that is weakened by the fact that the title was already used, and that he has no better explanation. Davies and Allison float several ideas, including a contrast with the two sons of Zebedee, or just an echo of the earlier healing. They also note that the two stories form a pair, one near the start of Jesus' public work and one near the end, and between them Israel's leaders have turned against him, but his mercy has not changed. The older Christian readers, like Origen and Augustine, made these two blind men into Jews and Gentiles, or into the world, with Jericho as the world. Chrysostom took a simpler line: these men saw better than many who had sight, and the crowd's rebuke only made their persistence show. And one early reader argued that "Son of David" showed only a human view of Jesus, and that when they simply said "Lord" they were healed. That is an old comment, and it is worth hearing as a record of how readers used the title. Matthew's own Gospel does not treat "Son of David" as a lesser name. He has just let it be shouted twice and heard.

When the unit ends, Jesus has just given sight to two beggars and is walking toward the city. The disciples had been unable to hear what was coming. The blind men have seen enough to follow.

Unit 5 · Matthew 5:1–48 · the Sermon on the Mount (I): the Beatitudes, salt and light, the Law, and the six contrasts

The SettingMatthew 5:1–2 · the second mountain — Jesus ascends and sits to teach

Going up the mountain · 5:1–2

1And seeing the crowds he went up onto the mountain;1 and when he had sat down, his disciples came to him.

2And opening his mouth he was teaching them, saying:

“Opening his mouth” (anoixas to stoma) is a Septuagintal solemnity-marker — the gesture before a weighty, oracular utterance (cf. Job 3:1; Ps 78:2, quoted at Matt 13:35). The narrator is telling us to lean in.
The BeatitudesMatthew 5:3–12 · the makarisms — eight, then a turn to “y'all”

The Beatitudes · 5:3–12

3Blessed2 are the poor in the spirit,3 for theirs is the kingdom of the skies.

4Blessed are the mourning, for they shall be comforted.

Verses 3–4 paraphrase Isaiah 61:1–2 (LXX): the Servant is anointed to bring good news to the poor and “to comfort all who mourn” (parakalesai pantas tous penthountas). The kingdom-people receive the very consolation the Anointed One came to bring.

5Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.

A near-verbatim quotation of Psalm 37:11 (LXX 36:11): hoi de praeis klēronomēsousin gēn. The same word praus (meek, gentle) is twice applied to Jesus himself (11:29; 21:5). A quiet anti-zealot note: the land comes to the gentle, not the violent.

6Blessed are those hungering and thirsting for righteousness,4 for they shall be fed full.

“Fed full” renders chortasthēsontai — a hearty, almost barnyard word (“foddered up”) that recurs at the feedings, where the crowds “were fed full” (14:20; 15:37). The satisfaction promised here gets a narrative preview in the wilderness.

7Blessed are the merciful, for they shall be shown mercy.

8Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

Psalm 24:3–4 asks who may ascend the LORD’s mountain: “he who has clean hands and a pure heart” (katharos tē kardia). To “see God” reverses Exodus 33:20 (“no one may see me and live”); John answers it — the unseen God is seen in the Son (John 1:18; 14:9) — and Revelation 22:4 consummates it.

9Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God.

eirēnopoioi — not the merely peaceful but those who actively make peace (Heb. shalom, the wholeness of a world set right). Sonship by family likeness; the logic returns at v45.

10Blessed are those who have been persecuted for the sake of righteousness, for theirs is the kingdom of the skies.

The bracket snaps shut: “righteousness” and “theirs is the kingdom” return together, sealing the eight (cf. 1 Pet 3:14).

11Blessed are y'all whenever they revile y'all and persecute and say every evil thing against y'all,5 lying, for my sake.

The grammar lurches from third person to second, and the ground shifts from “for righteousness’ sake” (v10) to heneken emou, “for my sake” — allegiance to Jesus quietly takes the place of righteousness itself.

12Rejoice and exult, for y'all's reward is much in the skies; for so they persecuted the prophets who were before y'all.

“Exult” (agalliasthe) is the cultic, near-ecstatic joy of the Psalms. The disciples are slotted into the line of persecuted prophets — a master-theme that detonates in ch. 23 and in Acts 7:52.

Luke 6:17–26 — on level ground, four blessings and four woes

Luke 6:17And coming down with them he stood on a level place, and a large crowd of his disciples, and a great multitude of the people from all Judea and Jerusalem and the coastland of Tyre and Sidon.
Luke 6:20–21Fortunate the destitute, because yours is the kingdom of God. Fortunate those hungering now, because y'all will be fed full. Fortunate those weeping now, because y'all will laugh.
Luke 6:24–25Except — woe to y'all the rich, because y'all are getting y'all's comfort in full. Woe to y'all who have been filled now, because y'all will hunger. Woe, those laughing now, because y'all will mourn and weep.

Luke has four blessings in the second person, matched one for one by four woes, spoken standing on flat ground to a crowd drawn from Judea and the pagan coast; Matthew has nine in the third person, no woes at all, and a mountain. Where Luke says "the destitute" and "those hungering," Matthew says "the destitute in spirit" and "those hungering and thirsting for right-doing" (dikaiosynē). Those qualifications are usually called spiritualizing, but characterizing is nearer: Matthew is describing a community rather than an economic class, and dikaiosynē — the word he adds here and again at 5:10 — is the term he will organize the rest of the Sermon around (5:20; 6:1; 6:33).

Salt and LightMatthew 5:13–16 · description becomes vocation

Salt and light · 5:13–16

13Y'all are the salt of the earth; but if the salt is made foolish,6 with what will it be salted? It is strong for nothing any longer except, being thrown out, to be trampled underfoot by men.

“Made foolish” renders mōranthē — literally “played the fool,” the verb behind the “fool” of v22. Salt was the covenant-bond (Lev 2:13; Num 18:19). The unthinkable thing — that the unfailing thing should fail — is the paradox.

14Y'all are the light of the world.7 A city set upon a mountain cannot be hidden;

The light that dawned on the people sitting in darkness (4:16, quoting Isa 9) is now declared to be them — gift becomes vocation. The mountain-city evokes Zion, to which the nations stream (Isa 2; Mic 4), folding the inclusion-of-the-nations theme into the Sermon’s opening.

15nor do they light a lamp and put it under the measuring-bowl, but upon the lampstand, and it shines for all those in the house.

The modios is a Latin loanword — a two-gallon grain-measure; inverted over a lamp it would simply snuff it. The lampstand is the word for the temple menorah in the Greek OT.

16So let y'all's light shine before men, that they may see y'all's good works and glorify y'all's Father who is in the skies.

The Father appears for the first time in the Sermon — and will saturate ch. 6. Note the tension held in suspension: here good works are to be seen; at 6:1 righteousness is not to be done “to be seen.” The difference is the direction the glory flows.
The Law and the ProphetsMatthew 5:17–20 · the thesis — fulfillment, not abolition; the inclusio opens

Not to abolish but to fulfil · 5:17–20

17Do not think that I came to tear down8 the Law or the Prophets; I came not to tear down but to fulfill.

Greek ouk ēlthon katalysai alla plērōsai This translation “not to tear down but to fulfill” NASB “I did not come to abolish but to fulfill” Note katalysai is a demolition word — used of razing the temple (24:2; 26:61). Against it stands the verb of Matthew’s formula quotations. This opens the inclusio closed only at 7:12 (“this is the Law and the Prophets”).

18For amen9 I say to y'all, until sky and earth pass away, not one iota or one little-stroke shall pass from the Law, until all things come to pass.

The iota stands for the Hebrew yod, smallest letter; the keraia (“little horn”) is the serif distinguishing near-identical letters. The Torah binds down to its typography — held in deliberate tension with the freedom Jesus is about to take.

19Whoever therefore relaxes10 one of the least of these commandments and so teaches men shall be called least in the kingdom of the skies; but whoever does and teaches them, this one shall be called great in the kingdom of the skies.

“Relaxes” (lysē) is the simple form of v17’s “tear down” (katalysai) — to relax a commandment is to begin demolishing the Law. A measure-for-measure pun: relax a least command, be called least.

20For I say to y'all that unless y'all's righteousness abounds beyond11 that of the scribes and Pharisees, y'all shall by no means enter into the kingdom of the skies.

The thesis sentence the rest of the chapter unfolds. Not a different righteousness but a more abundant one — perisseusē, “overflow.” To out-righteous the most scrupulous keepers of Torah in the land is an impossibly high bar — the door into the six case studies.
The Six ContrastsMatthew 5:21–48 · “y'all heard… but I say” — the Law driven to the heart

Anger and reconciliation · 5:21–26

21Y'all heard that it was said to the ancients: “You shall not murder; and whoever murders shall be liable to the judgment.”

22But I say to y'all that everyone who is angry with his brother shall be liable to the judgment; and whoever says to his brother, “Raca,”12 shall be liable to the council; and whoever says, “Fool,” shall be liable to the Gehenna of fire.

A built triad of escalating tribunals — local court → Sanhedrin → Gehenna. Rhaka (Aramaic, “empty-head”) and Mōre (“fool,” the same root as the “foolish” salt of v13) are contempt-words; the point is that the contempt behind murder is itself murderous.

23If therefore you are offering your gift upon the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you,

24leave there your gift before the altar, and go — first be reconciled to your brother, and then come, offer your gift.

Reconciliation outranks sacrifice — the prophetic “I desire mercy and not sacrifice” (Hos 6:6), which Matthew twice puts on Jesus’ lips (9:13; 12:7).

25Be well-disposed to your adversary quickly, while you are with him on the way, lest the adversary hand you over to the judge, and the judge to the officer, and you be thrown into prison.

26Amen I say to you, you shall by no means come out from there until you pay back the last quadrans.13

The kodrantēs is the Latin quadrans, the smallest Roman coin. Deal with the breach now, before judgment closes the account.

Adultery and the eye · 5:27–30

27Y'all heard that it was said: “You shall not commit adultery.”

28But I say to y'all that everyone who looks at a woman in order to covet her14 has already committed adultery with her in his heart.

“Covet” renders epithymēsai — the exact verb of the tenth commandment (Exod 20:17 LXX, “you shall not covet… your neighbor’s wife”). Jesus collapses the seventh commandment into the tenth: the act was always also about the covetous look that fathers it.

29And if your right eye trips you up,15 tear it out and throw it from you; for it is better for you that one of your members perish than that your whole body be thrown into Gehenna.

30And if your right hand trips you up, cut it off and throw it from you; for it is better for you that one of your members perish than that your whole body go away into Gehenna.

Semitic hyperbole, not surgery: nothing — not the most valued faculty (“right” eye, “right” hand) — is worth keeping if it drags the whole self to ruin. Repeated at 18:8–9, there about causing the “little ones” to stumble.

Divorce · 5:31–32

31And it was said: “Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.”

32But I say to y'all that everyone who divorces his wife — except for a matter of sexual immorality16 — makes her commit adultery, and whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery.

Mark 10:11–12 · Luke 16:18 — nobody else has the exception

Mark 10:11–12Whoever releases his wife and marries another commits adultery against her; and if she, having released her husband, marries another, she commits adultery.
Luke 16:18Everyone releasing his wife and marrying another commits adultery, and the one marrying a woman released from a husband commits adultery.

Matthew alone carries parektos logou porneias, "apart from a matter of sexual immorality," here and again in the fuller debate at 19:9; Mark and Luke state the prohibition flat. Mark alone imagines a wife initiating the divorce, which was a Roman legal possibility and not a Judean one — so on Mark priority we can watch one saying being fitted to two different courtrooms, Mark's to Gentile readers who needed the reciprocal case, Matthew's to readers for whom the live question was what counted as legitimate grounds under Deuteronomy 24:1. The exception clause is therefore not a softening of Jesus but Matthew placing him inside a rabbinic argument he is expected to have an opinion in.

Greek parektos logou porneias — “apart from a word/matter of porneia” NASB “except for the reason of sexual immorality” Synoptic Mark 10:11 & Luke 16:18 have no exception — the absolute saying. Matthew alone adds the clause. Note porneia is disputed: adultery? immorality broadly? marriage within forbidden kinship (its sense in Acts 15; Lev 18 LXX)? The exception lands Jesus roughly on the Shammai side of the Hillel–Shammai debate (m. Gittin 9:10) — or rejects its premise altogether (cf. 19:4–8).

Oaths · 5:33–37

33Again, y'all heard that it was said to the ancients: “You shall not swear falsely, but shall render to the Lord your oaths.”

34But I say to y'all not to swear at all: neither by sky, for it is God's throne;

35nor by the earth, for it is the footstool of his feet; nor toward Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King;

Sky-throne and earth-footstool quote Isaiah 66:1; “city of the great King” is Psalm 48:2. Every oath-substitute people used to keep technical deniability is shown to touch God after all — there is nowhere to swear that escapes his presence.

36nor by your head shall you swear, for you cannot make one hair white or black.

37But let y'all's word be “yes, yes,” “no, no”; and what is beyond these is from the evil one.17

Integrity that needs no oath to prop it up. James 5:12 carries this forward almost verbatim as a community standard. The literal reading made oath-refusers of the Anabaptists and Quakers; Augustine and most of the tradition read it as forbidding evasive, not solemn, oaths.

An eye for an eye · 5:38–42

38Y'all heard that it was said: “An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.”

The lex talionis (Exod 21:24; Lev 24:20; Deut 19:21) was a limit on vengeance, court-administered and by the first century largely commuted to monetary compensation — not a license for it.

39But I say to y'all not to resist the evildoer;18 but whoever strikes you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also;

A right-handed blow to the right cheek is a backhand — primarily an insult, the blow given an inferior. Turning the other refuses both retaliation and humiliation: standing as an equal who will not be shamed into the cycle.

40and to the one wanting to sue you and take your tunic, let him have also your cloak;

The outer cloak (himation) was the one garment the Torah required a creditor to return by nightfall, since the poor sleep in it (Exod 22:26–27). To hand it over is to stand stripped bare and expose the cruelty of the claim.

41and whoever will compel you to go one mile,19 go with him two.

“Compel” (angareusei) is the Roman right of military requisition — the same verb used when Simon of Cyrene is “compelled” to carry the cross (27:32). The second mile turns the instrument of subjugation into a free act.

42To the one asking you, give; and the one wanting to borrow from you, do not turn away.

Love your enemies · 5:43–48

43Y'all heard that it was said: “You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.”20

“Love your neighbor” is Leviticus 19:18 — but “hate your enemy” is found nowhere in the Hebrew Scriptures. The nearest thing is sectarian: the Qumran Community Rule’s charge to “love the sons of light… and hate the sons of darkness” (1QS 1:9–10).

44But I say to y'all, love y'all's enemies and pray for those persecuting y'all,

45so that y'all may become sons of y'all's Father who is in the skies, for he makes his sun rise on the evil and the good and rains on the just and the unjust.

Imitatio Dei — picking up the peacemakers’ sonship (v9). The Father’s family likeness is indiscriminate, enemy-embracing generosity; sun and rain do not wait for desert (cf. Luke 6:35).

46For if y'all love those loving y'all, what reward have y'all? Do not even the tax collectors do the same?

47And if y'all greet only y'all's brothers, what extraordinary thing do y'all do? Do not even the Gentiles do the same?

The kingdom-righteousness is defined by exceeding the reciprocity that even the most despised (collaborator tax-farmers) and the outsiders manage. The word for “extraordinary” (perisson) is the same root as the “abounding” righteousness of v20.

48Y'all therefore shall be whole21 as y'all's heavenly Father is whole.

Luke 6:27–36 — the same paragraph ends on "compassionate," not "complete"

Luke 6:32–34And if y'all love those loving y'all, what favour is it to y'all? For even the sinners love those loving them… even sinners lend to sinners so that they may get back the same.
Luke 6:35…and y'all will be sons of the Most High, because he himself is kind toward the ungrateful and evil.
Luke 6:36Become compassionate (oiktirmones), just as y'all's Father is compassionate.

Luke closes on oiktirmones — compassionate, moved in the guts — a word for a disposition; Matthew closes on teleioi, "complete, brought to their end," a word for a state. Teleios is what the Greek Old Testament uses for Hebrew tamim, "whole, unblemished," the covenant demand made of Abraham at Genesis 17:1 and of Israel at Deuteronomy 18:13. Matthew is not raising Luke's bar so much as changing its category: he ends the six contrasts by restating the Sinai vocation, which is why 5:48 reads as a summary of the whole chapter and not as a last word about enemies. Luke's "sons of the Most High" becoming Matthew's "sons of y'all's Father in the skies" runs the same way.

Greek esesthe oun hymeis teleioi hōs ho patēr hymōn ho ouranios teleios estin This translation “y'all shall be whole as y'all's heavenly Father is whole” NASB “you are to be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect” Luke 6:36 “be merciful, as your Father is merciful” — the parallel reads the wholeness specifically as mercy. Note teleios (from end/goal) renders Heb. tamim — whole, sound, single-hearted (Deut 18:13; Gen 17:1), not flawless. The chapter that opened calling these people salt and light closes calling them to God’s own wholeness — an inclusio of vocation.

Notes

  1. went up onto the mountain (v1). anebē eis to oros is the precise idiom Greek Exodus uses for Moses ascending Sinai (Exod 19:3; 24:18 LXX), with the article — “the mountain,” as if there were only one. The new-Moses staging; but where Moses ascended to receive the law and descended to deliver it, Jesus ascends, sits, and speaks his own words. This is the second of Matthew’s three weighted mountains: testing (ch. 4), teaching (here), commission (28:16).
  2. Blessed (v3). makarios (makarios) — not “happy” in the shallow sense but a wisdom-pronouncement that someone is well-off, congratulated, on the right side of reality. The whole Psalter opens with it (Ps 1:1 LXX, makarios anēr). The Qumran “Beatitudes Scroll” (4Q525) welds the form to Torah-wisdom; what Matthew adds is the eschatological, kingdom horizon those collections lack.
  3. poor in the spirit (v3). ptōchoi is the destitute, the crouching beggar — a stronger word than Luke’s simple “poor” (Luke 6:20). The old charge is that Matthew spiritualizes Luke’s economic blessing; the better reading is that he draws out the sense already in the Hebrew ‘anawim — the humble faithful who have nothing but God (Isa 61:1). Chrysostom (Hom. 15) glosses it “the humble and contrite,” adding that Jesus said “poor” rather than “humble” because it is the stronger word — those who tremble at God’s word (Isa 66:2) — and makes humility the “mother of the virtues,” the foundation the rest climb from. Cf. James 2:5.
  4. righteousness (v6). dikaiosynē in Matthew is closer to covenant faithfulness — doing God’s will, the right ordering of life under the reign of sky — and also God’s own setting-right of the world, than to the imputed-righteousness category of later Pauline systematics. Already planted at the baptism (3:15, “to fulfill all righteousness”), it is the spine of this unit: the hinge of the first beatitude-quartet (v6), the seal of the eighth (v10), and the entrance requirement of v20.
  5. every evil thing (v11). The variant marked in the apparatus adds “word/utterance” (rhēma); the sense is unchanged — reviling speech. The grammar turns here from describing a class (“blessed are the…”) to addressing this community (“blessed are y'all”), the bridge into the salt-and-light sayings. Chrysostom notes the shift signals that persecution is the special portion of those who will carry Jesus’ word.
  6. made foolish (v13). mōranthē — usually “lose its savor,” but the root is folly, not flavor (cf. mōros, “fool,” v22; the foolish builder, 7:26). Since pure salt does not lose saltiness, the saying trades on impossibility — the rabbis caught the same paradox in a riddle exchange (b. Bekhorot 8b): “when salt spoils, with what does one salt it?” … “and does salt spoil?” The kingdom-people are like the one thing that should not be able to fail — which makes the failure unthinkable, and the warning sharp. Salt was covenant (Lev 2:13; Num 18:19); Paul applies it to speech (Col 4:6).
  7. light of the world (v14). Closes the thread opened at 4:16, where Matthew quoted Isaiah 9:2 over Galilee — the people sitting in darkness who “saw a great light.” That light is now declared to be them. The claim is intelligible only as derived light: John makes Jesus himself “the light of the world” (8:12; 9:5); Paul, “you were once darkness, but now light in the Lord” (Eph 5:8; Phil 2:15, phōstēres). The unhideable mountain-city is Zion drawing the nations (Isa 2:2–3; Mic 4:1–2).
  8. tear down (v17). katalysai — to demolish a building; Matthew uses it of the temple’s destruction (24:2; 26:61). Set against plērōsai, “fulfill,” the verb that structured the infancy formula quotations. What “fulfill” means is the chapter’s most contested question. Bible Project / France / Wright: new-covenant Torah — Jesus fills the Law full by bringing out its true intent and embodying its goal, the law written on the heart (Jer 31; Ezek 36), livable now by the Spirit. Constable (dispensationalist): the “interim” view — ethical instruction for disciples from now until the earthly kingdom (rejecting both the strict-millennial and church-only readings). Paul leans the first way: love is the plērōma of the Law (Rom 13:10; Gal 5:14). Held open.
  9. amen I say to y'all (v18). In the Hebrew Scriptures and synagogue, “amen” is the response to another’s word; Jesus uses it to preface his own — a self-authenticating solemnity formula unparalleled in the tradition, of a piece with the “but I say to you” of the contrasts. The same “sky and earth will pass away” is said of his own words at 24:35.
  10. relaxes (v19). lysē, “loosen, relax” — the simple form of v17’s kata-lysai. The kingdom has a ranking, and one’s standing tracks fidelity even to the commandments one thought too small to matter. The verse most embarrasses readers wanting a Law-abolishing Jesus; Matthew’s Jesus does the opposite.
  11. abounds beyond (v20). perisseusē … pleion — not a different righteousness but a more abundant one, overflowing the scrupulous Torah-keeping of the scribes and Pharisees (who were, to the original hearers, precisely the righteous ones). The double negative ou mē eiselthēte is the strongest Greek negation: “absolutely never enter.” Same root returns at v47 (perisson, “extraordinary”).
  12. Raca / Fool (v22). Rhaka is Aramaic reqa, “empty-head, good-for-nothing”; Mōre is Greek “fool.” The escalation (judgment → Sanhedrin → Gehenna) is rhetorical, not a penal code for insults; the point is that contempt is murder’s root. geenna (Gehenna) is Ge-Hinnom, the valley south of Jerusalem where children were once burned to Molech, which Jeremiah renamed “the Valley of Slaughter” (Jer 7:31–32; 19:6) — a real, defiled place that became the standard image of fiery judgment.
  13. quadrans (v26). the Latin quadrans, smallest Roman coin (¼ of an as). Verses 25–26 are a little parable of urgency: settle the breach now, while there is still “the way,” before judgment closes the account.
  14. in order to covet her (v28). pros to epithymēsai expresses purpose — the look that aims at possessing, not the involuntary glance — which keeps the saying from being a counsel of despair. epithymeō is the verb of the tenth commandment (Exod 20:17 LXX), so the seventh commandment is being read through the tenth.
  15. trips you up (v29). skandalizei — “causes to stumble,” the verb behind “scandalize,” from the trigger-stick of a trap. The hyperbole of eye and hand conveys radical, costly seriousness; the early church (Origen excepted) did not read it as a mandate for literal mutilation. Repeated at 18:8–9.
  16. except for a matter of sexual immorality (v32). The exception clause is unique to Matthew; Mark 10:11 and Luke 16:18 give the saying absolutely. The Hillel–Shammai dispute hung on the odd phrase ‘ervat davar in Deut 24:1: Shammai restricted divorce to sexual indecency, Hillel allowed it for “any matter” — “even if she spoiled his dish” — and Akiva, “even if he found another prettier” (m. Gittin 9:10). Jesus lands near Shammai, or rejects the premise that Deut 24 legislates grounds at all (so 19:4–8, going back behind Moses to Gen 2). Note the gender reframing: the husband “makes her commit adultery” — the woman is the one wronged.
  17. from the evil one (v37). ek tou ponērou — “from the evil one” or “from evil”; the same ambiguity recurs in the Lord’s Prayer (6:13). Josephus reports the Essenes refused oaths on principle (War 2.135), so total truthfulness as a piety was already abroad in Second Temple Judaism.
  18. do not resist the evildoer (v39). mē antistēnai tō ponērō — “the evildoer” or, again, “the evil one.” The three illustrations (cheek, cloak, mile) have been read by Walter Wink as a creative nonviolent “third way” that exposes and shames the oppressor, and more straightforwardly by others as radical, unprotesting generosity. The long argument over force — Anabaptist/Tolstoyan pacifism vs. the Augustinian–Lutheran two-kingdoms distinction between private ethics and the magistrate’s sword — lives here; the text does not settle it. Chrysostom (Hom. 18) reads the cheek-turning as directed at personal injury “toward those without,” distinguishing it from the active correction commanded for a sinning brother (ch. 18).
  19. one mile (v41). angareusei is from a Persian loanword for the imperial postal-requisition system — a soldier’s legal right to conscript a provincial to carry his pack one Roman mile (milion, ~1,480 m). Same verb at 27:32 (Simon of Cyrene “compelled” to bear the cross).
  20. hate your enemy (v43). Only the first half (Lev 19:18, love your neighbor) is Scripture. The second half matches no Torah command but does match the sectarian dualism of Qumran: 1QS 1:9–10 charges members “to love all the sons of light… and to hate all the sons of darkness, each according to his guilt in God’s vengeance” (Community Rule), and Josephus says the Essenes vowed to hate the wicked. Jesus’ answer (vv44–45) grounds enemy-love in imitatio Dei: the Father’s sun and rain fall on all alike. Cf. Rom 12:14, 20.
  21. whole (v48). teleios (teleios), from telos (end, goal), means complete, mature, brought to one’s proper end — not morally flawless. In the LXX it renders Heb. tamim, the whole-heartedness asked of Abraham (“walk before me and be tamim,” Gen 17:1) and Israel (“you shall be tamim before the LORD,” Deut 18:13), and the unblemished soundness of an acceptable sacrifice. Luke’s parallel (6:36) glosses the wholeness as mercy. The verse forms the chapter’s closing inclusio with v14: salt-and-light of the world → wholeness of God. The whole body that must not go to Gehenna (vv29–30) and the whole self called to God’s wholeness are the same word doing the chapter’s moral arithmetic.

Where this sits — the deed-block of Matthew 8–9

Chapters 8–9 form one ten-deed answer to the words of chapters 5–7: three triads of miracle, each triad followed by an interlude on what following costs. Unit 8 covers the first triad, the first interlude, and the opening two-thirds of the second triad.

The leper · 8:1–4

1And as he was coming down from the mountain, large crowds followed him.

The descent answers the ascent of 5:1. The Sermon began with him going up; the deeds begin with him coming down — and the same ambiguous verb akoloutheō ("follow") is still doing double duty for crowds and disciples alike.

2And look — a leprous man,1 coming up, kept bowing low2 before him, saying, "Lord, if you are willing, you have the power to cleanse me."

What he does not doubt is the power. The only thing in question is whether Jesus wants to — which is exactly the fear of a man whose disease has taught him that everyone keeps their distance on purpose.

3And stretching out the hand he took hold of him, saying, "I am willing — be cleansed." And immediately his leprosy was cleansed.

Under the Law the flow runs one way: touch the unclean and you become unclean. Here it runs backward. Jesus answers the man's exact words back to him — willing for willing — and the contact that should have defiled him purifies the other instead.

4And Jesus says to him, "See that you tell no one, but go, show yourself to the priest and bring the gift Moses ordered — as evidence for them."3

Obedience to Leviticus 14, and simultaneously a calling card left with the Jerusalem priesthood: someone is doing in Galilee what the Bible records happening twice in a thousand years.

The centurion's boy · 8:5–13

5And as he was entering Capernaum a centurion came up to him, urging him

6and saying, "Lord, my boy4 has been thrown down in the house, paralyzed, dreadfully tormented."

Greek (translit.)ho pais mou beblētai … deinōs basanizomenos
Wooden"my boy has been thrown down … dreadfully tormented"
NASBsmooths beblētai into simply lying down at home, and renders the participle as fearful torment
Why it mattersballō ("throw") is violent, and it recurs three times in fourteen verses: the boy thrown down (8:6), the sons of the kingdom thrown out (8:12), the mother-in-law thrown down by fever (8:15). And basanizō comes back at 8:29 in the mouths of the demons — the tormentors afraid of being tormented.

7And he says to him, "I myself will come and heal him."

The emphatic "I" can also be read as a question — Am I to come? — which would put the strain on whether a Jewish teacher enters a Gentile house at all. Either way the weight falls on Jesus' own person.

8And answering, the centurion said, "Lord, I am not big enough5 for you to come under my roof; but only say it with a word, and my boy will be cured.

9For I myself am a man set under authority, with soldiers under me, and I say to this one 'March,' and he marches, and to another 'Come,' and he comes, and to my slave 'Do this,' and he does it."

This is the theological engine of the whole unit. He is not appealing to Jesus' compassion; he is reasoning from a chain of command. The word of a man under Rome's authority carries Rome; the word of this man carries whatever is over him. Chrysostom noticed the verb the centurion reaches for — not beg, not pray, but command.

10And hearing this, Jesus marveled and said to those following, "Amen I tell y'all, with no one in Israel have I found such trust.

One of only two moments in all four Gospels where Jesus is said to wonder at a person, and the only one where the wonder is admiring. The other is his amazement at unbelief.

11And I tell y'all that many will arrive from the risings and the settings6 and will recline at table with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob in the kingdom of the skies,

12but the sons of the kingdom will be thrown out into the darkness outside; there will be the weeping and the grinding of the teeth."7

Greek (translit.)hoi de huioi tēs basileias ekblēthēsontai eis to skotos to exōteron
Wooden"the sons of the kingdom will be thrown out into the darkness outside"
OT groundthe banquet of Isaiah 25:6–9, where the nations feast on the mountain and death is swallowed up; cf. Isaiah 65:13–14
Constablereads "sons of the kingdom" as Israel presuming on descent; the banquet itself is still future, so this is warning of a coming reversal rather than a present one
France · Wright · D&Aread the reversal as already underway in this very scene: the boundary of the kingdom's people is being redrawn around trust rather than ancestry, and a Roman officer is the demonstration
Held openeveryone agrees the verbs are future. The live question is whether that future has already begun leaking backward into 8:10 — and even the Bible Project's own notes file 8:11 among Jesus' future-kingdom sayings, so this is not simply traditional-vs-literary.

13And Jesus said to the centurion, "Go — as you trusted, let it happen for you." And the boy was cured in that hour.

Luke 7:1–10 · Luke 13:28–30 — the centurion never speaks, and the reversal saying is elsewhere

Luke 7:3And hearing about Jesus he sent to him elders of the Judeans, asking him to come and bring his slave safely through.
Luke 7:4–5And coming to Jesus they were urging him earnestly, saying that he is worthy for you to grant this, for he loves our nation and he himself built us the synagogue.
Luke 13:28–29There will be the weeping and the grinding of teeth, when y'all see Abraham and Isaac and Jacob and all the prophets in the kingdom of God, and yourselves being thrown outside. And they will come from risings and settings and from north and south and will recline in the kingdom of God.

In Luke the centurion never meets Jesus: Jewish elders vouch for him, and the stated ground for helping him is that he funded the local synagogue — he is worthy. Matthew deletes both the intermediaries and the credentials, so that the Gentile speaks for himself and receives nothing but Jesus' astonishment at his trust, and then Matthew imports into the scene a saying Luke keeps in a quite different setting: outsiders recline with the patriarchs while "the sons of the kingdom" are thrown into the outer darkness. On Mark priority plus a shared sayings source the relocation is Matthew's, and it turns a healing into the gospel's first statement of the reversal that 21:43 will make explicit.

Peter's mother-in-law, and evening · 8:14–17

14And coming into Peter's house, Jesus saw his mother-in-law thrown down and burning with fever.

15And he took hold of her hand, and the fever released her, and she was raised and began waiting on him.

Three roots land in one sentence. The touch that cleansed the leper; the release that will carry the paralytic's forgiveness one chapter from now (9:2); and the raising that runs through the storm (8:25–26) and out toward the empty tomb. "Waiting on him" is the same verb used of the angels serving Jesus after the wilderness (4:11) — healing here produces service immediately, without a gap.

16And when evening came, they brought him many who were demon-possessed; and he threw the spirits out with a word, and healed all who were in a bad way —

"With a word" is the centurion's own phrase from 8:8, now shown working exactly as he said it would.

17so that what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet might be filled full: "He himself took our weaknesses and carried off our diseases."

Greek (translit.)autos tas astheneias hēmōn elaben kai tas nosous ebastasen
SourceIsaiah 53:4 — Hebrew holayenu hu nasa, "our sicknesses he himself bore"
Text-formMatthew renders the Hebrew freshly rather than following the Greek Old Testament, which had already shifted the line toward bearing sin. Matthew restores the plain sense — sickness — and applies it to what Jesus is doing tonight.
The tensionDoes this put physical healing inside the atonement, claimable now on the same footing as forgiveness? Or are these healings a down payment on a cross still eighteen chapters away? The second reading holds the timeline better: Isaiah 53 casts its shadow forward over the healing ministry without the transaction having yet landed.

Two who would follow · 8:18–22

18And seeing large crowds around him, Jesus gave the order to go off to the other side.

"The other side" is Gentile territory, and it sets up 8:28. Note the trigger: he sees a crowd and leaves. The miracles are not being used to gather a following.

19And one scribe, coming up, said to him, "Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go off to."

The address matters. "Teacher" is professional courtesy — the same word Jesus' opponents use later (9:11; 12:38; 22:16) when they mean nothing more than rabbi. Nobody in Matthew who calls him Teacher is confessing anything.

20And Jesus says to him, "The foxes have burrows and the birds of the sky have roosts, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lean his head."

Greek (translit.)ho huios tou anthrōpou ouk echei pou tēn kephalēn klinē
Wooden"the Son of Man has nowhere to lean the head"
Behind itDaniel 7:13–14 — one like a son of man approaches the Ancient of Days and receives a dominion that will never pass away; see also the Son of Man of 1 Enoch
The paradoxThe title's first full outing in Matthew puts everlasting dominion and having no bed in the same breath. That refusal to resolve is the point — it is a self-designation that fits no ready-made box, which may be exactly why he chose it.

21And another of the disciples said to him, "Lord, let me first go off and bury my father."

22But Jesus says to him, "Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead."8

The same root that released the fever now means let go of, and it is aimed at the fifth commandment's most solemn application. No rabbinic parallel exists for a saying this severe against filial duty; it was startling in its own world, not only in ours.

The shaking on the sea · 8:23–27

23And as he stepped into the boat, his disciples followed him.

After two men whose following stalled at the threshold, the verb finally lands cleanly on the disciples — and immediately gets tested by weather.

24And look — a great shaking9 happened in the sea, so that the boat was being buried under the waves; but he himself was sleeping.

Sleeping through a storm at sea is Jonah's posture, and Matthew will make the Jonah comparison explicit at 12:40. Meanwhile Psalm 107:28–30 is the shape of the whole scene: sailors cry out, the storm is hushed to a whisper, the waves stand still.

25And coming up they roused him, saying, "Lord, save — we are being destroyed!"

26And he says to them, "Why are y'all cowards, y'all little-faiths?" Then, having been raised, he rebuked the winds and the sea, and there was a great stillness.

Greek (translit.)ti deiloi este, oligopistoi

Mark 4:35–41 — reproach, and the rebuke comes after the calm

Mark 4:37–38And a great squall (lailaps) of wind comes about… And he was in the stern, sleeping on the cushion; and they rouse him and say to him: Teacher, does it not matter to you that we are being destroyed?
Mark 4:39–40And waking he rebuked the wind and said to the sea: Be silent, be muzzled. And the wind died down and there was a great calm. And then he said to them: Why are y'all cowardly? Do y'all not yet have trust?

Mark's disciples reproach Jesus — does it not matter to you — where Matthew's pray to him, "Lord, save, we are being destroyed," using sōzō, the verb of 1:21. Mark's Jesus stills the sea first and reprimands the disciples afterwards; Matthew's reverses the order so the diagnosis precedes the rescue, and Mark's "y'all do not yet have trust" becomes oligopistoi, "little-faiths" — men with some trust rather than none. Matthew also swaps Mark's lailaps, a squall, for seismos, a shaking, the word he will use for the earthquakes at the cross and the tomb.

Wooden"why are y'all cowardly, y'all little-faiths" — a single compound word, plural, used as a name
NASBexpands the compound into a phrase about men of little faith
Why keep it uglyMatthew coined oligopistos as a near-nickname for the disciples — it appears at 6:30, here, then 14:31 and 16:8. Smoothing it into a phrase loses that it functions as a label he keeps re-attaching to the same people.
"Rebuked"the verb epitimaō is Matthew's standard exorcism word (17:18). Jesus addresses the storm the way he addresses a demon — placing the sea inside the old combat imagery of Psalm 104:7, Job 38:8–11, and Genesis 1's unruly deep.

27And the people marveled, saying, "What sort of being is this, that even the winds and the sea listen under him?"

The question is left hanging for the reader to answer out of the Psalter, where commanding the sea is something exactly one being does.

The Gadarene demoniacs · 8:28–34

28And when he came to the other side, into the country of the Gadarenes,10 two demon-possessed men met him, coming out of the tombs — so dangerous that no one had the strength to pass by along that road.

Gentile Decapolis, confirmed two verses on by a pig herd. Matthew has two demoniacs where Mark and Luke have one — his habit of pairing figures (compare the two blind men at 9:27), possibly to satisfy the two-witness standard of Deuteronomy 19:15.

29And look — they screamed, saying, "What is there between us and you,11 Son of God? Did you come here to torment us before the appointed time?"

Greek (translit.)ti hēmin kai soi, huie tou theou; ēlthes hōde pro kairou basanisai hēmas;
Wooden"what to us and to you, Son of God? Did you come here before the season to torment us?"
Who says itThe demons name him Son of God — a title no human character in this unit has managed. The disciples got as far as "what sort of being is this?" (8:27) two verses earlier. The demons already know, and it terrifies them.
pro kairou"before the appointed season" — the demons are working from an eschatological timetable and are startled to find judgment arriving early. Another data point for the inaugurated-but-unfinished tension running through this whole unit.
The inclusiobasanizō opened the unit with a human being tormented by illness (8:6) and closes it with the tormentors dreading torment. The word runs the chapter's whole arc in reverse.

30Now far off from them there was a herd of many pigs, grazing.

31And the demons kept urging him, saying, "If you throw us out, send us off into the herd of pigs."

Motive unstated: spite, or dread of the waterless places, or simply that demons hate homelessness — a horror Matthew spells out at 12:43–45.

32And he said to them, "Go." And coming out, they went off into the pigs. And look — the whole herd rushed down the cliff into the sea and died in the waters.

One word. The same sea that was burying the disciples eight verses ago now closes over the unclean instead.

33And the herdsmen fled, and going off into the city they reported everything, including the business of the demon-possessed men.

34And look — the whole city came out to meet Jesus, and seeing him they urged him to move on away from their borders.12

The same verb the demons used three verses earlier. The demons and the townspeople end up making structurally the same request — one out of dread, the other out of ledgers — and both want him gone. This is the first flat rejection of the Messiah anywhere in the narrative, and Matthew lets the thread run unbroken to Golgotha.

Mark 5:1–20 — twenty verses down to seven, and the name Legion goes

Mark 5:3–5…no one was able to bind him any longer, not even with a chain… and always, night and day, in the tombs and in the mountains, he was crying out and cutting himself down with stones.
Mark 5:9And he was asking him: What is your name? And he says to him: Legion is my name, because we are many.
Mark 5:19–20Go into your house to your own, and report to them how much the Lord has done for you, and that he had mercy on you. And he went off and began to proclaim in the Decapolis how much Jesus had done for him, and everyone was astonished.

Matthew cuts the chains, the self-cutting, the name Legion with its Roman freight, the restored man sitting clothed and sound-minded, and the commission to proclaim in the Decapolis — he keeps the confrontation and the drowning and nothing else, and he doubles the demoniac, as he doubles the blind men at 9:27 and again at 20:30. On Mark priority this is abbreviation of the most severe kind in the gospel, and it strips out precisely the material a preacher would want. What Matthew puts back is two words at 8:29, pro kairou, "before the appointed time": his demons are not negotiating about geography, as Mark's are, but protesting the schedule, which turns a local exorcism into a skirmish fought ahead of the judgment.

Notes

  1. leprous (8:2). Hebrew tsara'at ("scale disease") in Leviticus 13–14 covers a family of skin conditions, not modern Hansen's disease specifically. The exclusion was social and cultic as much as medical: outside the camp, outside worship, outside touch. Rabbinic tradition treated healing it as comparable to raising the dead, and a strand of interpretation reading Isaiah 53:4 even pictured the Messiah himself as one so afflicted — which makes Matthew's citation of that exact verse fifteen lines later worth pausing over.
  2. kept bowing low (8:2). prosekynei, imperfect of proskyneō — the verb used of the magi (2:2, 11) and of worship generally, but also of ordinary obeisance to a superior. Matthew declines to disambiguate and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion.
  3. as evidence for them (8:4). eis martyrion autois — the dative can mean "for" or "against." Chrysostom took the second: the healed man's Levitical offering functions as an accusation of the leaders' unbelief, since he became a witness to them precisely by trusting what they would not.
  4. boy (8:6). pais sits between "servant," "slave," and "son/boy"; Luke's parallel (Luke 7:2) uses the unambiguous word for slave. "Boy" keeps the English as undecided as the Greek and preserves the evident affection.
  5. not big enough (8:8). hikanos means "sufficient, up to the size of the thing" — a measuring word, not a moral one. "Worthy" (most versions) imports a guilt register the Greek does not have; the centurion is saying his house does not measure up, not that he is wicked.
  6. risings and settings (8:11). apo anatolōn kai dysmōn, literally "from risings and settings" — sunrise and sunset as compass points. anatolē is the same word used of the magi "from the rising" in 2:1 and of the star's rising in 2:2, and it carries the Greek Old Testament's freight for Hebrew tsemach, "Branch" (Zechariah 3:8; 6:12). The Gentiles arriving at the banquet come in on the same word the Gentile magi came in on.
  7. weeping and grinding of teeth (8:12). The formula appears six times in Matthew and nowhere else in the Gospels except once in Luke (8:12; 13:42, 50; 22:13; 24:51; 25:30). Note the definite articles — the weeping, the grinding — as though pointing at something already known rather than describing it fresh.
  8. let the dead bury their own dead (8:22). Two readings compete. If the father has just died, burial was same-day, and the man would not be standing here talking — which argues for the second reading: he is asking to defer indefinitely, staying home until his father eventually dies. That makes Jesus' answer harder, not easier, since the request was reasonable by every standard of the culture.
  9. shaking (8:24). seismos normally means earthquake; Matthew uses it of the sea here and of the ground at the crucifixion (27:54) and the tomb (28:2). The Sea of Galilee sits some six hundred feet below sea level with high ground east of it, and the resulting downdrafts make sudden violent squalls a standing feature of the lake.
  10. Gadarenes (8:28). The manuscripts split three ways — Gadarenes, Gerasenes, Gergesenes — as later copyists tried to fit the geography, since Gerasa sits some thirty miles inland. Patristic writers were already noting that Gadara and Gergesa lie near enough that residents of the wider region could be described either way.
  11. what is there between us and you (8:29). ti hēmin kai soi renders a Hebrew idiom of hostile disengagement — what have we to do with each other — used at 2 Samuel 16:10 and 1 Kings 17:18, and heard again from Jesus' own lips at John 2:4. It is a demand to be left alone.
  12. urged him to leave (8:34). Chrysostom's reading of the whole episode is that Jesus permitted the pigs precisely so that a region which did not know him would learn his power — and that its inhabitants proved senseless, since what should have produced worship produced eviction. See Homily 28 on Matthew.

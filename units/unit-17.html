    The first prediction, and the trip-up · 16:21–23

    21From then Jesus began to show his learners that he must go off to Jerusalem and suffer many things from the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and on the third day be raised.1

    The second "from then" (apo tote): at 4:17 Jesus "began to proclaim," and here he "began to show." Peter "began" in the next verse with the same verb. Elders, chief priests and scribes are the three groups who sit in the Jerusalem council; they return together at 26:3, 26:57 and 27:41.

    22And Peter, taking him aside, began to charge him sharply, saying, "God be gracious to you, Master! This shall by no means be for you."2

    epitimaō, "charge sharply": the verb Jesus uses of the winds at 8:26 and of the demon at 17:18, here turned on Jesus by his own learner.

    23But turning, he said to Peter, "Go away behind me, Adversary! You are a trip-up to me, because you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of people."

    hypage opisō mou, "go away behind me": "go away" is Jesus' word to the tester at 4:10, and "behind me" is the follower's place (4:19; 16:24). satanas, "Adversary," the title, as at 4:10. skandalon, "trip-up": the trigger-stick of a trap, the thing a foot catches on. "Trip-up" as a noun is a provisional rendering, kept to match the verb "trip up" used everywhere else.
    Isaiah 8:14 in the Hebrew calls God a rock of stumbling (the Greek Isaiah uses other words), and Romans 9:33 and 1 Peter 2:8, Peter's own letter, set "rock" beside "trip-up" in one phrase; here the man just called the rock (16:18) is a trip-up.

    The third testing in the wilderness Matt 4:8–10

    After forty days in the wilderness, the slanderer takes Jesus along to a very high mountain and shows him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. All of it can be his, the slanderer says, if he will fall down and worship. Jesus answers, "Go away, Adversary!" and quotes Deuteronomy 6:13: Yahweh alone is to be worshiped.

    • "go away" (hypage) with "Adversary" (satanas)
    • the world (kosmos, 4:8; 16:26) and glory (doxa, 4:8; 16:27)
    • a kingdom offered without a cross
    • a high mountain (4:8; 17:1)

    There the tester speaks for himself and offers the world outright. Here the voice is a friend's, Peter's, straight after he was blessed and named the rock, and what he offers is only that the suffering should not happen. Jesus adds "behind me," which sends Peter back to the place of a follower rather than away for good.

    It names the refusal of the cross as the same testing Jesus met in the wilderness, now coming from inside the circle of learners. The rock becomes a trip-up in the space of five verses.

    At 26:39 Jesus asks for the cup to pass from him and then yields to the Father's will: the road Peter tried to block, taken on purpose.

    Mark 8:31–33 · Luke 9:22 — Peter's words, and the rock reversed

    Mark 8:31–33He "began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected (apodokimazō) by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise (anistēmi)." He spoke the word openly. Peter takes him aside and begins to charge him sharply, but his words are not given. Jesus turns, looks at his learners, and charges Peter sharply: "Go away behind me, Adversary, because you are not setting your mind on the things of God but on the things of people."
    Luke 9:22"The Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and on the third day be raised." Then straight on to the sayings about the cross. No Peter, no Adversary.
    Matthew 16:21–23"From then Jesus began to show"; "go off to Jerusalem"; "on the third day be raised"; no "rejected." Peter's own words are given: "God be gracious to you, Master!" Jesus adds, "You are a trip-up to me."

    Only Matthew sets this scene straight after the blessing and the rock (16:17–19), so only Matthew can turn the rock into a trip-up, and only he lets Peter speak. He names Jerusalem as the destination and holds back the word "rejected" for the psalm Jesus quotes at 21:42, "the stone the builders rejected." He also says "he" where Mark and Luke say "the Son of Man": the title was the question at 16:13.

    The cost of coming behind · 16:24–28

    24Then Jesus said to his learners, "If anyone wants to come behind me, let him disown himself, and pick up his cross, and follow me.

    aparneomai, "disown": to say of someone, "I do not know him." stauros, "cross": the execution-stake a condemned man carried to the place of his death. "Come behind me" is the same opisō mou as "go away behind me" a verse earlier.
    Another Simon, Simon of Cyrene, is made to "pick up his cross" in the same words at 27:32 (Mark 15:21), on the day this Simon disowns Jesus.

    25For whoever wants to save his life will lose it; but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it.

    psychē, "life": the living self, the breath that makes a person alive, not a soul set apart from the body. The saying was first given at 10:39 and is repeated here at the first passion prediction.

    26For what will a person be profited if he gains the whole world but forfeits his life? Or what will a person give as an exchange-price for his life?

    antallagma, "exchange-price": what is handed over in return for something. The question has no answer in money, and the unit comes back to the price of a life with the two-drachma tax (17:24–27). "The whole world" (kosmos) is what the tester offered at 4:8.

    27For the Son of Man is about to come in the glory of his Father with his angels, and then he will pay back each one according to his doing.

    praxis, "doing": what a person does, the course of his actions; a rare word in the gospels.
    Psalm 61:13 in the Greek numbering (62:12 in English) and Proverbs 24:12 say God will "pay back each one according to his works"; Matthew puts the same formula in the hand of the Son of Man.

    28Amen I tell y'all: there are some of those standing here who will by no means taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom."3

    "Taste death" is the Hebrew idiom for dying. "Coming" (erchomai) is the plain verb, the one the Greek Daniel uses of "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds (Dan 7:13).

    Mark 8:34–9:1 · Luke 9:23–27 — who hears it; the paying back; the kingdom

    Mark 8:34–9:1"Summoning the crowd with his learners." "Whoever loses his life for my sake and the good-news' will save it." Then: "Whoever is ashamed of me and my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man will also be ashamed of him when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels." The promise: "until they see the kingdom of God come with power."
    Luke 9:23–27"He said to all." "Pick up his cross daily." The same saying about being ashamed. The promise: "until they see the kingdom of God."
    Matthew 16:24–28"Jesus said to his learners." No "good-news." No saying about shame; in its place, "he will pay back each one according to his doing." The promise: "until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom."

    Matthew narrows the audience to the learners and leaves out the shame saying (its cousin, about disowning, is already at 10:33). He puts a judgment formula from the psalms in its place, and in the last line he writes the Son of Man into the promise where Mark and Luke have "the kingdom of God." The kingdom becomes his kingdom.

    On a high mountain · 17:1–8

    1And after six days Jesus takes along Peter and James and John his brother, and brings them up into a high mountain, by themselves.

    "After six days" is the only exact time-mark in the unit. "A high mountain" is the phrase of the third testing (4:8). James and John are the sons of Zebedee (4:21; 10:2), whose mother will ask for their seats in the kingdom at 20:20–28.

    Moses on Mount Sinai Exod 24:15–18; 34:29–35

    After the covenant is sealed with blood, Moses goes up the mountain with Aaron, Nadab and Abihu and seventy elders, and they see the God of Israel. The cloud covers the mountain six days, and on the seventh day Yahweh calls to Moses out of the middle of the cloud. Moses goes into the cloud and stays forty days. When he comes down later with the second tablets, the skin of his face has been glorified, and Aaron and the people are afraid to come near him.

    • six days, then the call
    • a mountain, a cloud, a voice out of the cloud
    • three named companions (with the elders)
    • a face changed by the meeting, and fear in those who see it
    • "talk together with" (syllaleō), the verb of Exod 34:35, where Moses speaks with God, and of Matt 17:3

    Moses alone goes into the cloud, and his face is glorified (doxazō) because he has spoken with God; here Jesus is the one changed, and Matthew's verb for his face is "shone" (lampō). At Sinai the voice gives Moses the Law; here the voice tells the three to listen to Jesus. And Moses himself stands on this mountain beside him.

    It sets Jesus in Moses' place, at the mountain of the covenant, with the Moses of that mountain present to see it.

    2 Peter 1:16–18 recalls the voice, "This is my Son, my beloved, in whom I delighted," heard "on the holy mountain."

    2And he was changed in form in front of them, and his face shone like the sun, and his garments became white as the light.

    metamorphoomai, "be changed in form": from morphē, "form, outward shape." The change is to how he looks; the verb does not say what was there before. phōs, "light."
    At 13:43 "the righteous will blaze out like the sun in the kingdom of their Father," the same simile: what is shown here is promised to them.

    Mark 9:2–8 · Luke 9:28–36 — the shining face, the fear, the touch

    Mark 9:2–8"After six days." His garments became dazzling, very white, "such as no launderer on earth can whiten"; nothing is said of his face. "Elijah with Moses" appeared. Peter calls him "Rabbi," and Mark explains: "he did not know what to answer, for they were terrified." The voice: "This is my Son, the beloved; listen to him."
    Luke 9:28–36"About eight days after these words." "While he was praying" the look of his face became different. Moses and Elijah, appearing in glory, spoke of his exodos, "departure," which he was about to fulfill in Jerusalem. Peter and the others were heavy with sleep. The voice: "This is my Son, the Chosen One; listen to him." Afterward they kept silent of their own accord.
    Matthew 17:1–8"After six days." His face shone like the sun; his garments white as the light. "Moses and Elijah." Peter says "Master" and "if you wish." The voice adds "in whom I delighted." The learners fall on their faces and are very much afraid; Jesus comes, touches them and says, "Get up, and do not be afraid."

    Only Matthew puts the sun on the face, which is where Moses' change was seen. He names Moses first, gives the voice the baptism's full words (3:17), and drops Mark's excuse for Peter. The falling, the fear, the touch and the "get up" are his alone, and they make the scene end with Jesus raising the frightened rather than with their confusion.

    3And look: Moses and Elijah appeared to them, talking together with him.4

    syllaleō, "talk together with": the verb of Exodus 34:35, where Moses goes in to speak with God.

    Elijah at Horeb 1 Kgs 19:8–18

    After the fire on Carmel, Jezebel swears to take Elijah's life, and he runs. Fed by an angel, he walks forty days and forty nights to Horeb, the mountain of God, the mountain of Moses. Yahweh passes by: not in the wind, not in the quake, not in the fire, but in a low, still voice, and he sends Elijah back to his work. The last lines of the Greek prophets join the two men: remember the law of Moses given at Horeb, and God will send Elijah before the great day (Mal 3:22–24).

    • the same mountain, Sinai or Horeb
    • a man who met God there, now present
    • Moses and Elijah named together, as at the end of Malachi

    Elijah came to the mountain fleeing the rejection of his message; here he stands on a mountain beside the one who has just said he will be rejected and killed. At Horeb God was not in the spectacle; here the spectacle is the point, and then it is gone and Jesus is alone (17:8).

    Both men who met God on his mountain are present, with Jesus between them, and the voice speaks to him, not to them.

    4And Peter, answering, said to Jesus, "Master, it is good for us to be here. If you wish, I will make three tents here: one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah."

    skēnē, "tent": the word for the tent of meeting in the Greek Exodus, and also for the booths of the autumn feast. Either may sit behind Peter's offer.

    5While he was still speaking, look: a cloud full of light overshadowed them, and look: a voice out of the cloud, saying, "This is my Son, the beloved, in whom I delighted. Listen to him."

    phōteinos, "full of light," from phōs, "light." episkiazō, "overshadow." eudokeō, "delight in, be well pleased with": the voice repeats the baptism word for word (3:17), and adds one command.
    "Listen to him" is Moses' promise of a prophet like himself, whom Israel must hear, in the same words (Deut 18:15).

    6And the learners, hearing it, fell on their faces and were very much afraid.

    7And Jesus came up and, touching them, said, "Get up, and do not be afraid."

    egeirō, "get up, be raised": the same verb as "be raised" in 16:21, 17:9 and 17:23. haptomai, "touch," as at 9:29.

    Daniel falls and is raised Dan 8:17–18; 10:9–19

    At the end of a vision Gabriel comes near, and Daniel falls on his face; Gabriel calls him "son of man," tells him the vision is for the time of the end, touches him and sets him on his feet (8:17–18). Later, by the great river, Daniel hears the voice of a figure in shining linen and falls on his face. A hand touches him and raises him to his knees; he is told he is a man greatly loved and not to be afraid. Touched again, he is strengthened (10:9–19).

    • falling on the face at a voice
    • a touch, and being raised (egeirō)
    • "do not be afraid"
    • a "vision" (horama), and "son of man" (Dan 8:17)

    In Daniel the hand that touches belongs to an angel, and the glorious figure stays apart. Here the one who was shining is the one who comes over, touches and raises them.

    The glorious one is also the one who comforts. And the learners are "raised" before he is.

    8And lifting up their eyes, they saw no one except Jesus himself alone.

    Coming down: Elijah · 17:9–13

    9And as they were coming down from the mountain, Jesus commanded them, saying, "Tell no one the vision until the Son of Man is raised from the dead."

    horama, "vision, a thing seen": Matthew's only use of the word, and it is Jesus who calls the scene by it. The silence has a date set to it: the rising.

    10And the learners asked him, saying, "Why then do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?"

    11And he, answering, said, "Elijah does come, and will restore all things.

    apokathistēmi, "restore, put back in its place."
    Malachi's Elijah "restores" the heart of father to son with the same verb in the Greek (Mal 3:22–24 in the Greek numbering; 4:5–6 in English), and Sirach 48:10 has Elijah "restore" the tribes of Jacob.

    12But I tell y'all that Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him, but did with him whatever they wished. So also the Son of Man is about to suffer at their hands."

    "Whatever they wished" is what Herod and Herodias did to John (14:1–12), told beside Ahab and Jezebel's pursuit of Elijah in unit 14. paschō, "suffer," is the verb of 16:21.

    13Then the learners understood that he spoke to them about John the Immerser.

    syniēmi, "understand," literally "throw together": the pieces put together. John is the Elijah "who is about to come" of 11:14.

    Mark 9:9–13 — Elijah made plain

    Mark 9:9–13He orders them to tell no one "what they had seen." They keep the saying, "questioning what the rising from the dead is." Jesus: Elijah coming first restores all things, "and how is it written of the Son of Man that he must suffer many things and be treated with contempt?" Elijah has come, "and they did to him whatever they wished, as it is written of him." The scene ends there.
    Luke 9:36No command, no Elijah question. The learners keep silent and tell no one in those days.
    Matthew 17:9–13"Tell no one the vision." No puzzling over the rising. Neither "it is written" clause. The scene ends, "Then the learners understood that he spoke to them about John the Immerser."

    Matthew makes the identification plain and gives the understanding to the learners, where Mark leaves them questioning. He drops both appeals to what is written and lets the pattern carry the point instead: as John was treated, so the Son of Man will be.

    The boy below the mountain · 17:14–20

    14And when they came to the crowd, a man came up to him, kneeling before him

    15and saying, "Master, show loyal-love to my son, because he is moon-struck and suffers badly; for often he falls into the fire, and often into the water.5

    eleeō, "show loyal-love": the covenant kindness of Hosea 6:6, cried for by the blind men (9:27) and the Canaanite mother (15:22). paschō, "suffer," the verb of 16:21 and 17:12, now of a child.
    The Canaanite mother of 15:22 also cried "Show me loyal-love, Master" for a child "badly" afflicted, and her daughter was healed "from that hour"; her trust was great, and here it is the learners' trust that fails.

    16And I brought him to your learners, and they were not able to heal him."

    17And Jesus, answering, said, "O not-trusting and twisted generation, how long will I be with y'all? How long will I put up with y'all? Bring him here to me."

    apistos, "not-trusting," the opposite of pistis, "trust," rendered as at 13:58. diastrephō, "twist, turn aside." genea, "generation": the people of a time, as at 11:16 and 16:4.

    18And Jesus charged it sharply, and the demon went out from him, and the boy was healed from that hour.

    "From that hour" closes the healings of 9:22 and 15:28 the same way. epitimaō, "charge sharply," the verb Peter used on Jesus at 16:22.

    Moses comes down to a faithless people Exod 32; Deut 32:5, 20

    While Moses is on the mountain receiving the tablets, the people at its foot make a golden calf. Yahweh tells him to go down, for the people have acted lawlessly, and he comes down with the tablets in his hands and breaks them at the foot of the mountain. Later, in his song, Moses calls the people "a crooked and twisted generation" (Deut 32:5), and Yahweh calls them "sons in whom there is no trust" (32:20).

    • coming down from a mountain of glory to failure below
    • "generation" and "twisted" (genea, diastrephō, Deut 32:5)
    • a people without trust (pistis, Deut 32:20)

    At Sinai the failure is idolatry, and Moses breaks the tablets. Here the failure is the learners' own small trust, a father is pleading for his son, and Jesus does not break anything: he says "bring him here to me" and heals.

    The glory on the mountain is set against the failure in the valley, as at Sinai, and Jesus speaks Moses' complaint and God's together.

    Philippians 2:15 reuses "a crooked and twisted generation" for the world the church shines in. Luke's version of this cry also has "twisted" (9:41); Mark's has only "not-trusting" (9:19).

    19Then the learners, coming to Jesus privately, said, "Why were we not able to throw it out?"

    20And he says to them, "Because of y'all's little-faith. For amen I tell y'all, if y'all have trust like a grain of mustard, y'all will say to this mountain, 'Move over from here to there,' and it will move over; and nothing will be impossible for y'all."678

    oligopistia, "little-faith": the noun made from oligopistoi, "little-faiths," Jesus' nickname for the learners (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). "Faith" stays in that one word on purpose; pistis is "trust" everywhere else, even in this verse. There is no verse 21 in the Greek text this study follows (note 8).

    Mark 9:14–29 · Luke 9:37–43a — where the failure of trust falls

    Mark 9:14–29Sixteen verses. Scribes arguing with the learners; a mute spirit; a violent seizure in front of Jesus. The father: "if you can." Jesus: "all things are possible to the one who trusts." The father: "I trust; help my not-trusting!" Afterward: "This kind can come out by nothing but prayer."
    Luke 9:37–43aThe boy is an only child. "O not-trusting and twisted generation, how long will I be with y'all and put up with y'all?" All are astonished at the greatness of God.
    Matthew 17:14–20Seven verses. No scribes, no muteness, no seizure, no dialogue with the father about trust. The father cries, "Master, show loyal-love." "Twisted," with Luke. The learners ask privately, and the answer is "y'all's little-faith," then the mustard seed and the mountain.

    Matthew cuts the scene to its bones and moves the failure of trust from the father to the learners. Mark's answer is prayer; Matthew's is trust as small as a mustard seed, set against their little-faith, so the lesson is aimed at the learners who were on and below the mountain.

    The second prediction · 17:22–23

    22And as they were gathering in Galilee, Jesus said to them, "The Son of Man is about to be handed over into the hands of people,

    paradidōmi, "hand over": the verb that introduced Judas (10:4), and from here the verb of the passion. "Gathering" (systrephomai) follows the Greek text this study uses; other manuscripts read "staying."
    The servant of Isaiah 53:12 in the Greek is "handed over" to death and "handed over" because of their sins, and Daniel 7:25 has the holy ones "handed over" into the hand of the horn for a time.

    23and they will kill him, and on the third day he will be raised." And they were grieved very much.

    lypeō, "grieve": the same "grieved very much" comes back at the supper, when Jesus says one of them will hand him over (26:22).

    Mark 9:30–32 · Luke 9:43b–45 — grief, instead of not understanding

    Mark 9:30–32They pass through Galilee, and he does not want anyone to know. "The Son of Man is handed over (paradidotai) into the hands of people, and they will kill him, and having been killed, after three days he will rise." "But they did not understand the saying, and were afraid to ask him."
    Luke 9:43b–45"Put these words into y'all's ears: the Son of Man is about to be handed over into the hands of people." No death, no rising. "They did not understand this saying, and it was hidden from them so that they would not perceive it, and they were afraid to ask."
    Matthew 17:22–23Handed over, killed, "on the third day raised." "And they were grieved very much."

    Where Mark and Luke say the learners did not understand, Matthew says they grieved. Grief needs understanding: they have taken in that he will die. It fits 17:13, "then the learners understood," and the difference from the first prediction, where Peter fought the saying, is the difference a mountain made.

    The two-drachma coin · 17:24–27

    24And when they came into Capernaum, those who took the two-drachma coins came up to Peter and said, "Does y'all's teacher not pay the two-drachma?"

    didrachmon, "two-drachma coin": the yearly half-shekel each Jewish man paid for the upkeep of the temple. This is the last time Matthew names Capernaum (4:13; 8:5; 11:23). "Y'all's teacher": the collectors speak to Peter as one of a group.

    The half-shekel ransom Exod 30:11–16

    When Israel is counted, each man who passes over to be numbered gives half a shekel, "a ransom for his life" to Yahweh, so that no plague falls on them in the counting. Rich and poor give the same. The silver goes to the work of the tent of meeting, as a memorial for the people before Yahweh.

    • the half-shekel, which the Greek Exodus calls a didrachmon, "two-drachma"
    • "a ransom for his life" (lytron, psychē), answering 16:26's "what will a person give as an exchange-price for his life?"
    • the upkeep of God's dwelling

    In Exodus the half-shekel is paid once at a counting, and every man owes it. In Matthew it has become a yearly temple tax, and Jesus turns it into a question about kings and sons: the sons are free, and he pays anyway.

    The Son pays a ransom-coin he does not owe, for himself and for Peter, on the road where he will give his life as "a ransom for many" (20:28).

    25He says, "Yes." And when he came into the house, Jesus got to him first, saying, "What do you think, Simon? From whom do the kings of the earth take toll or census-tax: from their sons, or from the foreigners?"

    telos, "toll," a duty on goods; kēnsos, "census-tax," a head-tax. Jesus calls him "Simon," the name he had before the rock (16:17). "Got to him first" (prophthanō): Jesus speaks before Peter can raise it.
    The word for the census-tax (kēnsos) returns in Matthew only at 22:17 and 22:19, the question whether it is permitted to give it to Caesar.

    26And when he said, "From the foreigners," Jesus said to him, "Then the sons are free.

    huios, "son," runs through the unit nine times: the Son of Man, the beloved Son, the father's son, the kings' sons.

    27But so that we do not trip them up, go to the sea, throw a hook, and take the first fish that comes up; and opening its mouth you will find a stater. Take that and give it to them for me and you."910

    skandalizō, "trip up": the verb of the noun at 16:23, so the unit opens and closes on it. A statēr, "stater," is a four-drachma silver coin: the tax for two.

    Notes

    1. "Must" (v. 21). dei, "it must be," is the language of what has to happen in God's plan. Matthew uses it of Elijah who "must come first" (17:10) and in the arrest, where Jesus asks how the Scriptures would be filled full "that it must happen this way" (26:54). The Greek Daniel uses the same word for what "must come to be" in the last days (Dan 2:28–29). The first prediction is not a guess about danger; it is a road laid out beforehand.
    2. "God be gracious to you" (v. 22). The Greek is hileōs soi, literally "propitious to you": a short prayer, "may God be gracious to you." It is rendered close to that here. The Greek Old Testament uses the same phrase where the Hebrew says "far be it" (as at 2 Sam 23:17), and many English versions translate it that way ("Far be it from you, Lord!"). Either way Peter means the same thing: may God spare you this. "This shall by no means be for you" renders the strong double negative ou mē.
    3. Who will see the Son of Man coming (v. 28). The promise is among the hardest sayings in the book, and readings divide. (1) The Transfiguration, six days later: some of those standing there see him in glory on the mountain, a preview of the kingdom. The "after six days" of 17:1 ties the two together, and 2 Peter 1:16–18 calls the mountain scene the "power and coming" the apostles saw. Many older readers and a dispensational reading take it this way. (2) Vindication and enthronement: Daniel's "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds to the Ancient of Days to receive a kingdom (Dan 7:13–14), so the "coming" is a going up, fulfilled in the resurrection and, on some readings, in the judgment on Jerusalem within that generation (24:34). (3) Others point to the final return. Matthew keeps his own word for the final return, parousia (24:3, 27, 37), for later, and uses the plain "come" here; and he writes the Son of Man into the place where Mark has "the kingdom of God come with power." This study holds the question open; the readings can also be layered, the mountain as a first sight of what the rising and the end make full.
    4. Moses and Elijah (v. 3). One reading takes them as the Law and the Prophets, standing beside the one who fills them full (5:17). Another reads them as the two who left this life strangely, Moses buried where no one knows and Elijah taken up, now present in the kingdom's light. A caution runs against the first reading pushed too far: the scene does not set them aside, since they talk with him. What both clearly share is a meeting with God on Sinai, or Horeb, and a ministry rejected by the people. Malachi ends by naming them together (Mal 3:22–24 in the Greek; 4:4–6 in English). ↗
    5. "Moon-struck" (v. 15). selēniazomai, from selēnē, "moon," was the ordinary word for seizures that seemed to come with the moon's phases; it describes what we would call epilepsy. Matthew uses it only here and at 4:24, where it is rendered the same way. The father's description of falling into fire and water fits seizures, and Matthew also calls the cause a demon (17:18); he does not separate the two.
    6. "Little-faith" or "not-trusting" (v. 20). The Greek text this study follows reads oligopistia, "little-faith." Many later manuscripts read apistia, "not-trusting," the word of 13:58, and many older English Bibles follow them ("because of your unbelief"). The difference matters to the scene: the learners are not without trust altogether; they have too little of it, and Jesus answers that even a little, the size of a mustard grain, is enough.
    7. The mustard seed and the mountain (v. 20). The mustard grain is the seed of the kingdom parable at 13:31–32, "smaller than all the seeds." "This mountain" may point at the one they have just come down. The saying returns at 21:21 after the fig tree, "if y'all say to this mountain, 'Be lifted up and thrown into the sea'"; Luke 17:6 has a mulberry tree instead. Moving mountains was a proverbial picture of the impossible (Isa 40:4; 54:10; 1 Cor 13:2).
    8. The missing verse 21. Many later manuscripts and the King James add, "But this kind does not go out except by prayer and fasting," a saying close to Mark 9:29. The oldest Greek manuscripts do not have it, and the Greek text this study follows leaves it out, so the numbering here goes from 20 to 22.
    9. Three readings of the tax (vv. 24–27). (1) Jesus is the Son of the God whose house the temple is, so he is exempt from a tax paid to his own Father's house (compare Mal 3:1, the Lord coming to his temple); he pays only to avoid giving offence, and the scene looks ahead to the end of the temple order. A dispensational reading takes it this way, with humility as the lesson. (2) An acted parable of ransom: the half-shekel was "a ransom for a life" (Exod 30:12), and the free Son pays what he does not owe, for himself and for Peter, as he will give his life as a ransom for many (20:28). (3) A pastoral word to the sons of the kingdom: free though they are, they are not to make their freedom a trip-up to others. The readings need not exclude one another.
    10. The fish (v. 27). Matthew never says that Peter went or that the coin was found; the command is the end of the scene. Peter was called from casting nets (4:18–19), and here he is told to throw a single hook. Some hear the authority over "the fish of the sea" given to humankind (Gen 1:26) and to the "son of man" of Psalm 8 (Ps 8:4–8), a psalm Matthew quotes at 21:16; this study notes it as possible, no more.

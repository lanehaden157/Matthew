Rejection, a Beheading, Bread, and the Sea

Unit 14 · Matthew 13:54–14:36 · Nazareth stumbles, Herod feasts, Jesus feeds, and the sea is walked

Recurring roots — colour key

    “From where?” — a bracket around the household · 13:54–56

    The town asks the same question twice, pothen toutō, “from where, to this man?”, and answers it in between with his family. The reader already has the other answer: “from the Holy Spirit” (1:18, 20).

    Two tables — Herod’s feast and the wilderness meal · 14:6–21

    A diptych, not a chiasm: two meals set side by side, sharing their verbs. The reclining verbs differ in Greek (synanakeimai, “lie at table with”; anaklinō, “lean back”), so the pairing rests on scene and on the words that really do repeat.

    Five responses to him · 13:54–14:36

    Not a ring, just the order of the unit: each scene is a different answer to who he is.

    Nazareth tripped up→ Herod’s hall fear→ the wilderness fed→ the sea worship→ Gennesaret touch

    A prophet in his own hometown · 13:54–58

    54And having come into his hometown, he was teaching them in their gathering-place, so that they were struck out of themselves and were saying, “From where does this man get this wisdom and the acts of power?

    “Struck out of themselves” is ekplēssomai, the crowds’ astonishment at the Sermon (7:28); here it curdles into offense. Pothen, “from where?”, asks for his source.

    55Isn’t this the builder’s son? Isn’t his mother called Mary, and his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?1

    Tektōn is a craftsman in wood or stone, a builder, not only a carpenter. Mark has Jesus himself as “the builder”; Matthew moves the trade to the father.2

    56And his sisters — aren’t they all here with us? From where, then, does this man get all these things?”

    57And they kept being tripped up in him. But Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor except in his hometown and in his own house.”3

    Skandalizō, “trip up” (from skandalon, the trigger-stick of a trap), in a form of ongoing action: the tripping went on. Atimos, “without honor,” is literally “without worth.”
    Jer 11:21; 12:6 — Jeremiah’s hometown, Anathoth, and his own father’s house turn on the prophet; and Mic 7:6 as Jesus applied it at 10:36, “a person’s enemies are the members of his own household.”

    58And he did not do many acts of power there, because of their not-trusting.4

    Apistia is the negative of pistis, “trust”: not a failed opinion but a withheld reliance.

    Mark 6:5–6 — “could not” becomes “did not”

    Mark 6:5–6“And he could not do there any act of power, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them. And he marveled because of their not-trusting.”
    Matt 13:58“And he did not do many acts of power there, because of their not-trusting.”

    Matthew turns inability into restraint and drops Jesus’ own amazement, but keeps the cause. The limit is placed on Nazareth’s side of the exchange, not on his power. An old patristic reading adds that he held back so as not to deepen their condemnation.

    Herod hears, and a flashback · 14:1–12

    1At that season Herod the quarter-ruler heard the report about Jesus,

    Tetraarchēs, “ruler of a quarter” of a divided kingdom: Herod Antipas, son of the Herod who hunted the child in chapter 2. He literally “heard the hearing.”

    2and he said to his servants, “This is John the Immerser. He has been raised from the dead, and that is why the acts of power are at work in him.”

    “Has been raised” is passive, the form that usually implies God as the one acting. The gospel’s first “raised from the dead” comes from the man who had John killed, and it names the wrong person.

    3For Herod, having seized John, bound him and put him away in prison because of Herodias, the wife of Philip his brother;5

    4for John had been saying to him, “It is not permitted for you to have her.”6

    “Had been saying” renders an ongoing past: John said it repeatedly. Exestin, “it is permitted,” is the legal-question word the Pharisees used against the learners at 12:2.
    Lev 18:16; 20:21 — the Torah’s ban on uncovering a brother’s wife; John applies it to the ruler himself.

    5And though he wanted to kill him, he feared the crowd, because they held him as a prophet.

    Mark 6:19–20 — who wanted John dead

    Mark 6:19–20“Herodias held it against him and wanted to kill him, and could not; for Herod feared John, knowing him a righteous and holy man, and kept him safe.”
    Matt 14:5“And though he [Herod] wanted to kill him, he feared the crowd.”

    Matthew moves the wish to kill onto Herod and swaps reverence for John into fear of the crowd. That fear is the same one the temple leaders will confess about John at 21:26, and about Jesus at 21:46.

    6But when Herod’s birthday feast came, the daughter of Herodias danced in the middle and pleased Herod,7

    7so that he declared with an oath to give her whatever she might ask.

    8And she, coached by her mother, says, “Give me, here, on a platter, the head of John the Immerser.”

    Probibazō, “coach, put forward,” is rare. In the Greek of Deut 6:7 it is the word for parents teaching their children the commands.

    9And the king, grieved, because of the oaths and those reclining at table with him, commanded it to be given,8

    Called “quarter-ruler” in 14:1, he is now “the king,” a title Antipas never held and later lost everything seeking. The gospel’s true king was sought by magi in 2:2. The ruler fears the crowd (14:5), then his guests: he is ruled by both.

    10And he sent and beheaded John in the prison.

    11And his head was brought on a platter and given to the little girl, and she brought it to her mother.

    Korasion, “little girl,” appears elsewhere in Matthew only for Jairus’s daughter (9:24–25), the girl who was raised.

    12And his learners came and took up the corpse and buried him, and they went and reported to Jesus.

    1 Kgs 19:1–2; 21 — Jezebel’s threat against Elijah under Ahab. Jesus has already named John as Elijah (11:14), and will say of him, “they did to him whatever they wished; so also the Son of Man” (17:12).

    Bread in the wilderness · 14:13–21

    13And Jesus, having heard, withdrew from there in a boat to a wilderness place privately; and the crowds, having heard, followed him on foot from the towns.9

    Two hearings frame the chapter: Herod heard and feared (14:1); Jesus hears and withdraws. Anachōreō, “withdraw,” is Matthew’s standing verb for a tactical retreat (2:12–22, 4:12, 12:15).

    14And coming out, he saw a large crowd, and he was wrenched in the gut over them, and he healed their sick.

    Splanchnizomai, from splanchna, the inward organs: compassion felt as a blow to the gut (as at 9:36).

    15When evening came, the learners came to him saying, “The place is wilderness, and the hour has already gone by. Send the crowds away, so that they can go into the villages and buy themselves food.”

    16But Jesus said to them, “They have no need to go away. Y’all yourselves give them something to eat.”

    “Y’all yourselves” renders an emphatic hymeis: the learners’ own hands are named before the loaves are.
    2 Kgs 4:42–44 — Elisha says “give to the people and let them eat”; the servant protests that twenty loaves won’t feed a hundred; they eat and have some left over.

    17They say to him, “We have nothing here but five loaves and two fish.”

    18And he said, “Bring them here to me.”

    19And commanding the crowds to lean back on the grass, taking the five loaves and the two fish, looking up into the sky, he spoke the blessing, and breaking the loaves he gave them to the learners, and the learners to the crowds.10

    Took, spoke the blessing, broke, gave: the four actions of the Last Supper, in the same order.
    Matt 26:26 — “taking bread and speaking the blessing he broke it and, giving it to the learners, said…”; the wilderness meal already has the shape of the last one.

    20And they all ate and were fed full, and they took up the surplus of the broken pieces: twelve baskets full.11

    Chortazō, “fed full,” is built on chortos, the grass they are sitting on: they are pastured. “Took up” is the same verb as the corpse in 14:12.

    21And those eating were men, about five thousand, apart from women and children.

    Walking on the sea · 14:22–33

    22And at once he compelled the learners to get into the boat and go ahead of him to the other side, until he should send the crowds away.

    23And having sent the crowds away, he went up into the mountain privately to pray. When evening came, he was there alone.

    24But the boat was already many stadia away from the land, tormented by the waves, for the wind was against it.

    A stadion is about 185 meters. “Tormented” is basanizō, the word for the paralyzed servant (8:6) and the demons’ dread (8:29).

    25In the fourth watch of the night he came toward them, walking on the sea.

    The fourth watch is the last stretch before dawn, roughly 3 to 6 a.m. Phylakē, “watch,” is the same word as John’s “prison” in 14:3 and 14:10.
    Ps 77:19; Isa 43:16; 51:10 — God’s path through the sea, a way in the mighty waters, the deep made a road for the redeemed.

    26But the learners, seeing him walking on the sea, were thrown into turmoil, saying, “It’s an apparition!” and from fear they cried out.

    27But at once Jesus spoke to them, saying, “Take heart: I am. Do not fear.”12

    Greek (translit.)Tharseite, egō eimi; mē phobeisthe.
    NASB“Take courage, it is I; do not be afraid.”
    This study“Take heart: I am. Do not fear.” — the bare words, so that both hearings stay open: “it’s me,” and the “I am he” of Isaiah.
    ParallelsMark 6:50, the same three words; John 6:20, “I am; do not fear.”
    Isa 43:1–16; 51:10–12 — in the Greek Isaiah, God says “do not fear,” promises to be with Israel through the waters, makes a way in the sea, and declares egō eimi, “I am he.”

    28Peter answered him and said, “Master, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters.”

    The “if” grants the point rather than doubting it: “since it is you.” Peter asks the one who commanded the crowds (14:19) for a command of his own.

    29And he said, “Come.” And Peter, going down from the boat, walked on the waters and came toward Jesus.

    30But seeing the wind, strong, he was afraid, and beginning to be swallowed by the deep, he cried out, saying, “Master, save me!”13

    Ps 69:1–2 (68:2–3 in the Greek) — “Save me, O God, for the waters have come in, up to my life; I have sunk in the deep.”

    31And at once Jesus, stretching out his hand, took hold of him and says to him, “Little-faith, why did you waver?”14

    Oligopiste, a single word of address, “little-trust one”: the nickname of 6:30 and 8:26, now for one learner only.

    32And when they had gone up into the boat, the wind wore itself out.

    Kopazō, “grow weary, drop”: the wind is not rebuked this time, it simply gives out.

    33And those in the boat worshiped him, saying, “Truly you are God’s Son.”

    Matt 27:54 — the centurion at the cross, “Truly this was God’s Son”; the mockers’ quotation at 27:43, “I am God’s Son.” The same confession, in the same form, from the boat and from the execution squad.

    Mark 6:51–52 — hardened hearts, or worship

    Mark 6:51–52“And they were utterly beside themselves, for they had not understood about the loaves; their heart was hardened.”
    Matt 14:33“And those in the boat worshiped him, saying, ‘Truly you are God’s Son.’”

    Where Mark ends in incomprehension, Matthew ends in worship and confession. It answers the question of the first boat (8:27). Whether this is already the full confession of 16:16, or a real but unripe one, is held open: a dispensational reading stresses the immaturity; others hear the gospel’s high Christology arriving early.

    Gennesaret: the fringe of his cloak · 14:34–36

    34And having crossed over, they came onto land at Gennesaret.

    35And the men of that place, recognizing him, sent into all that surrounding region and brought to him all who were in a bad way,

    36and they kept urging him that they might only touch the fringe of his cloak; and as many as touched were brought safely through.15

    Kraspedon, the tassel of Num 15:38–39. Diesōthēsan, “brought safely through,” is the compound of sōzō, the word Peter cried at 14:30. His hometown would not trust him (13:58); this district asks only to touch him.
    Zech 8:23 — ten men from the nations take hold of the fringe of a Jew’s cloak, saying “let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you.”

    Notes

    1. The brothers and sisters (vv. 55–56). Four brothers are named and sisters unnamed. Three ancient readings: children of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus; children of Joseph by an earlier marriage; or cousins, a reading that points to “Mary the mother of James and Joseph” at 27:56. The text of Matthew does not settle it. James later leads the Jerusalem assembly (Gal 1:19), and the brothers appear not to have trusted him before the resurrection (John 7:5). The same family stood outside at 12:46–50. Compare Mark 6:3 ↗.
    2. “The builder’s son” (v. 55). Tektōn covers artisans and builders in wood and stone ↗. A proposal from modern Jewish scholarship reads “builder or builder’s son” as a Talmudic idiom for a learned expert who can solve a hard problem; the idiom is real, but its bearing on this verse is contested and nothing here depends on it.
    3. Homeland and house (v. 57). Matthew keeps Mark’s “hometown” and “house” but drops “among his relatives.” The rejected prophet at home is a type-scene (Jeremiah at Anathoth), and Ps 69:8 voices the same estrangement: “a stranger to my brothers, a foreigner to my mother’s sons.” It is the psalm Peter’s cry echoes at 14:30.
    4. Not-trusting (v. 58). Apistia is tagged with the pistis thread as its negated form. A dispensational reading takes the scene as a step in Jesus’ withdrawal from an unbelieving nation toward training his learners; inaugurated-kingdom readings hear the kingdom regathering Israel around him rather than turning from it. Both read the same sequence; they disagree about what it means for Israel.
    5. Josephus on John (vv. 3–5). Josephus (Antiquities 18.116–119) confirms that Antipas held John at the fortress of Machaerus and put him to death, but gives a political motive: Herod feared John’s sway over the crowds might turn to revolt. The Gospels give a moral one. Many historians think both were in play. ↗
    6. Why the marriage was barred (v. 4). The Torah bans taking a brother’s wife (Lev 18:16; 20:21), with the levirate exception when the brother died childless (Deut 25:5). A patristic reading points out that Herodias already had a child by Herod’s brother, so the exception could not apply. ↗
    7. A ruler’s birthday feast (v. 6). Scripture’s only other royal birthday feast is Pharaoh’s (Gen 40:20), where the fates of two prisoners are settled, one restored and one hanged. The Greek word for the feast here, genesia, is closely related to the one used there.
    8. Banquet and decree (v. 9). 3 Macc 5 is a Second Temple type-scene: a king drinking with the companions reclining beside him orders a slaughter of Jews. Matthew’s scene shares the shape (feast, companions, a death sentence at table) but not the vocabulary beyond the word for reclining.
    9. Withdrawal (v. 13). Moses’ flight from Pharaoh (Exod 2:15) uses the same verb in the Greek: a deliverer withdraws from a killing king. Matthew has used it already for the magi and Joseph fleeing Herod the Great (2:12–22).
    10. “Spoke the blessing” (v. 19). Eulogeō ↗, “speak well of”: the Jewish blessing said over bread, praising God as its giver. Rendered “spoke the blessing” rather than the flattened “blessed” (which also blurs it with makarios, the beatitude word). This is a new rendering choice, flagged for Lane.
    11. Fed full on the grass (v. 20). Chortazō ↗ began as a farming word, “feed with fodder,” from chortos, “grass, pasture.” It is the promise of Ps 132:15 (the Greek has the same verb), “her poor I will fill with bread,” and the beatitude’s “they shall be fed full” (5:6). A dispensational reading declines to read symbolism into the numbers (five, two, twelve); others hear the twelve baskets as Israel’s twelve tribes or the Twelve. The text leaves it unsaid.
    12. Egō eimi, “I am” (v. 27). The words are ordinary self-identification, “it’s me,” and in the dark that is enough. But the Greek Isaiah uses egō eimi for God’s own self-declaration, “I am he” (Isa 41:4; 43:10; 43:25; 46:4; 51:12; cf. Deut 32:39) ↗, and Isa 43 and 51 set it beside the sea and “do not fear,” the same cluster found here. Exodus 3:14 in the Greek reads differently (“I am the One Who Is,” ho ōn), so the tie is to Isaiah rather than the burning bush. Matthew’s own usage is telling. He keeps a bare egō eimi here but removes the one in Mark’s trial scene (26:64, where Mark 14:62 has it). He also drops Mark’s note that Jesus “meant to pass them by,” which recalls God passing by Moses and Elijah. What he adds instead is worship. Three readings stand, and they are not exclusive: plain self-identification; the presence promise of Emmanuel, “God with us” (1:23), which Jesus repeats in his own mouth at 18:20 and 28:20; and a claim to the divine “I am he.” The strongest scholarly case for the last is built mainly on John; Matthew leaves it for the reader to hear.
    13. “Swallowed by the deep” (v. 30). Katapontizō ↗, “be sunk in the open sea,” occurs in the New Testament only here and at 18:6, where the fate for whoever trips up one of the little ones is to be sunk in the deep of the sea.
    14. “Waver” (v. 31). Distazō ↗ is built on dis, “twice, in two”: to hesitate between two ways, not to disbelieve outright. Its only other New Testament use is 28:17, where the learners worship and some waver, the same pairing as this scene. “Master” renders kyrios as an address, as elsewhere in this study.
    15. “Brought safely through” (v. 36). Diesōthēsan is a compound of sōzō, “save,” and is tagged with that thread, as a compound sharing the stem. The fringe links this summary to the woman of 9:20–22, who touched it and was “saved,” and away from the leaders’ enlarged tassels at 23:5.

The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

Leaving Galilee: Marriage, Children, Riches

ap’ archēs de ou gegonen houtōs — but from the beginning it has not come to be so
Unit 19 · Matthew 19:1–30 · the last Galilean exit, and what following costs

    Leaving Galilee · 19:1–2

    1 And it came to be, when Jesus finished these words, he moved on from Galilee and came into the regions of Judea beyond the Jordan.

    The last Galilean exit: from here the road runs to Jerusalem. Moved on (metairō); beyond the Jordan (peran tou Iordanou), east of the river.
    Matt 7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 26:1 — the fourth of the five times Matthew closes a block of teaching with “when Jesus finished these words.” Here it also closes Galilee. The departure verb (metairō) is the one used at 13:53 and nowhere else in the book.
    Matt 4:15; 4:25; Deut 1:1 — the phrase that opened the ministry, in Isaiah’s quotation and in the crowd list, returns as the route to Judea. Tentative: the link to Moses’ last speeches “beyond the Jordan” is thin, because the phrase is also plain geography.

    2 And many crowds followed him, and he healed them there.

    Where Mark has him teach the crowds here, Matthew has him heal them (therapeuō, “heal”).

    The test on sending away · 19:3–9

    3 And Pharisees came to him, testing him and saying, “Is it permitted for a person to send away his wife for every cause?”1

    Send away (apolyō): the ordinary word for dismissing someone is the word for divorce. Cause (aitia); person (anthrōpos), the generic word.

    Mark 10:2–9 — for every cause

    Mark 10:2Mark’s Pharisees ask whether it is permitted for a man to send away a wife; they test him without the extra phrase. Jesus’ Genesis line comes without being put in God’s mouth.
    SameThe test, the question about divorce, Genesis 1 and 2 as the answer.
    DifferentMatthew adds “for every cause,” which drops the question into the fight between the schools of Hillel and Shammai. He also has the Creator speak the line about leaving father and mother.

    The Pharisees ask Jesus to choose a school; he answers from creation and declines both.

    4 And he answered and said, “Have y’all not read that the one who created them from the beginning made them male and female, (Gen 1:27; 5:2)

    Created … made: two verbs, ktizō (“create”) and poieō (“make”), as Genesis has both.
    Matt 12:3; 12:5; 21:16; 21:42; 22:31 — Jesus asks “have y’all not read?” six times: three times to Pharisees (here the third), twice to the chief priests in the temple (“never read”), once to Sadducees. He asks the readers of Scripture whether they have read it.

    5 and said, ‘On account of this a person will leave behind the father and the mother and will be glued to his wife, and the two will become one flesh’? (Gen 2:24)

    In Genesis this line is the narrator’s comment; Jesus makes God the speaker. Glued (kollaomai), from kolla, “glue”; Hebrew davaq, “cling.”

    6 So they are no longer two but one flesh. What then God joined together, let a person not separate.”

    Joined together (syzeugnymi), literally “yoke together”; separate (chōrizō).
    Matt 11:29–30; Ezek 1:11; 1:23 — the verb behind “joined together” is built on the yoke word, and may sound the yoke of the rest invitation (zygos). Tentative: Matthew gives no signal, and the two words are cognate, not identical. In the Greek Old Testament the verb is found only in Ezekiel’s vision.

    7 They say to him, “Why then did Moses command to give a document of divorce and to send her away?” (Deut 24:1)

    Document of divorce (biblion apostasiou): the law’s phrase for the Hebrew sefer keritut, “document of cutting off.”

    Mark 10:3–5 — who says “command”

    Mark 10:3–4Jesus asks what Moses commanded, and they answer that Moses permitted it.
    SameMoses, the document of divorce, hardness of heart.
    DifferentMatthew reverses the roles. Nobody asks them: the Pharisees say “command,” and Jesus replies that Moses allowed it.

    The trap fails and Moses is defended: Deuteronomy allowed divorce, it did not command it.

    8 He says to them, “Moses, toward y’all’s heart-hardness, allowed y’all to send away y’all’s wives; but from the beginning it has not come to be so.

    Y’all’s: the hard heart is plural, the people’s. Allowed (epitrepō) answers their command.
    Gen 1:27; 2:24; Deut 24:1 — Jesus ranks the earlier story above the later concession, Genesis over Deuteronomy. It is the same move as “y’all heard that it was said … but I say” in the Sermon.
    Deut 10:16; Jer 4:4; Sir 16:10 — the rare word for heart-hardness (sklērokardia) stands in the Greek of the command to circumcise the heart (Hebrew of Deut 10:16: “circumcise the foreskin of your hearts and stiffen your neck no more”). The Torah that carries the divorce allowance also names the heart as needing surgery. Tentative until the Greek verses are read.

    9 And I say to y’all that whoever sends away his wife, not for sexual immorality, and marries another, is guilty of adultery; and the one who marries a sent-away woman is guilty of adultery.”234

    Sexual immorality (porneia); is guilty of adultery (moichaomai). The last clause is uncertain in the manuscripts.
    Greek (translit.)hos an apolysē tēn gynaika autou mē epi porneia kai gamēsē allēn moichatai
    This translation“whoever sends away his wife, not for sexual immorality, and marries another, is guilty of adultery”
    NASB“whoever divorces his wife, except for immorality, and marries another woman commits adultery”
    NoteWhat porneia covers is contested. Matthew lists it beside adultery at 15:19, so it is not simply adultery. At 5:32 the exception is worded differently: parektos logou porneias, “apart from a matter of porneia.”

    Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18 — the exception clause

    Mark 10:11–12The saying with no exception; Mark adds that a woman who sends away her husband and marries another commits adultery.
    Luke 16:18The saying with no exception.
    SameDivorce and remarriage is adultery.
    DifferentMatthew has the exception (mē epi porneia here; parektos logou porneias at 5:32, in different words), and no woman-initiated case.

    Matthew places Jesus inside the Jewish debate on grounds: closer to Shammai on grounds, stricter than either school on remarriage. Readers divide on where the clause came from and on what porneia covers; both stay open.

    The divorce law turned on Israel Deut 24:1–4; Jer 3:1–8; Isa 50:1

    Deuteronomy allows a man to send a wife away with a document and forbids him to take her back after she has married another. Jeremiah turns that law on Israel: you have played the prostitute with many lovers, and would you return to me? He says God sent her away and gave her a document of divorce. Isaiah asks where the mother’s document of divorce is.

    • A marriage, a document, a sending away, adultery.
    • In the Greek of Jer 3:8, the phrase for the document and the adultery verb stand in one verse.

    Jeremiah and Isaiah make God himself the one who sends away; Jesus makes the law a concession and points behind Moses to Genesis.

    “What God joined together” sounds against a covenant story in which exile is divorce and God calls Israel back. Tentative: Matthew never cites either prophet, and the link is rarely drawn.

    Joseph’s quiet divorce Matt 1:18–25

    Joseph, a righteous man, finds Mary with child before they come together and plans to send her away quietly. An angel stops him in a dream, and he takes her.

    • The same verb, apolyō, “send away.”
    • A marriage at stake, apparent sexual wrong, and a righteous man’s right to divorce.

    Joseph’s divorce never happens; Jesus’ saying names grounds in general.

    It shows one view of the exception (sexual unfaithfulness during betrothal) from inside Matthew’s own first story. Tentative: Matthew does not signal it, and that reading is one of several.

    Mal 2:14–16 — Malachi calls the wife “your companion and the wife of your covenant” and speaks of “one,” as Genesis speaks of one flesh. Tentative: Matthew never cites it, and the Hebrew of 2:16 is disputed.

    Room for the word · 19:10–12

    10 The learners say to him, “If such is the cause of the person with the woman, it is not worth marrying.”

    The Pharisees’ word returns: cause now names the husband’s whole case with his wife.

    11 But he said to them, “Not all make room for this word, but those to whom it has been given.5

    Make room for (chōreō). Has been given: given and stays given; the giver goes unnamed, and most readers hear God.

    12 For there are castrated men who were born so from a mother’s belly, and there are castrated men who were castrated by people, and there are castrated men who castrated themselves for the sake of the kingdom of the skies. The one able to make room, let him make room.”

    Three kinds (eunouchos, “eunuch”): born so; had it done to them; did it themselves.
    Greek (translit.)eisin eunouchoi … eunouchisthēsan hypo tōn anthrōpōn … eunouchisan heautous dia tēn basileian tōn ouranōn
    This translation“castrated men who were castrated by people … castrated men who castrated themselves for the sake of the kingdom of the skies”
    NASB“eunuchs who were made eunuchs by men; and there are also eunuchs who made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven”
    Note“Eunuch” is the Greek word borrowed into English; this translation says plainly what it means. The first two kinds had it done to them; the third did it themselves, which most read as a figure for staying unmarried for the kingdom, not a literal act.

    Isaiah 56 and the eunuch Isa 56:3–5; Deut 23:1–2; Lev 22:24

    Deuteronomy barred the man with crushed or cut-off parts from the assembly of Yahweh, and Leviticus barred such animals from sacrifice. Isaiah promises the eunuch (Hebrew saris) who keeps the Sabbaths a place “in my house and within my walls,” a monument and a name better than sons and daughters, an everlasting name that will not be cut off.

    • The castrated man stands outside the family line, counted as cut off.
    • Jesus’ third kind “castrated themselves for the sake of the kingdom of the skies.”

    Isaiah gives a name and a place in the temple; Matthew’s castrated man is a learner who gets the kingdom, and the saying has no temple language.

    The saying is no insult: the unmarried and childless are the kingdom’s family, as Isaiah brought them into the covenant house.

    The Ethiopian eunuch of Acts 8; Isa 56:7 returns at Matt 21:13.

    The children · 19:13–15

    13 Then children were brought to him so that he would lay his hands on them and make a petition; but the learners sharply charged them.

    Make a petition (proseuchomai, “pray”); sharply charged (epitimaō), the verb Jesus used on the wind and sea.

    Mark 10:13–16; Luke 18:15–17 — let the children come

    Mark 10:14–16Jesus is indignant, takes the children in his arms, and says whoever does not receive the kingdom like a child will not enter it.
    SameThe children are brought, the learners charge them sharply, Jesus says to let them come.
    DifferentMatthew drops the indignation and the embrace, has the children brought for a petition, has Jesus lay hands on them and go on, and drops the “receive like a child” line (he gave it already at 18:3).

    The learners repeat the very fault chapter 18 warned about. The point is “such as these”: the kingdom of the skies belongs to people like children.

    Gen 48:14; Num 27:18 — laying on hands in blessing runs back to Jacob blessing the younger over the older and to Moses commissioning Joshua. Tentative: a pattern, not a quotation.

    14 But Jesus said, “Let the children alone, and do not hinder them from coming to me; for of such as these is the kingdom of the skies.”

    Let is aphiēmi, the verb that releases sins and leaves nets.

    15 And having laid his hands on them, he went on from there.

    The young man and the good · 19:16–22

    16 And look, one came up to him and said, “Teacher, what good thing shall I do so that I may have age-long life?”

    Good (agathos); age-long life (zōē aiōnios). Many manuscripts read “Good Teacher,” as Mark and Luke do.

    Mark 10:17–18; Luke 18:18–19 — the good

    Mark 10:17–18The man says “good teacher”; Jesus asks why he calls him good, since no one is good but God.
    SameThe question about age-long life; the answer pointing to God alone.
    DifferentMatthew moves “good” from Jesus to the deed: the man asks about a good thing, and Jesus asks why he asks about the good (“One there is who is the good”).

    It spares Jesus the awkwardness the other accounts leave open and turns the question toward God’s standard of goodness. The landowner returns to “good” at 20:15.

    17 And he said to him, “Why do you ask me about the good? One there is who is the good. But if you want to come into life, keep the commandments.”

    Many manuscripts read “Why do you call me good? No one is good but one, God,” as in Mark and Luke.
    Lev 18:5; Deut 30:15–20; Dan 12:2 — “come into life” and “age-long life” carry the Old Testament’s life language: the one who does the commandments lives; life and death set before Israel; some awake to age-long life. Tentative: only Dan 12:2 has been read in the Hebrew.

    18 He says to him, “Which?” And Jesus said, “You shall not murder; you shall not commit adultery; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false witness; (Exod 20:13–16; Deut 5:17–20)

    19 honor the father and the mother, (Exod 20:12; Deut 5:16) and you shall love your neighbor as yourself.” (Lev 19:18)

    Only Matthew has “Which?”; honoring parents comes after the others, and neighbor-love is added, the line that returns as the second great commandment (22:39).

    20 The young man says to him, “All these I have kept; what do I still lack?”

    Young man (neaniskos): Matthew uses the word only here and at 19:22.

    Mark 10:20–21; Luke 18:21–22 — what do I still lack

    Mark 10:20–21The man says he has kept all these from his youth, and Jesus, looking at him, loves him.
    SameSell, give to the poor, treasure in the skies, follow.
    DifferentMatthew drops the look and the love and makes him a young man; he adds “if you want to be whole.” The question of what he lacks sits on the man’s own lips.

    The Sermon’s command to be whole (5:48) gets its test case, and the man’s wanting is on the table.

    21 Jesus said to him, “If you want to be whole, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in the skies; and come here, follow me.”

    Whole (teleios), as at 5:48; possessions (ta hyparchonta); come here (deuro), a summons to one person.
    Matt 13:44; 4:19; 9:9 — “go, sell” are the treasure-finder’s verbs, who goes and sells all he has from his joy; this man lacks the finder’s joy. “Come here, follow me” joins the fishermen’s call (“come here after me”) and the tax collector’s (“follow me”).

    22 But the young man, hearing the word, went away grieving; for he was one having many properties.6

    Properties (ktēma), a different word from the possessions of 19:21.

    Riches and the impossible · 19:23–26

    23 And Jesus said to his learners, “Amen I say to y’all that a rich man will come into the kingdom of the skies with difficulty.

    24 Again I say to y’all, it is easier for a camel to come in through the hole of a sewing needle than for a rich man to come into the kingdom of God.”7

    Kingdom of God: one of Matthew’s four uses, and the same kingdom as “of the skies” one line before.
    Matt 23:24; 27:57; 13:22 — the camel returns at 23:24, swallowed by scrupulous stage-actors. The only other rich man in Matthew is Joseph of Arimathea, who himself became a learner of Jesus; the deceit of riches at 13:22 chokes the word. Tentative: word-level links, with no signal from Matthew.

    25 And hearing it, the learners were knocked clean out of themselves, exceedingly, saying, “Who then is able to be saved?”

    26 And looking at them, Jesus said to them, “With people this is impossible, but with God all things are possible.”8

    Genesis 18 and “possible with God” Gen 18:10–14

    Sarah laughs at the promise of a son in her old age, and Yahweh answers, “Is anything too wonderful for Yahweh?” (Hebrew hayippaleʾ, “is it too wonderful”).

    • An impossible thing, and a word that God can do it.
    • Luke has Gabriel give Mary the same line.

    Matthew puts the saying in Jesus’ mouth and ties it to being saved, not to a birth.

    The kingdom is a gift and not the product of the man’s effort. Tentative: Matthew never cites Genesis 18.

    Matt 26:39 — “all things are possible” with God returns in Gethsemane as “if it is possible, let this cup pass.” Tentative: a word link only.

    Thrones and a hundredfold · 19:27–30

    27 Then Peter answered and said to him, “Look, we left all things and followed you; what then will there be for us?”

    A wage question: it sets up the vineyard parable’s pay-line at 20:8.

    Mark 10:28; Luke 18:28 — Peter’s question

    Mark 10:28Peter says they have left everything and followed; in Luke, “we have left our own.”
    SameThe claim of having left everything.
    DifferentOnly Matthew adds “what then will there be for us?”

    The addition turns the claim into a wage question, which lets Jesus answer with thrones and sets up the wages of the vineyard.

    28 And Jesus said to them, “Amen I say to y’all that y’all who followed me, in the new genesis, when the Son of Man sits on the throne of his glory, y’all too will sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.910

    New genesis (palingenesia, “genesis again”): Matthew’s one use, sounding the book’s first line, “the book of the genesis.”
    Greek (translit.)hymeis hoi akolouthēsantes moi en tē palingenesia, hotan kathisē ho huios tou anthrōpou epi thronou doxēs autou
    This translation“y’all who followed me, in the new genesis, when the Son of Man sits on the throne of his glory”
    NASB“you who have followed Me, in the regeneration when the Son of Man will sit …”
    Note“Regeneration” and “renewal” are the usual words; “new genesis” keeps the link to 1:1. The readings differ on when the new genesis comes: a future earthly kingdom, or a new age begun at the resurrection and finished at the end.

    Luke 22:28–30 — thrones after the rich man

    Luke 22:28–30At the Last Supper, after a dispute about greatness, Jesus promises those who stayed with him in his trials that they will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes.
    SameThrones, judging the twelve tribes.
    DifferentMatthew sets the promise on the road, right after the rich man, and ties it to the Son of Man on the throne of his glory “in the new genesis.”

    The promise stands against the man’s refusal: the Twelve, who left their nets, receive Daniel’s reward.

    Daniel 7 and the thrones Dan 7:9–27

    Daniel sees thrones set and an Ancient of Days take his seat. One like a son of man receives dominion. The holy ones of the Most High receive the kingdom, and judgment is given to them.

    • Thrones.
    • A Son of Man on a throne.
    • Judgment given to others.

    In Daniel the holy ones are Israel as a people; in Matthew the Twelve sit over the twelve tribes, and he does not call them holy ones.

    The Twelve, who left their nets, receive Daniel’s reward, and the promise answers the rich man’s refusal.

    Matt 25:31, the Son of Man on the throne of his glory; 26:64.

    Isa 65:17; 66:22 — behind the new genesis stand Isaiah’s new heavens and new earth (Hebrew boreʾ shamayim ḥadashim va-ʾareṣ ḥadashah, “creating new heavens and a new earth”).

    29 And everyone who left houses or brothers or sisters or father or mother or wife or children or fields on account of my name will receive a hundredfold and will inherit age-long life.11

    On account of (heneken), the same word that opened the Genesis line at 19:5: leaving for a wife, and leaving for the name.

    Mark 10:29–30; Luke 18:29–30 — a hundredfold, and not “now”

    Mark 10:30Mark promises a hundredfold “now in this time, with persecutions,” and age-long life in the age to come.
    SameLeaving houses and family brings a hundredfold and age-long life.
    DifferentMatthew drops “now” and “with persecutions” and says “on account of my name.” His list includes “wife” (uncertain in the manuscripts), which Mark lacks.

    The reward reads as future, and the leaving is for Jesus’ name. A dispensational reading takes the hundredfold as many more people who are like family, not literal houses.

    2 Sam 24:3; 1 Chr 21:3 — the rare word for “hundredfold” (hekatontaplasiōn) is the Greek of Joab’s wish that Yahweh add to the people a hundred times over. Tentative: only a shared rare word, and Joab’s wish comes as a warning against a census.
    Matt 19:5; 19:12; 19:19 — father and mother run four times through the unit: left behind for a wife, the mother’s belly, honored, and left for Jesus’ name. The unit sets them side by side and does not rank them. Tentative: a pattern in the unit, not an echo Matthew signals.

    30 But many first will be last, and last first.

    Mark 10:31; Luke 13:30 — first and last

    Mark 10:31The same saying, “But many …”.
    Luke 13:30Luke has “some,” and uses it of those shut outside the door.
    SameFirst and last reversed.
    DifferentMatthew repeats the saying at the close of the vineyard parable with the order turned (“the last first, the first last,” 20:16), and the parable’s pay-line begins “from the last to the first” (20:8).

    It frames the arc from the man’s question to the wage (19:16–20:16). The frame is real; no verbal chiasm stands inside it.

    Notes

    1. for every cause (v. 3). The question drops into a live debate over Deuteronomy’s phrase for the ground of divorce (Hebrew ʿerwat davar, “a matter of nakedness,” Deut 24:1). The school of Shammai read it narrowly, as sexual wrong; the school of Hillel widely, as any cause. At Qumran, divorce seems to have been allowed not at all. Jesus answers from Genesis and sides with neither school.
    2. the one who marries a sent-away woman (v. 9). The closing clause is uncertain: the Greek text marks it as a variant point, and manuscripts differ on whether it belongs. The manuscript evidence itself has not been weighed here.
    3. not for sexual immorality (v. 9). Two phrasings of one exception: at 5:32 parektos logou porneias, “apart from a matter of porneia”; here mē epi porneia, “not for porneia.” Mark and Luke have no exception at all.
    4. sexual immorality (v. 9). What porneia covers. Matthew lists it beside adultery at 15:19, so it is not simply adultery. The views held: sexual sin broadly; incest (marriage inside forbidden degrees); sexual unfaithfulness during betrothal, as Joseph suspected of Mary (1:19); no real exception at all; or permission to separate without remarriage. The translation’s “sexual immorality” keeps the word as wide as Matthew’s other uses.
    5. this word (v. 11). Whether “this word” is the learners’ conclusion (it is better not to marry) or the whole teaching on marriage stays open; both readings have long standing.
    6. went away (v. 22). The man “went away” (aperchomai); the learners “left” (aphiēmi, 19:27). One leaves Jesus; the others leave everything for him.
    7. the hole of a sewing needle (v. 24). The story of a “needle gate” in Jerusalem has no basis that has been found. The plain reading is a camel and a sewing needle (rhaphis, “needle”; trypēma, “hole”): an impossibility, which is where 19:26 takes it.
    8. possible (v. 26). A small cluster of ability words: dynamai, “be able” (19:12, 25), and dynatos / adynatos, “possible / impossible” (19:26). The one able to make room; who is able to be saved; with God all things are possible. Not tagged: dynamai is common across the book.
    9. in the new genesis (v. 28). The phrase stands between “who followed me” and the “when” clause, so it can attach to either: following in the new genesis, or sitting in it. Most attach it to the sitting.
    10. when and what (v. 28). One reading takes the new genesis as the future earthly kingdom, with the Twelve ruling Israel. Another takes it as the new age begun at the resurrection and finished at the end, with the Twelve leading a renewed Israel. Both stay open.
    11. or wife (v. 29). The Greek text marks “or wife” as a variant point, and Mark’s list has no wife. If it stands, the list reaches back to 19:5: a person leaves father and mother for a wife, and leaves even a wife for the name.

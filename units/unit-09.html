The paralytic forgiven · 9:1–8

1And getting into a boat, he crossed over and came into his own city.

Capernaum. Constable notes this healing likely happened, chronologically, before the Gadarene exorcism that closed Unit 8 — Matthew has moved it here for the argument he's building, not for the timeline.1

2And look — they were carrying to him a paralyzed man, thrown on a mat. And Jesus, seeing their trust, said to the paralyzed man: "Take heart, child — your sins are released."23

3And look — some of the scribes said among themselves: "This man blasphemes."

4And Jesus, knowing their thoughts, said: "Why are y'all thinking evil in y'all's hearts?

5For which is easier — to say 'Your sins are released,' or to say 'Get up and walk'?

6But so that y'all may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to release sins" — then he says to the paralyzed man: "Get up, pick up your mat, and go away to your house."4

7And getting up, he went away to his house.

Mark 2:1–12 — the roof goes, and the crowd's last line changes

Mark 2:3–4And they come bringing to him a paralytic, lifted by four. And not being able to bring him to him because of the crowd, they unroofed the roof where he was, and having dug it out they lower the pallet where the paralytic was lying down.
Mark 2:12And he was raised, and immediately taking up the pallet he went out in front of everyone, so that all were beside themselves and glorified God, saying: We never saw anything like this.

Matthew cuts the crowd, the four bearers and the famous hole in the roof, keeping only what the argument needs: faith seen, sins released, authority questioned, authority demonstrated. The cut costs him something, and he takes the cost — with the roof gone, "seeing their faith" has no visible referent at all, and the scene turns entirely on what Jesus perceives rather than on what a crowd watched. Then he changes the closing acclamation: where Mark's witnesses say they have never seen the like, Matthew's glorify God "who gave such authority to human beings," plural — a healing has become a statement about what has been handed to the community that will be told at 18:18 to bind and release.

The calling of Matthew · 9:9–13

9And as Jesus was passing on from there, he saw a man sitting at the toll booth, called Matthew, and says to him: "Follow me." And getting up, he followed him.

The same call type-scene as the fishermen (4:18–22) — see, command, immediate response — now landing on the most socially despised trade available.5

10And it happened, as he reclined at table in the house, look — many tax collectors and sinners came and reclined at table together with Jesus and his disciples.

11And the Pharisees, seeing it, were saying to his disciples: "Why does y'all's teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?"

12But he, hearing it, said: "Those who are strong have no need of a healer, but those who are sick do.

13But go and learn what this is: 'I want loyal-love and not sacrifice.' For I didn't come to call the upright, but sinners."6

Greek (translit.)eleos thelō kai ou thysian

Mark 2:15–17 · Luke 5:29–32 — Matthew alone wedges in Hosea 6:6

Mark 2:16And the scribes of the Pharisees, seeing that he was eating with the sinners and tax-collectors, were saying to his disciples: Why is he eating with the tax-collectors and sinners?
Mark 2:17Those who are strong have no need of a healer, but those who are badly off. I did not come to call righteous people but sinners. (Nothing between the two clauses.)
Luke 5:32I have not come to call righteous people but sinners to a turning-around.

Matthew's version is Mark's with one sentence driven into the middle of it: "But go and learn what this is — I want loyal-love (eleos) and not sacrifice," citing Hosea 6:6, where the Greek eleos stands in for Hebrew hesed, covenant loyalty rather than pity. "Go and learn" is a rabbinic study formula, so Matthew has Jesus answering scribes on their own ground and telling them to go do their homework. He repeats the same citation at 12:7, which makes Hosea 6:6 the shared hinge of the meal controversy and the Sabbath controversy, and the closest thing Matthew gives us to a stated hermeneutic for the law.

The question about fasting · 9:14–17

14Then John's disciples come to him, saying: "Why do we and the Pharisees fast often, but your disciples don't fast?"

15And Jesus said to them: "The sons of the wedding-hall can't mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them, can they? But days will come when the bridegroom is torn away from them, and then they will fast."

Bridegroom imagery reaching back to Hosea 2 and Isaiah 62 — and forward to John the Baptist's own line about Jesus in John 3:29, "the friend of the bridegroom." "Torn away" is the first, still-veiled hint of the cross, well before the "from that time" hinge opens the theme formally at 16:21.7

16And no one puts a patch of unshrunk cloth on a worn-out cloak — for its patch tears away from the cloak, and a worse tear happens.

17Nor do people pour new wine into worn-out skins — otherwise, the skins burst, and the wine spills out, and the skins are destroyed. But they pour new wine into fresh skins, and both are kept safe together.

Two different Greek words for "new" are doing separate work: neos ("new" in time — just arrived) and kainos ("new" in kind — a different sort of thing altogether). Constable's notes describe these as Aramaic, which looks like a slip — they're the Greek vocabulary of the text in front of us.8

The ruler's daughter and the bleeding woman · 9:18–26

18While he was saying these things to them, look — a certain ruler came and knelt low before him, saying: "My daughter has just died — but come, lay your hand on her, and she will live."

19And Jesus got up and began following him, and his disciples too.

20And look — a woman hemorrhaging for twelve years came up behind him and touched the fringe of his cloak.

Almost certainly one of the tzitzit commanded in Numbers 15:38–41. A connection is often drawn to Malachi 4:2's "healing in his wings" — kanaph, the same Hebrew word for both a garment's corner and a bird's wing — though it's worth holding as a suggestive echo rather than a certain one.9

21For she was saying to herself: "If I only touch his cloak, I will be saved."

22And Jesus, turning and seeing her, said: "Take heart, daughter — your trust has saved you." And the woman was saved from that hour.

23And Jesus, coming into the ruler's house, and seeing the flute-players and the crowd in commotion,

24was saying: "Y'all clear out — the little girl hasn't died, she's sleeping." And they were laughing at him.

25But when the crowd had been thrown out, he went in and took hold of her hand, and the little girl was raised.

26And this news went out into that whole land.

Mark 5:21–43 — in Mark she is dying, and the death arrives mid-story

Mark 5:22–23…one of the synagogue-rulers comes, Jairus by name, and seeing him falls toward his feet and urges him much, saying: My little daughter is at her last — come and lay your hands on her, so that she may be saved and live.
Mark 5:35–36…people come from the synagogue-ruler's house saying: Your daughter died. Why still trouble the teacher? But Jesus, overhearing the word being spoken, says to the synagogue-ruler: Do not fear, only trust.
Mark 5:41–43Talitha koum, which is translated: Little girl, I say to you, get up. And immediately the girl stood up and was walking about, for she was twelve years old… And he ordered them much that no one should know this, and said to give her something to eat.

Mark takes twenty-three verses and Matthew takes nine: gone are the name Jairus, the messengers who bring the death report, the three disciples, the Aramaic words, the girl's age, the food, and the command to silence. The decisive cut is the delay — Matthew's father says at the outset that she has just now died, so there is no interval in which hope has to survive bad news. Mark's story is about trust holding through an interruption; Matthew's is about a ruler who does obeisance (proskyneō, where Mark's man merely falls at his feet) and asks for a resurrection in his opening sentence.

Two blind men · 9:27–31

27And as Jesus was passing on from there, two blind men followed him, crying out and saying: "Have loyal-love on us, Son of David!"

The first time in the gospel anyone addresses Jesus directly by this title. It's going to keep recurring — 12:23, 15:22, 20:30–31, 21:9, 21:15 — always as a cry from someone desperate, always tied to healing.11

28And when he had come into the house, the blind men came up to him, and Jesus says to them: "Do y'all trust that I'm able to do this?" They say to him: "Yes, Lord."

29Then he touched their eyes, saying: "According to y'all's trust, let it be to y'all."

30And their eyes were opened. And Jesus snorted at them with strong feeling, saying: "See that no one knows."

The mute man who spoke · 9:32–34

32And as they were going out, look — they brought to him a mute man, possessed by a demon.

33And when the demon had been cast out, the mute man spoke. And the crowds were amazed, saying: "Never has it appeared like this in Israel."

34But the Pharisees were saying: "By the ruler of the demons he casts out demons."

The first appearance of the accusation that will get a name — Beelzeboul — and a full hearing at 12:24. The Pharisees don't dispute that something supernatural happened; they dispute its source.13

Notes

  1. Out of sequence (v. 1). Constable and others place this healing, chronologically, before the Gadarene exorcism that closed Unit 8 (Mark 5:21–22; Luke 8:40–41 have Jesus crossing back before this happens). Matthew has relocated it here for the argument he's building, not the timeline.
  2. "Thrown" (v. 2). Bebēmenon — perfect passive of ballō. The same verb, same tense, describes the centurion's servant at 8:6: "my servant has been thrown down, paralyzed." Two paralyzed sufferers, one verb.
  3. Grammar of release (v. 2). The present-tense form here functions less like an ongoing action and more like a formal pronouncement made once, on the spot — closer to an English "I hereby release you" than "I keep on releasing you."
  4. Son of Man (v. 6). Reaches back to Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" is given dominion and authority before the Ancient of Days. First met at 8:20 in its homeless sense; here it carries a claim to a divine prerogative instead. ↗
  5. Tax collectors (v. 9). Local agents of the Roman tax-farming system, socially equivalent in most eyes to Gentiles and thieves — their testimony wasn't even admissible in a Jewish court. The phrase "tax collectors and sinners" recurs at 11:19, 18:17, and 21:31–32.
  6. Hosea 6:6 (v. 13). Repeated verbatim at 12:7 against the same critics over the Sabbath. "Go and learn" is a real rabbinic idiom — "you need to go back and study this, because you've missed the point." ↗
  7. The bridegroom (v. 15). John the Baptist calls himself "the friend of the bridegroom" and names Jesus the bridegroom outright at John 3:29 — Jesus turns his own teacher's language back on John's disciples here.
  8. Neos vs. kainos (v. 17). Two distinct Greek words for "new" — recent-in-time and different-in-kind — doing separate work in the same saying. The messianic age is new both ways at once.
  9. The fringe (v. 20). Kraspedon, likely the LXX's word for the tzitzit of Numbers 15:38. The kanaph/"wings" resonance with Malachi 4:2 is a popular and plausible connection, not one this study found stated outright in France, Wright, or Davies-Allison.
  10. Sōzō (v. 22). The same verb recurs, with devastating irony, at the cross: "he saved others; he cannot save himself" (27:42).
  11. Son of David (v. 27). Dennis Duling's phrase "the therapeutic Son of David" names a pattern some scholars trace to Second Temple traditions of Solomon as healer/exorcist — live scholarship, not settled fact.
  12. Enebrimēthē (v. 30). Occurs only five times in the New Testament (also Mark 1:43; Mark 14:5; John 11:33, 38) — every other instance carries real emotional weight, not mere sternness.
  13. Beelzeboul (v. 34). The charge gets its full hearing, and its name, at 12:24 — this is its quiet first entrance.

Unit 15 · Matthew 15:1–39 · hands, hearts, crumbs and seven loaves

Recurring roots — colour key

    Where the unit goes

    Three scenes held together by eating: bread with unwashed hands, the bread of the children, seven loaves in a wilderness.

    Galilee, place unnamed 15:1–20→ The parts of Tyre and Sidon 15:21–28→ The mountain by the Sea of Galilee 15:29–38→ The borders of Magadan 15:39

    The two feedings side by side

    The second meal repeats the first almost phrase for phrase. The differences are the ones the text itself keeps apart when both are recalled at 16:9–10.

    The handing-down and the commandment · 15:1–9

    1Then there come to Jesus from Jerusalem Pharisees and scribes, saying,

    The first delegation from the capital since the magi story. The dispute that follows is carried out among people who all take the Torah seriously.

    2"Why do your learners overstep the handing-down of the elders? For they do not wash their hands whenever they eat bread."1

    paradosis, "handing-down": what one generation passes to the next. parabainō, "overstep": to step beside the line. artos, "bread", is the unit's keyword from its first sentence.

    3But he, answering, said to them, "Why do y'all also overstep the commandment of God for the sake of y'all's handing-down?

    He hands their verb back to them. Their charge was overstepping the elders; his is overstepping God.

    4For God said, 'Honor the father and the mother,' (Exod 20:12) and, 'The one speaking evil of father or mother, let him end in death.' (Exod 21:17)2

    timaō, "honor": to set a value on someone. Behind it stands a Hebrew verb meaning "give weight to"; behind "speak evil of" stands one meaning "treat as light".

    5But y'all say, 'Whoever says to the father or the mother, "A gift is whatever you might have been helped by from me,"

    dōron, "gift": here something vowed to God, and so put beyond a parent's reach.

    6he shall certainly not honor his father.' And y'all voided the word of God for the sake of y'all's handing-down.3

    akyroō, "void": to cancel the legal force of something.

    7Stage-actors! Well did Isaiah prophesy about y'all, saying,

    hypokritēs, "stage-actor": one who plays a part. Used of the show-pious at 6:2, 5, 16; here it is first aimed at the Pharisees.

    8'This people honors me with the lips, but their heart keeps far off from me; (Isa 29:13)

    9and in vain they revere me, teaching as teachings the precepts of humans.'" (Isa 29:13)4

    entalma, "precept": a thing commanded. A different word from the "commandment" (entolē) of God in verse 3.

    What makes a person common · 15:10–20

    10And summoning the crowd he said to them, "Hear and understand:

    syniēmi, "understand": to put things together. The verb of chapter 13, where the crowds heard and did not understand.

    11not what comes into the mouth makes the person common, but what goes out of the mouth, this makes the person common."5

    koinoō, "make common": the opposite of holy, set apart. "What goes out of the mouth" reuses the wording of 4:4, where a person lives on every word going out through the mouth of God.
    Greek (translit.)ou to eiserchomenon eis to stoma koinoi ton anthrōpon
    This translationnot what comes into the mouth makes the person common
    Traditionalmost versions say "defiles"; that names the result, where the Greek names the category the person is moved into

    12Then the learners, coming up, say to him, "Do you know that the Pharisees, on hearing the word, were tripped up?"

    skandalizō, "trip up": to spring a trap-stick under someone.

    13But he, answering, said, "Every planting that my Father in the skies did not plant will be uprooted.6

    phyteia, "planting": a thing planted. ekrizoō, "uproot": the verb the householder refused to allow over the weeds at 13:29.

    14Leave them: they are blind guides of the blind. And if a blind one guides a blind one, both will fall into a pit."7

    aphiēmi, "leave, release": the householder's "let both grow together" (13:30) uses the same verb. hodēgos, "guide": one who leads along the road.

    15And Peter, answering, said to him, "Spell out for us this parable."

    16But he said, "Are y'all too still without understanding?

    asynetos, "without understanding": the negative of the verb in verse 10. At 13:51 the learners said they had understood everything.

    17Do y'all not grasp that everything going into the mouth makes its way into the belly and is thrown out into the latrine?

    18But the things going out of the mouth come out of the heart, and those make the person common.

    The mouth speaks from the heart, as at 12:34.

    19For out of the heart come evil reasonings, murders, adulteries, sexual immoralities, thefts, false witnessings, defamations.8

    After the reasonings, the list walks through the Ten Words in order: murder, adultery, theft, false witness.
    Hos 4:2, where "cursing, and lying, and murder, and theft, and adultery abound in the land", in a lawsuit that opens with no loyal-love and no knowledge of God (4:1).

    20These are the things that make the person common; but to eat with unwashed hands does not make the person common."9

    The closing line returns to the opening charge of verse 2: unwashed hands.

    Mark 7:19 — a clause Matthew does not have

    Mark 7:19"…because it does not go into his heart but into the belly, and goes out into the latrine, katharizōn panta ta brōmata, cleansing all the foods."

    Mark's last clause is usually read as the narrator's comment that all foods are clean. Matthew leaves it out and ends on handwashing instead. Whether that narrows the saying to the question that was asked, or simply leaves the wider conclusion unstated, is the open question of this passage.

    The Canaanite woman · 15:21–28

    21And going out from there Jesus withdrew into the parts of Tyre and Sidon.10

    anachōreō, "withdraw": the book's verb for stepping back under pressure. Tyre and Sidon are the cities of 11:21–22.

    22And look: a Canaanite woman, coming out from those borders, kept crying out, saying, "Show me loyal-love, Master, Son of David! My daughter is badly demon-possessed."11

    eleeō, "show loyal-love": the verb of the covenant kindness God asks for in Hosea 6:6. Chananaia, "Canaanite", occurs only here in the New Testament.

    23But he did not answer her a word. And his learners, coming up, kept asking him, saying, "Send her away, because she is crying out behind us."

    apolyō, "send away, release": it can mean "be rid of her" or "let her go with what she asks". The learners said the same of the hungry crowd at 14:15.

    24But he, answering, said, "I was not sent out except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel."12

    Word for word the charge given to the Twelve at 10:6.
    Ezek 34:16, where Yahweh, against shepherds who fed themselves, says "I will seek that which is lost"; the same saying sends the Twelve at Matt 10:6.

    25But she, coming, was bowing low to him, saying, "Master, help me!"

    proskyneō, "bow low": the magi's verb (2:2, 11) and the learners' in the boat (14:33). The form pictures her as staying down.

    26But he, answering, said, "It is not fitting to take the bread of the children and throw it to the little dogs."13

    kynarion, "little dog": a diminutive of the word for dog; how much softening it carries is uncertain.
    Exod 22:31 (22:30 in the Greek numbering), where a people called holy must not eat flesh torn by beasts but "cast it to the dog".

    Mark 7:27 — "first"

    Mark 7:27"Let the children prōton, first, be filled; for it is not fitting to take the bread of the children and throw it to the little dogs."

    Mark's "first" implies a later turn for others. Matthew has no "first" and adds the lost-sheep saying of verse 24, so in his telling the limit is stated at its sharpest before it gives way.

    27But she said, "Yes, Master, for even the little dogs eat from the crumbs falling from the table of their masters."

    "Master" and "masters" are one Greek word, kyrios. She accepts his picture and argues from inside it.
    Greek (translit.)nai, kyrie, kai gar ta kynaria esthiei…
    This translationYes, Master, for even the little dogs eat…
    NASB (1995)"Yes, Lord; but even the dogs feed on the crumbs"
    Notekai gar is "for even", not "but even": her reply gives a reason for her "yes", not an objection to what he said.

    28Then Jesus, answering, said to her, "O woman, great is your trust! Let it come to be for you as you want." And her daughter was healed from that hour.14

    pistis, "trust". The only trust in the book called great, set against Peter's "little" at 14:31.

    On the mountain · 15:29–31

    29And moving on from there Jesus came alongside the Sea of Galilee, and going up onto the mountain he was sitting there.

    Up the mountain and seated, as at 5:1.
    Isa 25:6, where Yahweh makes "a feast for all the nations" on "this mount"; Isa 35:5–6, where the blind, the deaf, the lame and the mute are restored.

    30And many crowds came to him, having with them the mute, the blind, the lame, the maimed, and many others, and they flung them down at his feet; and he healed them,

    31so that the crowd marveled, seeing the mute speaking, the maimed whole, and the lame walking, and the blind seeing; and they glorified the God of Israel.15

    Isa 29:18, "the eyes of the blind shall see", a few lines after the verse quoted at 15:8–9.

    Seven loaves in a wilderness · 15:32–39

    32But Jesus, summoning his learners, said, "I am wrenched in the gut over the crowd, because already three days they stay with me and they do not have anything to eat; and to send them away fasting I do not want, lest they give out on the road."

    splanchnizomai, "be wrenched in the gut": from the word for the inner organs. Here Jesus says it of himself.

    33And the learners say to him, "From where, for us in a wilderness, so many loaves of bread as to fill so great a crowd?"16

    erēmia, "wilderness": an empty, unsettled place. chortazō, "fill": the verb of the promise at 5:6.
    Num 11:13, Moses' "Whence have I flesh to give to all this people?"; Ps 78:19–20 (77 in the Greek numbering), "Will God be able to prepare a table in the wilderness?… will he be able also to give bread?"; 2 Kgs 4:43, the servant's "Why should I set this before a hundred men?", answered by "They shall eat and leave."

    34And Jesus says to them, "How many loaves of bread do y'all have?" And they said, "Seven, and a few little fish."

    35And instructing the crowd to recline on the ground,

    36he took the seven loaves of bread and the fish, and having given thanks he broke them and was giving them to the learners, and the learners to the crowds.17

    eucharisteō, "give thanks", where 14:19 had "blessed". Took, gave thanks, broke, gave.
    Matt 26:26–27, where he takes bread, blesses, breaks and gives, then gives thanks over the cup; 1 Cor 11:23–24, "having given thanks he broke".

    37And all ate and were filled, and what was left over of the broken pieces they took up: seven hampers full.18

    spyris, "hamper": a different word from the "baskets" of 14:20.

    38And those eating were four thousand men, apart from women and children.19

    39And having sent away the crowds he got into the boat and came into the borders of Magadan.20

    Notes

    1. Washing hands (v. 2). The Torah commands priests to wash before they serve (Exod 30:17–21) ↗; it does not command ordinary Israelites to wash before ordinary meals. The practice extends priestly care to every table, a serious way of living as "a kingdom of priests" (Exod 19:6). The complaint is therefore about the handing-down, not about the written Law. The parallel in Mark stops to explain these customs to outsiders (Mark 7:3–4); this telling assumes readers who know them.
    2. "God said" (v. 4). The parallel has "Moses said". The two texts joined are Exod 20:12 and Exod 21:17 (21:16 in the Greek numbering). In Hebrew the pair is a contrast of weight: honor is kabbed, "give weight to", and the offence is meqallel, "treat as light". The Greek kakologeō, "speak evil of", is nearer to "insult" than to "curse".
    3. The gift (vv. 5–6). The parallel keeps the Aramaic term korban, "dedicated". The sentence in verse 5 breaks off in Greek and is completed by verse 6. Twice already this book has quoted Hos 6:6, "loyal-love and not sacrifice" (9:13; 12:7) ↗; a gift to God that cancels care for a parent is the same contrast in practice, though Hosea is not cited here. akyroō, "void", returns at Gal 3:17, where a later law cannot void an earlier covenant.
    4. Isaiah in Greek and in Hebrew (vv. 8–9). The quotation follows the Greek form of Isa 29:13 ↗, where the last line reads "in vain do they worship me, teaching the commandments and doctrines of men". The Hebrew has no "in vain": it says "their fear of me is a commandment of men, learned by rote". The Greek puts the weight on the teachers; the Hebrew on the worship itself. The same phrase returns at Col 2:22 against rules of handling and tasting, and Titus 1:14–15 sets "commandments of humans" beside "to the clean all things are clean".
    5. Common (v. 11). Leviticus asks Israel to distinguish "between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Lev 10:10) ↗; in Hebrew the pair is qodesh, "holy", and ḥol, "common". "Make common" is kept here so the word's later history stays audible: it is the word of Peter's vision in Acts 10.
    6. The planting (v. 13). Israel as God's planting is an old image (2 Sam 7:10; Isa 5:1–7). Here it is turned: some plantings are not the Father's. One reading takes the planting to be persons, the leaders themselves, as with the weeds of 13:24–30, 38. Another takes it to be their teaching or their standing as guides. An early reader warned against using the verse to sort people into two fixed kinds, since a planting can also go wrong (Jer 2:21). The verse does not settle it.
    7. Blind guides and the pit (v. 14). Some manuscripts lack "of the blind". The pit, bothynos, is the word for the Sabbath pit at 12:11. The saying stands in a different setting at Luke 6:39.
    8. Out of the heart (v. 19). The Greek of Gen 6:5 says each one "in his heart was intently brooding over evil", and Gen 8:21 says the same of the human bent "from youth" ↗. The wording here is close in sense, not in vocabulary. Inside this book the line picks up 12:34–35.
    9. Food, or hands? (vv. 11–20). Two readings stand side by side. One holds that the saying of verse 11 ends the food laws in principle, reading it with the parallel's "cleansing all the foods" and with Acts 10; an early homily ties this passage directly to Peter and Cornelius ↗. The other notes that this telling omits that clause, begins and ends on handwashing (vv. 2, 20), and lists what comes from the heart in the order of the Ten Words, and so keeps the dispute inside the question that was asked. Both agree on the centre: the heart is the source.
    10. Tyre and Sidon (v. 21). At 11:21–22 these are the cities that would have turned in sackcloth. Elijah also withdrew to Sidonian land after opposition, to a foreign widow, a little meal and a child (1 Kgs 17:8–24) ↗. The story is not cited here; the resemblance is offered as possible.
    11. Canaanite, Son of David (v. 22). The parallel calls her "a Greek, Syrophoenician by birth" (Mark 7:26). "Canaanite" reaches back to Israel's oldest neighbours and to the women of the genealogy. The same plea, "show loyal-love", comes from another parent of a demon-afflicted child at 17:15, where the learners' "little trust" (17:20) answers her "great trust".
    12. The silence and the limit (vv. 23–24). Four readings are live. The delay is a test meant to bring her trust into the open. The limit is real, a mission to Israel first, and the scene also teaches the learners. It is a teacher's deliberate challenge that does not state his own last word. Or he is himself moved by her. The first three can be held together; the fourth is harder to square with the lost-sheep saying being his own, repeated from 10:6.
    13. Dogs, holy things, and "masters" (vv. 26–27). At 7:6 the same teacher said "do not give what is holy to the dogs", with the same verb "throw" in the next clause. Here he states that rule and then gives. In her reply the "children" of his saying become "masters", so that she lowers herself and raises them in one breath.
    14. "Let it come to be" (v. 28). genēthētō, "let it come to be", is the formula of granted trust at 8:13 and 9:29, the word of the prayer at 6:10, and of Gethsemane at 26:42. An old reading hears in it the "let there be" of Gen 1:3. Like the centurion's servant (8:13), the daughter is healed at a distance, "from that hour".
    15. "The God of Israel" (v. 31). The phrase is the hinge of an open question: who is this crowd? One reading says outsiders would speak this way, and the parallel sets the scene in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31), so this is a second feeding for the nations. Another says the phrase is ordinary Jewish praise (Ps 41:13; 72:18; Isa 29:23) and that this telling names no Gentile place. The text leaves room for both.
    16. A table in the wilderness (v. 33). The learners' question repeats Israel's own. The pattern of too little food, an objection, everyone eating and some left over runs from the manna (Exod 16) through Num 11 to Elisha's twenty loaves (2 Kgs 4:42–44) ↗. "Were filled" (v. 37) is the promise of 5:6 kept for the hungry. The woman's "table" (v. 27) and this wilderness meal belong together: Ps 78:19 ↗.
    17. Thanks and blessing (v. 36). The first feeding has "blessed" (14:19); this one "gave thanks"; the supper has both (26:26–27). The feeding in John shares this passage's verbs for reclining and giving thanks and its word for the broken pieces (John 6:10–13).
    18. Hampers and baskets (v. 37). The two container words are kept apart when both meals are recalled (16:9–10), so the difference is deliberate. It is often said that one is a Jewish basket and the other a Gentile one. The second claim is weak: at Acts 9:25 Paul, a Jew, is lowered in a spyris.
    19. The numbers (v. 38). Counting the men and setting the rest apart is how the exodus company is counted (Exod 12:37) ↗. Seven has been read as the nations of Canaan (Deut 7:1; Acts 13:19) or simply as fullness, and four thousand as the four winds. None of these is marked by the text.
    20. Magadan (v. 39). The place is unknown; some manuscripts read Magdala. The parallel has Dalmanutha (Mark 8:10).

    The handing-down of the elders · 15:1–9

    1Then there come to Jesus from Jerusalem Pharisees and scribes, saying,

    2"Why do your learners step across the handing-down of the elders? For they do not wash their hands whenever they eat bread."1

    paradosis, "handing-down": a handing-over, what is passed down from teacher to teacher. parabainō, "step across": the verb for overstepping a boundary.
    The same challenge, the same shape: 9:14 asks why the learners do not fast, 12:2 why they pluck grain on the Sabbath, and each time Jesus answers out of Scripture.

    3And he, answering, said to them, "Why do y'all also step across the commandment of God because of your handing-down?

    4For God said, 'Honor your father and your mother,' (Exod 20:12) and, 'The one who speaks ill of father or mother, let him end in death.' (Exod 21:17)2

    5But y'all say, 'Whoever says to his father or mother, "A gift is whatever you might have been helped by from me,"

    dōron, "gift": a thing given to God. Mark gives the Aramaic word, qorban, and translates it.

    6he shall by no means honor his father.' And y'all have stripped the word of God of its authority because of your handing-down.3

    akyroō, "strip of authority": from a-, "without," and kyros, "authority, validity"; to cancel, to strip of force.

    7Stage-actors! Isaiah prophesied well about y'all, saying,

    hypokritēs, "stage-actor": a person who plays a part. The same word as in the Sermon's three acts of piety.

    8'This people honors me with the lips, but their heart holds far away from me;

    9and to no purpose they show devotion to me, teaching as teachings the commandments of men.' (Isa 29:13)4

    Mark 7:1–13 — the same dispute, told in another order

    Mark 7:3–4Mark stops to explain the Jewish washing customs to readers who may not know them. Matthew gives no explanation.
    Mark 7:6–13Mark puts Isaiah first ("Isaiah prophesied well about y'all, stage-actors"), then the commandment set aside for the handing-down, then the vow, with the Aramaic qorban translated, and ends "and many such things you do."
    Matthew 15:3–9Matthew opens with Jesus' counter-question, "Why do y'all also step across ...?", gives the vow, and only then brings Isaiah.

    Matthew drops the explanation and reverses the order, so that Isaiah's words fall as a verdict on the vow just described. The counter-question, with its "also," is his own: the disciples are charged with stepping across the handing-down, and Jesus turns the charge back on the questioners.

    What defiles a person · 15:10–20

    10And calling the crowd to him, he said to them, "Hear and understand:

    11not what goes into the mouth defiles the person, but what comes out of the mouth, this defiles the person."5

    koinoō, "defile": literally "make common," to make ordinary and no longer set apart. The loss is a negative one: what has become common can no longer approach the holy, which is why English Bibles say "defile."
    Greek (translit.)ou to eiserchomenon eis to stoma koinoi ton anthrōpon
    This translation"not what goes into the mouth defiles the person"
    WEB"That which enters into the mouth doesn't defile the man"
    NoteThe verb is koinoō, from koinos, "common, shared by all." Literally the verb means "make common": to take something out of the set-apart and put it into the ordinary, which in this setting is a loss of standing before God, hence "defile."

    12Then the learners, coming up, say to him, "Do you know that the Pharisees, hearing the saying, were tripped up?"

    13But he, answering, said, "Every planting that my Father in the skies did not plant will be uprooted.

    14Leave them. They are blind guides of the blind. And if a blind man guides a blind man, both will fall into a pit."

    Isaiah 56:10 calls Israel's watchmen blind, and Deuteronomy 27:18 curses anyone who misleads a blind man on the road.

    Luke 6:39 — the blind guides, and what Matthew adds around them

    Mark 7No counterpart: Mark has no offended Pharisees, no planting, no "leave them," and no blind guides.
    Luke 6:39"Can a blind man guide a blind man? Will they not both fall into a pit?" It is a proverb inside a sermon about judging.
    Matthew 15:12–14The same proverb, set as Jesus' verdict on Israel's teachers, after the planting saying and the order "leave them."

    Matthew alone gives the offense, the planting that the Father did not plant, and the order to leave them. The blind-guide proverb becomes a sentence on the Pharisees here, and he brings it back in the woes of chapter 23.

    15And Peter, answering, said to him, "Explain this parable to us."

    16And he said, "Are y'all, too, still without understanding?

    asynetos, "without understanding": the opposite of syniēmi, "put together, grasp," the verb of verse 10.

    17Do y'all not take in that everything going into the mouth moves into the belly and is thrown out into the latrine?

    aphedrōn, "latrine": a blunt word for a blunt point.

    18But what comes out of the mouth comes out from the heart, and those things defile the person.

    19For out of the heart come evil reasonings, murders, adulteries, sexual immoralities, thefts, false witnessings, defamations.

    porneia, "sexual immorality"; pseudomartyria, "false witnessing"; blasphēmia, "defaming."
    Hosea 4:2 and Jeremiah 7:9 set the same sins side by side as the prophets' charge against the land; here they are traced to the heart. The list follows the order of the second half of the Ten Words, as it does again at 19:18.

    20These are the things that defile the person; but to eat with unwashed hands does not defile the person."

    Mark 7:14–23 — the private teaching, and what Matthew leaves out

    Mark 7:17Inside a house, away from the crowd, "his learners asked him about the parable."
    Mark 7:19After the belly and the latrine: "(cleansing all foods)," a comment in the narrator's voice.
    Mark 7:21–22Thirteen items, from evil reasonings to pride and folly.
    Matthew 15:15–20Peter asks. No house, no comment on foods, seven items, and a closing line that returns to the first question: eating with unwashed hands.

    Matthew omits the comment on foods, shortens the list to seven in the order of the Ten Words, names Peter, and ends where the dispute began. Whether that narrows the saying to the question that was asked, or leaves the wider conclusion unstated, is the open question of this passage.

    The Canaanite woman · 15:21–28

    21And going out from there, Jesus withdrew into the parts of Tyre and Sidon.6

    anachōreō, "withdraw": the book's verb for stepping back under pressure. Tyre and Sidon are the cities of 11:21–22.
    In 11:21–22 Tyre and Sidon are the cities for whom the day of judgment will be more bearable than for Chorazin and Bethsaida; now Jesus goes to them.

    Elijah and the widow of Zarephath 1 Kgs 17:8–24

    In a drought, Yahweh sends Elijah to Zarephath, "which belongs to Sidon," where he has commanded a widow to feed him. She is gathering sticks, with a handful of meal and a little oil, to cook a last meal for herself and her son. Elijah asks for bread, and for a little cake for himself first; she does it, and the jar of meal never runs out. Later her son dies, Elijah raises him, and she says, "Now I know that you are a man of God."

    • a woman from Sidon's territory
    • a child in mortal danger
    • a prophet of Israel's God, and bread at the center
    • an outsider's confession

    Elijah goes to her at God's word, and she feeds the prophet before her son; here the woman comes out to Jesus, and her request is for her daughter, not for bread. The danger there is death, here a demon.

    It shows that God's mercy has already crossed into Sidonian country inside Israel's own story, which unsettles "the children's bread only" before Jesus has even said it.

    Luke has Jesus himself name this widow in the synagogue at Nazareth (Luke 4:25–26).

    22And look: a Canaanite woman, coming out from those borders, kept crying out, saying, "Show me loyal-love, Master, Son of David! My daughter is badly demon-possessed."

    eleeō, "show loyal-love": the verb of the covenant kindness God wants more than sacrifice (Hosea 6:6). Chananaia, "Canaanite woman," occurs only here in the New Testament.

    Rahab of Jericho Josh 2:1–21; 6:22–25

    Rahab is a Canaanite woman in Jericho. She hides Israel's two spies, confesses that Yahweh your God is God in the sky above and on the earth beneath, and asks for ḥesed, "loyal-love," for her father's household. When Jericho falls she and her family are spared, and Matthew has already named her in the Anointed's line (1:5).

    • a Canaanite woman who crosses a line
    • a confession of Israel's God
    • life for a household won by bold speech

    Rahab bargains from knowledge of what Yahweh did at the sea and offers the spies something in return. This woman has nothing to offer but need, and she asks for her daughter, not for herself.

    It makes her one of the Canaanite women the book has already put in the Messiah's own line (1:3, 5), so that an outsider asking for loyal-love is not new to the story but is its pattern.

    Mark 7:24–26 — who she is, and where Jesus is

    Mark 7:24Jesus goes to the region of Tyre, enters a house, wants no one to know, and cannot escape notice.
    Mark 7:25–26A woman whose little daughter has an unclean spirit falls at his feet: "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by birth."
    Matthew 15:21–22No house, no secrecy. A Chananaia, "Canaanite woman," coming out "from those borders," crying "Master, Son of David."

    Mark's woman is a Greek from Syrian Phoenicia; Matthew's is a Canaanite, a name that carries Israel's oldest enemies and the women of the genealogy. Only Matthew gives her the title Son of David and a Jesus who is not hiding.

    23But he did not answer her a word. And his learners, coming up, kept asking him, saying, "Send her away, because she is crying out behind us."

    apolyō, "send away, release": it can mean "be rid of her" or "let her go with what she asks." The learners said the same of the hungry crowd at 14:15.

    24But he, answering, said, "I was not sent except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel."

    Word for word the charge given to the Twelve at 10:6. Only Matthew has this saying here.
    Ezekiel 34:16 has Yahweh promise to seek what was lost, against shepherds who fed themselves, and Jeremiah 50:6 (27:6 in the Greek numbering) calls his people "lost sheep."

    25But she, coming, kept bowing low to him, saying, "Master, help me."

    proskyneō, "bow low": the magi's verb (2:2, 11) and the learners' in the boat (14:33). The Greek pictures an action that goes on, so she stayed down.

    26But he, answering, said, "It is not good to take the bread of the children and throw it to the little dogs."7

    kynarion, "little dog": a diminutive of kyōn, "dog." Mark has the same word.
    Greek (translit.)ouk estin kalon labein ton arton tōn teknōn kai balein tois kynariois
    This translation"It is not good to take the bread of the children and throw it to the little dogs."
    WEB"It is not appropriate to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs."
    Notekalon is "good, fine, fitting"; kynarion is a diminutive. The text does not tell us how much the diminutive softens.
    In Exodus 22:31, torn flesh from the field is what Israel must not eat: "you shall throw it to the dogs." Matthew 7:6 has already said not to give what is holy to the dogs.

    27But she said, "Yes, Master, for even the little dogs eat from the crumbs that fall from the table of their masters."8

    psichion, "crumb": another diminutive, and another word Mark shares.

    28Then Jesus, answering, said to her, "O woman, great is your trust. Let it come to be for you as you wish." And her daughter was healed from that hour.

    pistis, "trust." genēthētō, "let it come to be": the verb form of "let your will come to be" in the Lord's Prayer.
    "Let it come to be for you as you wish" uses the verb form and the root of "let your will come to be" (6:10); the centurion heard "let it come to be for you as you trusted" (8:13), and both are Gentiles asking on behalf of someone else.

    Mark 7:27–30 — "first," and what the woman is told

    Mark 7:27"Let the children first be filled, for it is not good to take the children's bread and throw it to the little dogs."
    Mark 7:28"Yes, Master; even the little dogs under the table eat from the children's crumbs."
    Mark 7:29–30"For this saying, go; the demon has gone out of your daughter." She goes home and finds the child lying on the bed.
    Matthew 15:26–28No "first," no "under the table." "O woman, great is your trust. Let it come to be for you as you wish." The daughter is healed "from that hour."

    Mark's saying sets an order: the children first, then the little dogs. Matthew leaves out "first," so the saying states who the bread is for, and the woman's reply is met with a verdict on her trust, not on her saying. The healing comes at once.

    The mountain and the seven loaves · 15:29–39

    29And moving on from there, Jesus came along the sea of Galilee, and going up onto the mountain he sat there.

    The Sermon began with this same going up and sitting (5:1), and the book ends on a mountain (28:16); Isaiah 25:6–10 pictures a feast on a mountain for all peoples.

    30And many crowds came up to him, having with them the mute, the blind, the lame, the maimed, and many others, and they tossed them down at his feet, and he healed them,

    kōphos, "mute": the Greek word can also mean "deaf"; here the mute are later seen speaking.

    31so that the crowd was amazed, seeing the mute speaking, the maimed whole, and the lame walking, and the blind seeing; and they glorified the God of Israel.9

    The list is Isaiah 35:5–6 and the answer Jesus gave John (11:5); the phrase "blind seeing" is Isaiah 29:18, and "the God of Israel" closes the same chapter at 29:23, the chapter quoted in the first scene.

    Mark 7:31–37 and 8:1–10 — one healing becomes many, and the second feeding

    Mark 7:31–35From Tyre through Sidon to the sea of Galilee, within the region of the Ten Cities. One man, deaf and barely able to speak, is taken aside; Jesus uses spittle and says Ephphatha, "be opened."
    Mark 7:37"He has done all things well. He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak."
    Mark 8:1–10The second feeding in almost the same terms: seven loaves, seven baskets, "about four thousand people," and a crossing to the district of Dalmanutha.
    Matthew 15:29–31, 38–39No named region, no single healing. A mountain, a crowd, Isaiah's list, and "they glorified the God of Israel." The count is four thousand men "apart from women and children," and the boat lands at Magadan.

    Matthew trades Mark's one healing, with its spittle and its Aramaic word, for a mass healing on a mountain built from Isaiah's list. The crowd's cry changes from praise of Jesus to praise of Israel's God, which makes the scene sound like an answer to the hearts far from God at the start of the chapter.

    32And Jesus, calling his learners to him, said, "I am wrenched in the gut over the crowd, because already three days they stay with me and have nothing to eat. And I do not wish to send them away fasting, lest they give out on the road."

    splanchnizomai, "be wrenched in the gut": a blunt bodily word for compassion. nēstis, "fasting, without eating."

    33And the learners say to him, "From where could we get, in a wilderness, so many loaves as to feed so large a crowd?"

    erēmia, "wilderness": a deserted place.

    Israel's wilderness question Exod 16; Num 11:4–23; Ps 78:19–29

    In the wilderness Israel complains of hunger, and Yahweh gives manna and quail. Moses, told to feed the people, asks, "From where am I to get meat for all this people?" and wonders whether even all the fish of the sea would be gathered for them. Yahweh answers, "Is Yahweh's hand shortened?" A psalm later asks whether God can set a table in the wilderness.

    • a hungry mass in a deserted place
    • a leader asked "from where?" for so many
    • fish named beside the bread
    • God supplies

    Israel grumbles and craves; this crowd has simply stayed three days. Moses had no earlier feeding behind him, the learners have just seen five thousand fed. And manna spoiled if it was kept, while here seven hampers of pieces are left over.

    It makes the learners' question Israel's old doubt, now asked by people who have already seen the answer, and it puts Jesus in the place of the God who answers it.

    34And Jesus says to them, "How many loaves do y'all have?" And they said, "Seven, and a few little fish."

    35And instructing the crowd to recline on the ground,

    36he took the seven loaves and the fish, and having given thanks he broke them and kept giving them to the learners, and the learners to the crowds.

    eucharisteō, "give thanks": at 14:19 the verb was eulogeō, "bless."

    37And they all ate and were filled, and they took up what was left over of the broken pieces, seven hampers full.10

    spyris, "hamper": a different word from the kophinos, "basket," of 14:20.
    As with Elisha's twenty loaves, they eat and have some left over (2 Kings 4:42–44), the same pattern the five thousand already followed.

    38And those eating were four thousand men, apart from women and children.11

    39And sending the crowds away, he got into the boat and came into the borders of Magadan.12

    Notes

    1. The handing-down of the elders (v. 2). paradosis is "a handing-over," the noun of the verb paradidōmi, "hand over." The Torah has no general command for ordinary people to wash their hands before a meal; the nearest text is the washing of hands and feet by priests before they approach the altar (Exod 30:17–21). The handing-down extends priestly purity to every table, and the Mishnah later gave the washing of hands a tractate of its own. The question was serious and sincere, not trivial.
    2. "Speaks ill of" (v. 4). The verb is kakologeō, "speak badly of," the verb of the Greek Old Testament at Exod 21:17 (21:16 in the Greek numbering). It is stronger than a quarrel and weaker than "curse." The law is repeated in Lev 20:9.
    3. The vow (vv. 5–6). The Greek is elliptical: "Gift—whatever you might have been helped by from me." A pledge to give to God what a parent needs becomes a way to keep it. Mark supplies the Aramaic qorban. The law of vows is Num 30 and Deut 23:21–23; the point is that a vow made to God cannot be used to dodge the commandment to honor one's parents.
    4. Isaiah 29:13 (vv. 8–9). The Hebrew of the second half is, in plain terms, that their fear of God has become "a commandment of men, learned by rote." The Greek Old Testament, which Matthew follows, reads "to no purpose do they show devotion to me, teaching commandments and teachings of men." That wording gives Jesus both the empty worship and the human teaching. The chapter belongs to Isaiah's oracle against "Ariel," a name for Jerusalem under threat, with a sealed book; its promise of reversal comes at 29:18–24 and is answered at 15:30–31 in this chapter. The same verse is quoted at Col 2:22. NETS ↗
    5. What the saying does and does not say (v. 11). A dispensational reading holds that Jesus ends the food distinctions of the Mosaic system, and it points to Mark's comment that he "declared all foods clean." Another reading notes that Matthew does not repeat that comment, ends on the question of unwashed hands, and keeps Jesus a faithful Jew who relocates what defiles a person into the heart while leaving the food question unasked. Those who first heard the saying remembered people who chose death over eating what was common (1 Macc 1:62–63; compare Dan 1:8). The text does not decide between the two readings, and this study does not either.
    6. "The parts" (v. 21). Jesus goes into "the parts" of Tyre and Sidon, not the cities themselves. One ancient Christian reading takes that wording to mean that the Gentiles believe "in part," and notes that she comes out from the same parts so that they meet at the border.
    7. The little dogs (v. 26). kynarion is a diminutive, and both it and "crumb" are words Mark has too. One reading hears household pets under the table and a softened word; another notes that diminutive endings often lose their force in everyday Greek and rests nothing on the suffix. A second question is whether Jesus is stating the order of the bread in earnest, testing her, or speaking for the learners' sake. All three are readings; the Greek does not settle them. The Torah's own dogs get what Israel may not eat (Exod 22:31).
    8. "Yes, for even" (v. 27). The Greek is nai ... kai gar, "yes ... for even," not "but even." She concedes his premise and argues from inside it. One reading hears in the exchange Jacob's wrestling with God (Gen 32:24–32); nothing in Matthew's wording points there.
    9. "The God of Israel" (v. 31). Some read the phrase as outsiders' language, praise from people who do not call him their own; others note that it also fits an Israelite crowd. Isaiah 29:23 has the same phrase, which makes the scene answer the first one. The bracketed words "the maimed whole" are missing from some manuscripts.
    10. Two basket words (v. 37). The first feeding ended with twelve kophinoi, "baskets" (14:20); this one ends with seven spyrides, "hampers." Matthew has Jesus recall both words, kept apart, at 16:9–10, so the difference is deliberate. A common guess is that the first was a Jewish carrying-basket and the second a Gentile one; that is an inference, not something the text says.
    11. Who are the four thousand? (v. 38). One side argues for a Gentile crowd: the Ten Cities region in Mark, the praise of "the God of Israel," the different basket word, the number seven, and a context that comes straight after the Canaanite woman. The other side points out that Matthew drops Mark's region, places Jesus on a mountain beside the Sea of Galilee, and that seven need not mean Gentile. Matthew's own geography is thin. This study holds the question open and notes that the literary context is the strongest argument for a Gentile crowd.
    12. Magadan (v. 39). The place is unknown. Some manuscripts read Magdala, and Mark has Dalmanutha. If it was on the west shore, the next verse's Pharisees and Sadducees fit.

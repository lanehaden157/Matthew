The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

Master of the Sabbath, the Chosen Servant

kyrios gar estin tou sabbatou ho huios tou anthrōpou — for master of the Sabbath is the Son of Man
Unit 12 · Matthew 12:1–50 · two Sabbath fights, Isaiah's quiet servant, and the accusers put on trial

    The shape of the chapter · 12:1–50

    Six scenes. The first three turn on the Sabbath and end in Isaiah's servant; the last three turn on speech and judgment. This is a sequence, not a ring — no chiasm is claimed.

    Grainfield 12:1–8→ Synagogue 12:9–14→ Withdrawal & Isaiah 42 12:15–21→ Beelzeboul 12:22–37→ Sign of Jonah 12:38–45→ Mother & brothers 12:46–50

    "Something greater is here" — three comparisons · 12:6, 41, 42

    The shared word is hōde, "here." The comparative adjectives differ (meizon at 12:6, pleion at 12:41–42), and all three are neuter — "something," not "someone."

    What is surpassedMatthew's wording
    The temple — the place of the Presence and of priestly Sabbath work (12:5)"something greater than the temple is here" (12:6, meizon)
    Jonah — the prophet whose proclamation turned Nineveh (Jonah 3)"something more than Jonah is here" (12:41, pleion)
    Solomon — the king whose wisdom drew the queen of the south (1 Kgs 10)"something more than Solomon is here" (12:42, pleion)

    Isaiah 42:1–4 in three forms · 12:18–21

    Matthew's wording matches neither the Hebrew nor the Greek Old Testament exactly. Where it departs, the departures point back to the baptism (3:17) and outward to the nations.

    Hebrew (MT) / Greek OT (LXX)Matthew 12
    MT: "Look, my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen one, in whom my being delights." LXX: "Jacob my servant, I will help him; Israel my chosen, my being received him.""Look, my servant whom I chose, my beloved, in whom my being delighted." — no Jacob, no Israel; "beloved" as at 3:17
    MT and LXX: "I have put / I gave my spirit upon him.""I will put my Spirit upon him."

    Hungry learners in the grainfield · 12:1–8

    1In that season Jesus went on the Sabbaths through the grainfields. His learners were hungry, and they began to pluck heads of grain and to eat.1

    Sabbath — sabbaton, the seventh-day rest; the Greek often uses the plural for a single Sabbath day. Learners — mathētai, from manthanō, "to learn" (usually "disciples"). Hungry — peinaō; only Matthew says the learners were hungry, which sets up David's hunger in v. 3.

    2But the Pharisees, seeing it, said to him, "Look, your learners are doing what is not permitted to do on a Sabbath."

    Permitted — exestin, "it is permitted, it is allowed," the verb of legal questions; it returns at vv. 4, 10 and 12. Plucking grain by hand in a neighbour's field was itself allowed (Deut 23:25); the charge is Sabbath work.

    3He said to them, "Did y'all not read what David did when he was hungry, and those with him —2

    "With" — meta, the little word of "God-with-us" (1:23); here the company of the rejected, anointed king.

    4how he went into the house of God and they ate the loaves of the presentation, which was not permitted for him to eat, nor for those with him, but only for the priests?

    House — oikos; the chapter's houses run from God's house here to the divided house (v. 25), the strong man's house (v. 29) and the unclean spirit's "my house" (v. 44). Loaves of the presentation — the bread of the Presence, reserved for priests (Lev 24:5–9).

    5Or did y'all not read in the Law that on the Sabbaths the priests in the temple profane the Sabbath and are guiltless?3

    6I say to y'all: something greater than the temple is here.4

    Greek (translit.)tou hierou meizon estin hōde
    NASBsomething greater than the temple is here!
    KJVin this place is one greater than the temple (following the masculine variant)
    NoteThe better-attested neuter "something" leaves open whether the referent is Jesus himself, the kingdom arriving in him, or the whole situation.

    7And if y'all had known what this is — 'Loyal-love I want, and not sacrifice' (Hos 6:6) — y'all would not have condemned the guiltless.5

    Loyal-love — eleos, the Greek Old Testament's word for Hebrew chesed, covenant faithfulness. The "if … would not" grammar describes something that did not happen: they did not know. Guiltless — the same word as the priests in v. 5.

    8For master of the Sabbath is the Son of Man."6

    Master — kyrios, placed first for emphasis. The Son of Man — ho huios tou anthrōpou, the figure of Daniel 7:13–14.

    Mark 2:27–28 — the line Matthew leaves out

    Mark 2:27"The Sabbath came to be for the sake of the human, and not the human for the sake of the Sabbath."
    Mark 2:28"So master is the Son of Man even of the Sabbath."

    Matthew drops Mark's humane principle and replaces it with the temple and Hosea arguments (vv. 5–7). The principle was itself at home in later rabbinic teaching, so Matthew's distinctive move is not the principle but the claim of authority over the Sabbath.

    A withered hand in the synagogue · 12:9–14

    9Moving on from there, he came into their gathering-place.

    10And look — a person having a withered hand. And they questioned him, saying, "Is it permitted on the Sabbaths to heal?" — so that they might accuse him.

    11He said to them, "What person will there be among y'all who will have one sheep, and if it falls on the Sabbaths into a pit, will he not take hold of it and raise it?7

    Raise — egeirō, the everyday verb for lifting or rousing that Matthew also uses for resurrection; here at its most ordinary.

    12How much, then, a person differs from a sheep! So it is permitted on the Sabbaths to do well."

    An argument from lighter to heavier (Hebrew qal va-chomer). The conclusion "to do well" is broader than saving a life, and it answers them with their own verb.

    13Then he says to the person, "Stretch out your hand." And he stretched it out, and it was restored, healthy as the other.

    Stretch out — ekteinō. The verb returns at v. 49, when Jesus stretches out his own hand over his learners.

    14But the Pharisees went out and took counsel against him, how they might destroy him.8

    Destroy — apollymi, "lose, destroy," the verb of Herod's plot to destroy the child (2:13).

    The servant who will not quarrel · 12:15–21

    15But Jesus, knowing it, withdrew from there. And many followed him, and he healed them all,

    Withdrew — anachōreō, Matthew's verb for tactical retreat (2:13–14, 4:12). Followed — akoloutheō.

    16and he charged them sharply that they not make him manifest,9

    17so that what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet might be filled full, saying:

    Filled full — plēroō, the verb of Matthew's formula quotations.

    18"Look, my servant whom I chose, my beloved, in whom my being delighted. I will put my Spirit upon him, and he will announce judgment to the nations. (Isa 42:1)10

    Servant — pais, which can mean both "servant" and "child." Delighted — eudokeō, the word of the voice at the baptism (3:17). Being — psychē, "life, being." Spirit — pneuma. Judgment — krisis, "judgment, justice." Nations — ethnē.

    19He will not quarrel, nor cry out, nor will anyone hear his voice in the broad streets. (Isa 42:2)

    Broad streets — plateiai, the same word as the street corners where the performers pray to be seen (6:5).

    20A crushed reed he will not break, and a smouldering wick he will not quench, until he throws out judgment to victory. (Isa 42:3)

    Throws out — ekballō, the same verb Jesus uses for throwing out demons in vv. 24–28 and for bringing good out of a good treasure in v. 35.

    21And in his name nations will hope." (Isa 42:4)

    By whom does he throw out demons? · 12:22–30

    22Then a demonized man, blind and mute, was brought to him; and he healed him, so that the mute man spoke and saw.

    Spoke — laleō, "speak." The mute man given speech opens a chapter that goes on to put every idle word on trial (vv. 34–37).

    23And all the crowds were beside themselves and were saying, "This one isn't the Son of David, is he?"

    Son of David — huios Dauid, the royal title Matthew ties to healing (9:27). The Greek question form expects a hesitant answer; readers divide on whether it expects "no" or tentatively hopes for "yes."

    24But the Pharisees, hearing it, said, "This one does not throw out the demons except by Beelzeboul, ruler of the demons."11

    25Knowing their thoughts, he said to them, "Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste, and every city or house divided against itself will not stand.

    Kingdom — basileia. Laid waste — erēmoutai, literally "is made a wilderness."

    26And if Satan throws out Satan, he was divided against himself; how then will his kingdom stand?

    27And if I throw out the demons by Beelzeboul, by whom do y'all's sons throw them out? For this reason they will be y'all's judges.

    Y'all's sons — most likely the Pharisees' own followers who practised exorcism. They are the first of three sets of unexpected judges in the chapter (then the Ninevites, v. 41, and the queen of the south, v. 42).

    28But if by the Spirit of God I throw out the demons, then the kingdom of God has arrived upon y'all.12

    Greek (translit.)ara ephthasen eph' hymas hē basileia tou theou
    NASBthen the kingdom of God has come upon you
    NoteNot the "has drawn near" (ēngiken) of 3:2 and 4:17 but phthanō, "arrive, come upon." One of Matthew's rare uses of "kingdom of God" in place of "kingdom of the skies."

    29Or how can anyone go into the house of the strong man and seize his goods, unless he first binds the strong man? And then he will plunder his house.13

    30The one who is not with me is against me, and the one who does not gather with me scatters.

    Gather — synagō, the root of "synagogue." Gathering and scattering return at 23:37 and 26:31.

    The one release that will not come · 12:31–37

    31For this reason I say to y'all: every sin and defaming will be released to people, but the defaming of the Spirit will not be released.1415

    Sin — hamartia, "missing the mark." Released — aphiēmi, "release, let go," the verb usually translated "forgive." The passive "will be released" is a way of speaking of God acting without naming him. "To y'all" is plural: the warning is aimed at the accusers.
    Greek (translit.)pasa hamartia kai blasphēmia aphethēsetai tois anthrōpois
    NASBany sin and blasphemy shall be forgiven people
    Noteblasphēmia is injurious, defamatory speech; "blasphemy" is its loan-word. "Defaming" is used here to keep the plain sense audible (see note 15).

    32And whoever says a word against the Son of Man, it will be released to him; but whoever says a word against the Holy Spirit, it will not be released to him, neither in this age nor in the one to come.

    33Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree rotten and its fruit rotten; for by the fruit the tree is known.

    Fruit — karpos, as in 7:16–20. "Make" is a plural command: the accusers are told to be consistent.

    34Offspring of vipers! How can y'all speak good things, being evil? For out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks.

    Offspring of vipers — John's phrase for the Pharisees (3:7), returning at 23:33. Evil — ponēros.

    35The good person out of the good treasure throws out good things, and the evil person out of the evil treasure throws out evil things.

    Treasure — thēsauros, "storehouse"; heart and treasure were already paired at 6:21. Throws out — the verb of the exorcisms (v. 28), turned to speech.

    36I say to y'all that every idle word that people will speak, they will give back an account of it in the day of judgment.16

    37For by your words you will be shown to be in the right, and by your words you will be condemned."

    Shown to be in the right — dikaioō, as at 11:19. Condemned — katadikazō, the verb of v. 7: those who condemned the guiltless face their own verdict.

    The sign of Jonah · 12:38–42

    38Then some of the scribes and Pharisees answered him, saying, "Teacher, we want to see a sign from you."

    Sign — sēmeion. "Teacher" (didaskalos) is how outsiders address Jesus in Matthew.

    39He answered and said to them, "An evil and adulterous generation seeks after a sign, and no sign will be given to it except the sign of Jonah the prophet.17

    Generation — genea, here a class of people, as at 11:16. Adulterous — the prophets' image for covenant unfaithfulness (Hos 2–3; Ezek 16). Seeks after — epizēteō, a compound of zēteō, "seek."

    40For just as Jonah was in the belly of the sea-monster three days and three nights (Jonah 1:17), so the Son of Man will be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights.1819

    Greek (translit.)en tē koilia tou kētous … en tē kardia tēs gēs
    NASBin the belly of the sea monster … in the heart of the earth
    KJVin the whale's belly
    Text-formkētos is the Greek Old Testament's word at Jonah 1:17 (Hebrew 2:1) for the dag gadol, "great fish": a sea-monster, not a species.

    Luke 11:29–30 — the sign without the fish

    Luke 11:29"This generation is an evil generation; it seeks a sign, and a sign will not be given to it except the sign of Jonah."
    Luke 11:30"For just as Jonah became a sign to the Ninevites, so the Son of Man will also be to this generation."

    Luke leaves the sign as Jonah himself — his person and his proclamation. Matthew alone adds the belly of the sea-monster and the three days, which ties the sign to death and return while keeping the proclamation in v. 41.

    41Men of Nineveh will stand up in the judgment with this generation and will judge against it, because they turned at the proclamation of Jonah — and look, something more than Jonah is here.

    Judge against — katakrinō, "condemn," built on the same stem as "judgment." Turned — metanoeō, "turn, change one's mind" (usually "repent"). Proclamation — kērygma, the noun of "proclaim."

    42The queen of the south will be raised in the judgment with this generation and will judge against it, because she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon — and look, something more than Solomon is here.

    Two Gentile witnesses rise at the judgment: the Ninevites "stand up" (anistēmi) and the queen "will be raised" (egeirō) — two different verbs. The queen of Sheba came to Solomon in 1 Kings 10.

    The swept and empty house · 12:43–45

    43Whenever the unclean spirit goes out from the person, it goes through waterless places seeking rest, and does not find it.20

    Unclean — akathartos, "not cleansed," from the same stem as katharizō, "cleanse." Rest — anapausis, the rest Jesus promised the weary at 11:28–29: those who come to him find it; this spirit does not.

    44Then it says, 'Into my house I will go back, from where I came out.' And coming, it finds it vacant, swept, and put in order.

    Put in order — kosmeō, "adorn, set in order," the verb used at 23:29 for adorning the tombs of the righteous.

    45Then it goes and takes along with itself seven other spirits more evil than itself, and going in they settle there; and the last state of that person becomes worse than the first. So it will be also for this evil generation."

    ponēros, "evil," occurs seven times in vv. 34–45, in a passage whose climax is seven spirits more evil than the first. The count is noted, not claimed as design.

    Mother and brothers · 12:46–50

    46While he was still speaking to the crowds, look — his mother and his brothers stood outside, seeking to speak to him.

    47[Someone said to him, "Look, your mother and your brothers are standing outside, seeking to speak to you."]21

    48He answered and said to the one telling him, "Who is my mother, and who are my brothers?"

    49And stretching out his hand over his learners, he said, "Look — my mother and my brothers.22

    The hand he told the withered man to stretch out (v. 13) he now stretches out himself, over his learners.

    50For whoever does the will of my Father in the skies, that one is my brother and sister and mother."

    Father — patēr. The list has no "father": his Father is in the skies. Will — thelēma, in Matthew always the Father's will (6:10, 7:21, 18:14, 21:31, 26:42).

    Notes

    1. Sabbath work (v. 1). The Mishnah's list of thirty-nine forbidden categories of Sabbath labour includes reaping, threshing and winnowing (Mishnah Shabbat 7:2). The Mishnah is later than Matthew, but Constable notes it likely reflects earlier practice, with earlier Sabbath lists in Jubilees and the Damascus Document. So the Pharisees' charge is a real legal question, not a straw man. ↗ Plucking by hand from a neighbour's field was explicitly allowed: Deut 23:25 ↗.
    2. David and the bread (vv. 3–4). The story is 1 Sam 21:1–6 ↗; the priests-only rule is Lev 24:5–9 ↗. Constable holds two readings, Edersheim's "on the Lord's service" and his own preferred "human need takes precedence over ritual." A third reading takes David as a type: the rejected anointed king and his loyal company. Matthew omits Mark's naming of Abiathar as high priest (1 Samuel names Ahimelech).
    3. The priests (v. 5). The temple service in view is probably the Sabbath replacement of the showbread and the doubled Sabbath offering (Num 28:9–10 ↗). "Profane" speaks from the accusers' standpoint, which Jesus grants in order to win the point. Verses 5–7 have no parallel in Mark or Luke.
    4. "Something greater" (v. 6). The neuter meizon is the better-attested reading; the "one greater" of older English versions follows a masculine variant. The same "here" (hōde) returns at 12:41–42 with Jonah and Solomon (see the table above). Constable, quoting Wiersbe, maps the three onto priest, prophet and king; the temple–Jonah–Solomon sequence is solid, but that mapping is his.
    5. Hosea 6:6 again (v. 7). The same quotation as 9:13. In Hebrew idiom "X and not Y" often means "X more than Y," so the sense is priority, not the abolition of sacrifice. Constable, quoting Lenski, observes that the Pharisees keep asking "is it permitted?" and never "is it loyal-love?" ↗
    6. Master of the Sabbath (v. 8) — readings held open. Constable reads the Sabbath's observance as ended when Jesus "terminated the whole Mosaic Code." Chrysostom (Homily 39) says Jesus never breaks the Sabbath without giving reasons, so as to bring the law to an end without startling his hearers. ↗ Against both, Jesus argues from inside Torah here, and Matthew alone keeps "pray that your flight not be on a Sabbath" (24:20). The humane principle was also held by later rabbinic teachers: the Mekhilta on Exod 31:14 attributes to Shimon ben Menasya the saying that the Sabbath is given to you, not you to the Sabbath ↗, and Yoma 85b has a similar saying from Yonatan ben Yosef ↗. Abolition, fulfilment and authoritative reinterpretation all remain on the table. The Bible Project reads 11:28–12:14 as one movement about seventh-day rest: ↗.
    7. The sheep in the pit (v. 11). The Damascus Document's Sabbath rules (CD 11:13–14) forbid lifting an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath. Jesus appeals instead to what he expects his Pharisaic questioners would do. The Pharisaic position, according to Constable, was that healing on the Sabbath was allowed only when life was in danger (Mishnah Yoma 8:6), and neither the sheep nor the man is in mortal danger. Matthew replaces Mark's question (Mark 3:4) with this sheep saying, which resembles Luke 14:5.
    8. "Took counsel … destroy" (v. 14). symboulion elabon, "they took counsel," is the phrase of the leaders' plotting throughout Matthew (22:15, 27:1, 27:7, 28:12). apolesōsin, "that they might destroy," is the verb of Herod's plot at 2:13, and in both places Jesus then withdraws (2:14; 12:15). Constable, quoting Kingsbury, calls the leaders' repudiation now irreversible.
    9. Rendering flag — "charged them sharply" (v. 16). epitimaō is usually rendered "rebuke." Lexica give "censure, rebuke" but, with a "that … not" clause as here, "admonish strongly, charge strictly" — one NT lexicon cites this very verse for the latter sense. ↗ translation-choices.md has no ruling for this verb, and its rendering at 8:26 (the wind and sea) has not been checked against this one. Lane to decide whether it gets a standing rendering.
    10. Isaiah 42 and its text-form (vv. 18–21). The longest Old Testament quotation in Matthew. The Greek Old Testament identifies the servant as "Jacob … Israel"; Matthew does not, and he replaces "chosen" with "beloved," the word of the baptism (3:17) and the Transfiguration (17:5). The last line agrees with the Greek Old Testament ("in his name nations will hope") against the Hebrew ("the coastlands wait for his torah"). Scholars divide on Matthew's source: a fresh rendering of the Hebrew, or a revised Greek text he then shaped. One such study is Irons' ↗. Constable reads krisis here as coming judgment, not merely justice. Scholarly discussions trace a split in Jewish interpretation: diaspora readings took the servant corporately, as the LXX's "Jacob/Israel" shows, while some Palestinian and later readings, including Maimonides, took the passage messianically.
    11. Beelzeboul (v. 24). The name is disputed. It is commonly linked to Baal-zebub, "lord of flies," the god of Ekron (2 Kgs 1:2 ↗). Other proposals include "lord of the heavenly dwelling" and a mocking "lord of dung." ↗ If zebul means "dwelling," the name would be "lord of the house." That fits 10:25, where Jesus calls himself the "master of the house," and the houses of vv. 25–29 — but the etymology is not certain.
    12. "Has arrived upon y'all" (v. 28) — readings held open. Constable reads it as the kingdom present in the King's person, with an earthly Davidic reign still to come. He quotes the claim that after the Beelzeboul charge the nearness of the kingdom is not proclaimed again, and so reads chapter 13 as new, previously unrevealed teaching. Readers outside the dispensational frame usually take chapter 13 as the same kingdom arriving in a hidden, contested form. The verb phthanō means "arrive, come upon, reach" ↗.
    13. The strong man (v. 29). The image echoes Isa 49:24–25 ↗, where plunder is taken from the mighty. Constable adds the Assumption of Moses 10:1, where Satan's end comes with God's kingdom. Chrysostom, as the patristic catena records, said the devil's strength grew from human sloth.
    14. The defaming of the Spirit (vv. 31–32) — readings held open. (a) Constable: attributing Jesus' Spirit-empowered works to Satan during his earthly ministry. In that exact form it cannot be committed today; the unforgivable sin now is rejection of Christ (John 3:18). He quotes Hagner: the Spirit is the dynamic behind the entire messianic ministry, which is why defaming the Spirit is graver than a word against the Son of Man. (b) Chrysostom, as one summary of him puts it, explained the difference this way: people did not yet know who the Son of Man was, but they had long experience of the Spirit through the prophets, so refusing the Spirit's evident work is inexcusable. (c) Other church writers identified it as heresy against the Spirit's deity, or as schism, simony, or grave sin after baptism. (d) A Jewish-frame reading (a synthesis offered here, not a claim about Matthew's intent): Numbers 15 separates unintentional sins, which can be atoned for, from sin "with a high hand" that reviles Yahweh (Num 15:30 ↗). The exodus generation "rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit" (Isa 63:10 ↗). Having just seen the Spirit put on the servant (12:18), the accusers call the Spirit's liberating work demonic. Mark 3:30 supplies the explanation "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit.'" Only Matthew adds "neither in this age nor in the one to come." The scene describes settled hostility to the Spirit's evident work, not the fear of having crossed a line; Constable points troubled readers to pastoral help.
    15. Rendering flag — "defaming" (v. 31). blasphēmia is injurious, defamatory speech, and "blasphemy" is only its loan-word (↗). "Slander" was avoided because the study already uses "the slanderer" for diabolos (4:1–11). translation-choices.md has no ruling, and the rendering of the verb at 9:3 has not been checked. Lane to decide.
    16. The idle word (v. 36). "Idle" is argos, from a-ergos, "without work" — a word with no deed (ergon) behind it. Constable: the words do not save or condemn by themselves; they show what is in the heart.
    17. The sign of Jonah (vv. 39–41). Mark Allan Powell distinguishes three levels: in the shared source behind Matthew and Luke the sign is Jonah's proclamation; in Luke it is Jonah's person and mission; in Matthew it is the prophet's stay in the fish's belly. ↗ Luz called the pericope extremely difficult and controversial (quoted in Waters, ↗). Matthew holds both levels: the death-and-return (v. 40) and the proclamation Nineveh answered (v. 41). Jonah is also the prophet who resented God's chesed toward outsiders (Jonah 4:2 ↗), the word behind "loyal-love" in v. 7 — a resonance, not a claimed allusion. The sign lands at 27:62–64, the only place the Pharisees appear in Matthew's passion, remembering "after three days."
    18. "Three days and three nights" (v. 40). The phrase is idiomatic. The Egyptian of 1 Sam 30:12–13 ↗ had not eaten for "three days and three nights" yet had fallen sick "three days ago." Esther calls a three-day fast and goes to the king "on the third day" (Esth 4:16–5:1 ↗). Rabbinic reckoning counted part of an onah (a day-or-night unit) as the whole. Matthew's own usage mixes "on the third day" (16:21) and "after three days" (27:63). Constable takes inclusive reckoning. Some insist on seventy-two literal hours, which requires moving the crucifixion day.
    19. "Heart of the earth" (v. 40). Jonah cries from the "belly of Sheol" and "the heart of the seas" (Jonah 2:2–3), which Matthew's "heart of the earth" likely echoes (Waters, above). The phrase may mean burial in the tomb (Constable) or descent to the realm of the dead; some find an ancient Near Eastern underworld-descent theme here. Held open.
    20. The swept, empty house (vv. 43–45). Constable reads it as demon repossession of those who stay neutral toward Jesus, representing the nation. Others read Israel's history: idolatry expelled by exile, a worse return later. A resonance offered here, not a claim about Matthew's intent: Ezek 36:25–27 ↗ pairs cleansing with "I will put my Spirit within you." This house is cleaned but not filled — directly after the Spirit on the servant (12:18) and the Spirit of God throwing out demons (12:28).
    21. Verse 47 (text). The verse is absent from Sinaiticus (original hand) and Vaticanus and from some early versions. The SBLGNT prints it with a variant marker; the UBS committee rated it "C," considerable doubt, allowing that it may have been dropped accidentally because vv. 46 and 47 end with the same Greek verb. Bracketed here. ↗
    22. Mother and brothers (vv. 46–50). Matthew omits Mark 3:21, where Jesus' family set out to seize him because people said he was out of his mind. Constable takes the brothers as Mary's later sons. He notes the alternatives, which remain the traditional readings of large parts of the church: sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage, or cousins. The doctrinal question is held open. "My brothers" returns at 25:40 and 28:10.

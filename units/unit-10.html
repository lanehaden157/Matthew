The Gospel According to Matthew · Study Translation

The Sending

idou egō apostellō hymas — look — I myself am sending y'all out
Unit 10 · Matthew 9:35–11:1 · the harvest, the twelve, and what it costs to be sent

Recurring roots — colour key

    Where this sits

    The second of Matthew's five discourses, with a narrative on-ramp pulled forward out of chapter 9.

    Matthew 9:35 repeats 4:23 almost word for word — teaching, proclaiming, healing — closing the bracket around everything Jesus has done since Galilee and opening a new phase in which he hands that same threefold work to other people. The unit runs past the chapter line in both directions: it takes the harvest-and-compassion summary (9:35–38) as its on-ramp and closes at the discourse formula of 11:1, kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iēsous ("and it happened, when Jesus finished"), the second of the five formulas that structure the whole gospel.

    Ezekiel 34 behind the shepherdless sheep

    Why 9:36 is a claim and not a mood.

    Ezekiel 34 (and its kin)Matthew 9:36–10:6
    Israel's shepherds feed themselves; the flock is scattered with none to search for it (Ezek. 34:5–6)The crowds are harassed and flung down, "like sheep having no shepherd"
    "I myself will search for my sheep… I will rescue them" (Ezek. 34:11–16)Jesus, gut-wrenched, moves toward the crowds himself
    Moses asks for a successor "so the congregation not be as sheep with no shepherd" (Num. 27:17)Twelve are summoned and given a share of his own authority

    The harvest and its workers · 9:35–38

    35And Jesus was going around all the towns and the villages, teaching in their gathering-places and heralding the good news of the kingdom and tending every disease and every weakness.1

    Nearly identical to 4:23 — Matthew closing a bracket. Everything since Galilee has been these three verbs; now they get handed on.

    36And seeing the crowds, he was wrenched in the gut over them, because they were harried and flung down like sheep that have no shepherd.2

    "Wrenched in the gut" renders esplanchnisthē, from splanchna, the inward organs — a bodily word, not a tender one.

    Mark 6:34 — the shepherdless sheep belong to the feeding, and produce teaching

    Mark 6:34And coming out he saw a large crowd, and he was moved in the guts over them, because they were like sheep not having a shepherd, and he began to teach them many things. (Immediately before the feeding of the five thousand.)

    In Mark the line belongs to the feeding of the five thousand and what it produces is teaching; Matthew lifts it out and sets it at the head of the mission discourse, where what it produces is workers. He also adds two participles Mark does not have — eskylmenoi kai errimmenoi, "mangled and thrown down" — which import the indictment of Ezekiel 34, where the shepherds of Israel are charged with exactly this treatment of the flock. Relocated and expanded, the compassion stops being a pastoral mood before a miracle and becomes the premise of an argument: the flock has been abused by its own leaders, so labourers have to be thrown out into the harvest.

    Greek (translit.)esplanchnisthē … eskylmenoi kai errimmenoi
    NASB"He felt compassion for them, because they were distressed and dispirited"
    Hart"he was moved to the depths … harried and prostrate"
    Noteerrimmenoi is from rhiptō, "to hurl, fling down" — a different verb from the ballō family tracked below, though the image rhymes.

    37Then he says to his learners: "The harvest indeed is much, but the workers are few.

    38So beg the Lord of the harvest that he may thrust out workers into his harvest."3

    The prayer is answered in the next verse by the ones who prayed it. ekbalē is the same violent verb used for expelling demons — being sent is not being invited.

    The twelve summoned and named · 10:1–4

    1And summoning his twelve learners, he gave them authority over unclean spirits, so as to throw them out and to tend every disease and every weakness.4

    The exousia ("authority") the crowds marvelled at in 7:29 and the centurion recognised in 8:9 is now handed over — a transfer without precedent in Israel's story.

    2And of the twelve emissaries the names are these: first Simon, the one called Rock, and Andrew his brother; and Jacob the son of Zebedee, and John his brother;

    "Emissary" renders apostolos, "one sent out" — the only time Matthew uses the word anywhere in the gospel.

    3Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew the tax-collector; Jacob the son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus;

    4Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot — the very one who handed him over.5

    The verb is paradidōmi, "to hand over, deliver up." Hold onto it: it fires three more times before this chapter ends, and every time it is the disciples being handed over.

    The charge: where, what, how · 10:5–15

    5These twelve Jesus sent out, ordering them, saying: "Onto a road of the nations do not go off, and into a town of Samaritans do not enter;

    6go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.6

    "Lost" is apolōlota, from apollymi — "to lose, to be destroyed." The same root turns reflexive at v. 39 and turns into a promise at v. 42.

    Mark 6:7–13 · Luke 9:1–6 — nobody else fences the mission

    Mark 6:7–8And he summons the twelve, and he began to send them out two by two, and he was giving them authority over the unclean spirits, and he charged them to take nothing for the road except a staff only — no bread, no bag, no copper in the belt.
    Mark 6:9…but to be shod with sandals, and not to put on two tunics. (Matthew forbids both the staff and the sandals, 10:10.)
    Luke 9:2And he sent them out to proclaim the kingdom of God and to heal the weak. (No territorial instruction of any kind.)

    Neither Mark nor Luke restricts where the Twelve may go. Matthew alone opens the charge with a prohibition — no road of the nations, no Samaritan town — and a redirection to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel," a phrase he will put in Jesus' mouth again at 15:24. This is the sharpest unresolved tension in the gospel: the same book that closes with "make disciples of all the nations" (28:19) has Jesus fencing the mission off from those nations here, and Matthew makes no attempt to harmonize the two — he stages them, leaves the fence standing until the resurrection, and lets the reader feel the order change.

    Greek (translit.)eis hodon ethnōn mē apelthēte
    NASB"Do not go on a road to Gentiles"
    Reading AConstable: the kingdom is offered to Israel exclusively at this stage; the nations mission is held back to a later arrangement.
    Reading BFrance / Bible Project: a deliberately bounded first stage of one mission that was always headed for "all the nations" (28:19) — expanded, not replaced.

    7And going, herald, saying that 'The kingdom of the skies has drawn near.'7

    Word for word what John said (3:2) and what Jesus said (4:17). The Twelve inherit the sentence exactly.

    8Tend the weak, raise the dead, cleanse the skin-diseased, throw out demons. As a gift y'all received; as a gift give.8

    Four commands matching four things just narrated in chapters 8–9. They are sent to reproduce what they watched.

    9Do not acquire gold, nor silver, nor copper for y'all's belts;

    10no travelling-bag for the road, nor two tunics, nor sandals, nor a staff — for the worker is worthy of his food.9

    The kit of the self-sufficient wandering teacher, itemised and forbidden. Dependence on hospitality is part of the message, not an inconvenience attached to it.

    11And into whatever town or village y'all enter, search out who in it is worthy, and stay there until y'all go out.

    12And entering into the house, greet it;

    13and if indeed the house is worthy, let y'all's peace come upon it; but if it is not worthy, let y'all's peace turn back to y'all.

    Peace is treated as a thing with weight that can be set down and picked back up — an almost physical blessing, not a wish.

    14And whoever does not receive y'all nor hear y'all's words — going out of that house or that town, shake off the dust of y'all's feet.10

    15Amen I say to y'all: it will be more bearable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah on the day of judgement than for that town.

    The free gift of v. 8 turns out to carry the heaviest possible stakes in its refusal.

    Sheep among wolves · 10:16–25

    16"Look — I myself am sending y'all out as sheep in the middle of wolves. So become shrewd as the snakes and unalloyed as the doves.11

    "Unalloyed" is akeraioi, "unmixed, without additive" — a metalworker's word. Cunning and purity held together, neither cancelling the other.

    17And beware of the people; for they will hand y'all over to councils, and in their gathering-places they will flog y'all;

    18and before governors and even kings y'all will be led, for my sake, as a testimony to them and to the nations.12

    The door closed at v. 5 stands slightly ajar here — the nations are already, incidentally, an audience.

    19And whenever they hand y'all over, do not be anxious how or what y'all should speak; for it will be given to y'all in that hour what to speak;

    20for it is not y'all who are the ones speaking, but the Spirit of y'all's Father, the one speaking in y'all.

    21And brother will hand over brother to death, and a father a child; and children will rise up against parents and put them to death.

    22And y'all will be hated by everyone on account of my name; but the one enduring to the end — this one will be saved.13

    Rescue tied not to a crisis-moment of trust but to staying put under pressure over time.

    Mark 13:9–13 · Matthew 24:9–14 — signs of the end, moved into the mission charge

    Mark 13:9But watch yourselves. They will hand y'all over to councils, and y'all will be beaten in synagogues, and y'all will be stood before governors and kings for my sake, for a testimony to them. (Inside the Olivet discourse, among the signs of the end.)
    Mark 13:11…do not be anxious beforehand what y'all should say, but whatever is given y'all in that hour, say that; for it is not y'all who are speaking but the holy Spirit.
    Matt 24:9, 13Then they will hand y'all over to pressure and will kill y'all, and y'all will be hated by all the nations because of my name… but the one who endures to the end, this one will be saved. (Matthew's own second, shorter version.)

    In Mark this paragraph sits inside the Olivet discourse among the signs of the end; Matthew lifts it out, drops it into the mission charge nearly verbatim, and then writes a second, shorter version back into his own Olivet discourse at 24:9–14 — so he carries the material twice, once as the working condition of the mission and once as a sign of the end. On Mark priority the relocation is the strongest evidence in the gospel that Matthew reads the sending of the Twelve as the church's standing assignment rather than one Galilean errand: what Mark reserves for the last days, Matthew hands to the disciples on day one. He also substitutes "the Spirit of y'all's Father speaking in y'all" for Mark's "holy Spirit," which keeps the promise inside the Sermon's Father-language rather than importing a separate agent.

    23And whenever they pursue y'all in this town, flee to the other; for amen I say to y'all, y'all will not finish the towns of Israel until the Son of Man comes.14

    Greek (translit.)heōs an elthē ho huios tou anthrōpou
    NASB"until the Son of Man comes"
    Reading AConstable: points past the historical mission entirely, to a future Israel-focused preaching before the literal return.
    Reading BFrance, Davies & Allison, and others: variously the fall of Jerusalem, Jesus rejoining the Twelve before their circuit ends, or Matthean prophetic compression. Genuinely unsettled.

    24A learner is not above the teacher, nor a slave above his lord.

    25Enough for the learner that he become as his teacher, and the slave as his lord. If they called the master of the house Beelzeboul, how much more his household!15

    The slander of 9:34 — that he throws out demons by the ruler of demons — is expected to transfer straight onto them.

    Do not fear · 10:26–33

    26So do not fear them; for nothing is covered that will not be uncovered, and hidden that will not be known.

    27What I say to y'all in the dark, speak in the light; and what y'all hear in the ear, herald upon the rooftops.

    28And do not fear those killing the body but unable to kill the life; fear rather the one able to destroy both life and body in Gehenna.16

    29Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? And not one of them will fall onto the ground apart from y'all's Father.

    30And of y'all even the hairs of the head are all numbered.

    31So do not fear; y'all are worth more than many sparrows.

    Four commands against fear in six verses. The argument runs upward from the smallest thing God attends to — not from anything the disciples have achieved.

    32Everyone, then, who will acknowledge me before the people, I too will acknowledge him before my Father in the skies;

    33and whoever disowns me before the people, I too will disown him before my Father in the skies.17

    Courtroom language. The verb for "disown" is the one Peter's denial will be told with at 26:70–75.

    Not peace but a sword · 10:34–39

    34"Do not suppose that I came to throw peace onto the earth; I did not come to throw peace, but a sword.18

    The same root that expelled demons and thrust out workers now governs peace itself — and governs its refusal.

    35For I came to divide a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a bride against her mother-in-law;

    36and a man's enemies will be the members of his own household." (Mic 7:6)19

    SourceMicah 7:6 — son dishonours father, daughter rises against mother, a man's enemies are his household
    Micah's settingA lament: society so corrupt that even kinship loyalty has collapsed.
    Matthew's turnThe same collapse, but the fracture line is allegiance to Jesus — not corruption but claim.

    37The one loving father or mother above me is not worthy of me; and the one loving son or daughter above me is not worthy of me;

    38and whoever does not take up his cross and follow behind me is not worthy of me.20

    The first appearance of stauros ("cross, execution-stake") in Matthew — stated as a general condition of following, long before it is a prediction about Jesus.

    39The one finding his life will lose it, and the one losing his life for my sake will find it.21

    The chapter opened with sheep that were lost by neglect (v. 6); here the losing becomes something a person does on purpose.

    Whoever receives y'all · 10:40–11:1

    40"The one receiving y'all receives me, and the one receiving me receives the one who sent me.22

    41The one receiving a prophet in the name of a prophet will take a prophet's wage; and the one receiving a just person in the name of a just person will take a just person's wage.

    42And whoever gives one of these small ones only a cup of cold water to drink, in the name of a learner — amen I say to y'all, he will certainly not lose his wage."23

    11:1And it happened, when Jesus finished laying out orders for his twelve learners, he moved on from there to teach and to herald in their towns.24

    The formula closes the discourse but not the work — he goes and does exactly what he sent them to do.

    Notes

    1. going around all the towns (9:35). Near-verbatim repetition of 4:23, forming an inclusio around the teaching (chs. 5–7) and the healing (chs. 8–9). "Gathering-places" renders synagōgais — a synagogue is literally a "gathering-together." "Tending" renders therapeuōn, the root behind English "therapy" — service and care, not primarily miracle-language. ↗
    2. wrenched in the gut (9:36). esplanchnisthē, from splanchna ("the inward organs"). "Like sheep having no shepherd" runs through Numbers 27:17, 1 Kings 22:17, and 2 Chronicles 18:16, but lands hardest on Ezekiel 34, where God indicts Israel's shepherds for scattering the flock and promises to shepherd it himself. Matthew is making a claim about who has arrived, not merely reporting a mood. ↗
    3. thrust out workers (9:38). ekbalē — a compound of ballō ("throw, cast"), the tracked throw-root. In Unit 9 this family expelled demons and drove out mourners; here it governs what God does to the people praying the prayer. Three occurrences in four verses (9:38, 10:1, 10:8) make this a genuine cluster, and 10:34 adds a fourth in a different key.
    4. gave them authority (10:1). exousia — the delegated right to act, tracked from 7:29 (the crowds astonished at his authority) through 8:9 (the centurion, who understands delegated authority professionally) and 9:6–8, and out to 28:18. This verse is the transfer point, and it belongs in that chain. Constable notes, rightly, that this has no precedent in Israel's story: Moses and Elijah did not delegate miraculous power to disciples while still present; Elisha received Elijah's mantle only after Elijah was taken up.
    5. handed him over (10:4). paradidōmi — literally "to give alongside/across," used for legal delivery into custody as much as for treachery. Judas is introduced by this verb, and then it is used three more times in this chapter (vv. 17, 19, 21) — every time for what will be done to the disciples. It runs on to 26:15, 21, 45–48 and through the trial narratives.
    6. lost sheep of the house of Israel (10:6). The phrase gathers Isaiah 53:6, Jeremiah 50:6, and Ezekiel 34. On the restriction itself: the two readings in the compare box are not disputes about what the sentence says but about what kind of pause it marks. Current Matthew scholarship increasingly reads chapter 10 and 28:19 as one stable, expanding missiology rather than two competing programmes — but the tension is real and Matthew does not smooth it. ↗
    7. has drawn near (10:7). ēngiken is a perfect-tense form: it describes a state already entered and still holding, not a single completed action. Plainly: "has come near and is near," not "came near once." Identical in form to 3:2 (John) and 4:17 (Jesus).
    8. As a gift y'all received; as a gift give (10:8). dōrean elabete, dōrean dote — the adverb means "as a gift, gratis, without payment." The repetition is the whole argument: unearned power cannot be sold. The four commands map onto four miracles just narrated — the sick (8:14–16), the dead (9:18–26), a skin-diseased man (8:1–4), and demons (8:28–34, 9:32–33).
    9. no bag, no staff (10:9–10). The verb is ktēsēsthe, "acquire, procure" — the ban is on outfitting for the trip, not on emptying one's pockets first. The itemised kit closely matches that of the wandering Cynic teachers of the period, who characteristically carried a bag (sometimes for begging) and a staff as marks of self-sufficient itinerancy; the prohibition reads as a deliberate inversion of that recognisable figure. Speculative but worth noting: m. Berakhot 9:5 forbids entering the Temple Mount with staff, sandals, or money-belt — which would cast the mission's stripped kit as temple-approach holiness. Attractive, but not something to build weight on.
    10. shake off the dust (10:14). A gesture of disassociation — the town's very soil is refused. Note that the disciples are told to leave, not to argue: the refusal is allowed to stand.
    11. shrewd as the snakes (10:16). phronimoi — the tracked shrewd-root, from the wise builder of 7:24 out to the faithful and shrewd slave (24:45) and the five shrewd virgins (25:2). This occurrence belongs in that chain. akeraioi ("unmixed, unalloyed") is used of unadulterated metal or unwatered wine. The pairing asks for two traits that do not naturally sit together.
    12. and to the nations (10:18). ethnesin — the tracked nations-root, opened at 4:15 ("Galilee of the nations") and closed at 28:19. Its appearance here, on the far side of the v. 5 prohibition, is a small crack in an otherwise sealed door: the nations are already, incidentally, an audience.
    13. will be saved (10:22). sōthēsetai, from sōzō — the tracked save-root, running from 1:21 ("he will save his people") through the woman healed at 9:21–22 and the sinking Peter at 14:30 to the mockery at the cross (27:42). Striking here for what it attaches to: not a moment of crisis-trust but sustained endurance.
    14. until the Son of Man comes (10:23). ho huios tou anthrōpou — the tracked Son of Man title, from Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds and is given everlasting dominion. Whatever the verse means, it is unambiguously an occurrence of the title and belongs in that thread's chain. The interpretive question is genuinely open; see the compare box. ↗
    15. Beelzeboul (10:25). Probably from Hebrew baal zebul, "Prince Baal" — Baal being the chief Canaanite deity, recast by Israelite usage as a name for the satanic. The charge itself was made at 9:34 and will be made again at 12:24.
    16. life and body (10:28). psychē is the animating life, the self — not a disembodied soul in the later philosophical sense; rendering it "life" keeps it consistent with v. 39, where the same word appears four times. "Gehenna" is geenna, the Valley of Hinnom south of Jerusalem, associated with child sacrifice under Ahaz and Manasseh (2 Kings 23:10; Jer. 7:31) and thereafter with judgement-fire.
    17. acknowledge / disown (10:32–33). homologeō ("say the same thing as, confess") and arneomai ("deny, disown") — both at home in courtrooms. arneomai is the verb Matthew uses for Peter at 26:70–75, which makes this saying quietly ominous in retrospect.
    18. throw peace (10:34). balein — the tracked throw-root again, and in a startlingly different register: what is (not) thrown here is not a demon or a worker but peace itself. Worth noting that the negation is about Jesus' immediate effect, not his ultimate purpose; the same gospel calls peacemakers blessed (5:9).
    19. a man's enemies (10:35–36). Near-verbatim Micah 7:6. In Micah the collapse of family loyalty is a symptom of national corruption; Matthew reassigns the same fracture to allegiance to Jesus. The shift is deliberate and worth sitting with rather than softening. ↗
    20. take up his cross (10:38). stauros — the first occurrence in Matthew, well before any passion prediction, and framed as a general condition of following. Recurs at 16:24, right at the second "from that time" hinge, and then at the crucifixion itself (27:32, 40, 42). "Follow behind" is akolouthei opisō — the tracked follow-root, a discipleship keyword since 4:20.
    21. finding and losing (10:39). A tight chiasm: find–lose / lose–find. apolesei and apolesas are from apollymi, the same root as the "lost" sheep of v. 6 — the chapter's third and most demanding use of it follows in v. 42.
    22. receiving y'all receives me (10:40). About as direct a statement of the Jewish legal principle of agency as the New Testament contains. The rabbinic maxim — a person's shaliach ("agent, one sent") is as the person himself — is close to word-for-word what is claimed here theologically, and it explains retroactively why the whole discourse has treated the disciples' reception or rejection as equivalent to Jesus' own. ↗
    23. will not lose his wage (10:42). misthos — "wage, pay," the same economic word used of the worker at v. 10. apolesē is apollymi a third time in the chapter: lost sheep (v. 6), losing one's life (v. 39), a wage that cannot be lost (v. 42) — one root doing three different kinds of work across one discourse.
    24. when Jesus finished (11:1). kai egeneto hote etelesen ho Iēsous — the second of the five discourse-closing formulas (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1) that Bacon read as marking Matthew's five books, a new Pentateuch with Jesus as the new Moses. ↗

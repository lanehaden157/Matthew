The heart of the discourse — a concentric ring · 13:24–50

The strongest verifiable pattern in the chapter: the crowd parables and the learner parables mirror each other around Matthew's own comment on what parables are. Every pairing rests on shared Greek words, not topic alone.

A24–30
The darnel sown "in the midst" (ana meson) of the wheat; let both grow until the harvest — mixed field, delayed sorting
B31–33
Twins that "took": mustard "which a man took" (labōn) · leaven "which a woman took" (labousa) — small, placed, total effect
C34–43
"Things hidden since the foundation" (Ps 78:2); into the house; the darnel explained, ending in the furnace refrain — what parables are, and the first key
B′44–46
Twins that "found": treasure and pearl — found (heurōn), went, sold "all he had," bought — the kingdom's worth
A′47–50
The dragnet sorted; the evil taken "out of the midst" (ek mesou); the furnace refrain repeated word for word (13:42 = 13:50) — mixed catch, final sorting

The frames around the ring

Outer correspondences, weighted honestly. The full nine-part chiasm sometimes proposed for this chapter holds at its core and weakens toward its edges; the pairs below are the ones the Greek actually supports.

The sower goes out · 13:1–9

1On that day Jesus, going out of the house, was sitting beside the sea.

2And many crowds were gathered to him, so that he stepped into a boat to sit, and the whole crowd was standing on the beach.

3And he spoke to them many things in parables, saying: "Look — the sower went out to sow.1

The parabolē is a "throwing-beside," one thing set alongside another; behind it stands the Hebrew mashal — proverb, riddle, taunt, story. "Went out" repeats the verb of verse 1: Jesus went out, and the sower went out.

4And as he sowed, some fell beside the road, and the birds came and devoured them.2

5Others fell on the rock-like ground, where it did not have much earth, and immediately it sprang up, because of not having depth of earth.

6But when the sun had risen it was scorched, and because of not having root it withered.3

One verb, anatellō ("rise, spring up"), for both the sprout and the sun: the seed "rose," the sun "rose," and the thing that should make a plant grow kills the one without a root.

7Others fell on the thorns, and the thorns came up and choked them.

8Others fell on the good earth and kept giving fruit — one a hundred, one sixty, one thirty.4

Gen 26:12 — "Isaac sowed in that land and found in that year barley yielding a hundredfold, and the LORD blessed him": a patriarch's harvest in a year of famine.

9The one having ears — let him hear."

Why parables · 13:10–17

10And the learners came near and said to him, "Why do you speak to them in parables?"

11He answered and said to them, "Because to y'all it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of the skies, but to those it has not been given.5

"It has been given" names no giver — a "divine passive," the reverent Jewish way of saying God acted — and its tense (perfect) means a gift given that still stands.
Deut 29:4 (29:3 Greek) — "the LORD has not given y'all a heart to know, or eyes to see, or ears to hear, to this day." Now, to these, it has been given.

12For whoever has, it will be given to him and he will overflow; but whoever does not have, even what he has will be taken from him.6

13For this reason I speak to them in parables: because seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor do they understand.

syniēmi, "understand," is literally "throw together" — bringing two things into one. A parable throws things side by side; understanding brings them together.

Mark 4:11–12 · Luke 8:10 — "because" or "in order that"

Mark 4:12"… everything comes in parables, hina — in order that — seeing they may see and not perceive … lest they turn and it be forgiven them."
Luke 8:10"… to the rest in parables, hina — in order that — seeing they may not see and hearing they may not understand."
Matt 13:13"I speak to them in parables hoti — because — seeing they do not see …"

Mark and Luke make the blindness sound like the parables' purpose; Matthew makes it their cause. Yet Matthew keeps "it has not been given" two verses earlier, so divine gift and human refusal stand side by side, unranked. Mark's "forgiven" also matches the Aramaic synagogue paraphrase of Isaiah; Matthew goes on to quote the Greek text, which says "heal."

14And in them the prophecy of Isaiah is being filled up, which says: 'With hearing y'all will hear and by no means understand; and seeing y'all will see and by no means perceive.

"With hearing y'all will hear" imitates a Hebrew idiom, the verb doubled for intensity (shim'u shamoa'). The Hebrew is a command — "hear indeed!" — which the Greek text turned into a prediction.

15For the heart of this people has been made thick, and with their ears they heard heavily, and their eyes they have shut — lest they perceive with their eyes and hear with their ears and understand with the heart and turn back — and I will heal them.' (Isa 6:9–10)7

Greek (translit.)epachynthē gar hē kardia tou laou toutou … tous ophthalmous autōn ekammysan … kai iasomai autous
NASBFor the heart of this people has become dull … and they have closed their eyes, otherwise they would … return, and I would heal them.
Text-formHebrew: commands to the prophet — "make fat the heart (hashmen) … make heavy the ears (hakhbed) … smear shut the eyes." Greek Old Testament, followed by Matthew: descriptions — the heart "has been made thick," and "they" have shut their own eyes. "I will heal" is a plain future after a string of "lest" verbs.
Exod 8:15 (8:11 Greek) — "Pharaoh made his heart heavy and did not listen." Isaiah's "make the ears heavy" is Pharaoh's word (Hebrew kabed, Greek baryn- / bareōs): the people once rescued from a heavy-hearted Pharaoh now stand where he stood.

16But y'all's eyes are blessed, because they see, and y'all's ears, because they hear.

17For amen I say to y'all: many prophets and righteous ones longed to perceive what y'all see and did not perceive, and to hear what y'all hear and did not hear.8

The sower explained · 13:18–23

18Y'all, then, hear the parable of the sower.

19Anyone hearing the word of the kingdom and not understanding — the evil one comes and snatches what was sown in his heart. This is the one sown beside the road.9

Matthew never says "the seed is the word." Four times he says "this is the one sown": the hearer is the seed as sown — which prepares for 13:38, "the good seed are the sons of the kingdom."

Mark 4:14–15 · Luke 8:11–12 — who snatches, and what is sown

Mark 4:14–15"The sower sows the word … Satan comes and takes away the word sown in them."
Luke 8:11–12"The seed is the word of God … then the slanderer comes and takes away the word from their heart."

Matthew alone adds "and not understanding," making the first soil a failure of the Isaiah verb; alone calls the thief "the evil one," the Lord's Prayer's word (6:13); and alone identifies the person, not the word, as the thing sown.

20And the one sown on the rock-like ground — this is the one hearing the word and immediately receiving it with joy;

21but he does not have root in himself; rather, he is for-a-season, and when pressure or persecution comes because of the word, immediately he is tripped up.10

proskairos, "for-a-season, temporary"; thlipsis, "pressure, crushing" — the word that becomes "the great pressure" of 24:21.

22And the one sown into the thorns — this is the one hearing the word, and the care that pulls apart, of the age, and the deceit of wealth choke the word, and it becomes fruitless.

23And the one sown on the good earth — this is the one hearing the word and understanding, who indeed bears fruit and makes — one a hundred, one sixty, one thirty."

Darnel among the wheat · 13:24–30

24Another parable he set before them, saying: "The kingdom of the skies was likened to a man sowing good seed in his field.11

"Set before" (paratithēmi) is the verb for putting food on a table — a "throwing-beside" set beside them.

25But while the people were sleeping, his enemy came and sowed darnel over, in the midst of the wheat, and went away.12

zizania: almost certainly darnel, a grass that is nearly indistinguishable from wheat until the heads form, and whose grain is toxic.

26And when the blade sprouted and made fruit, then the darnel also showed itself.

Gen 1:11 — "let the earth sprout grass, sowing seed … making fruit": four shared words (blastanō, chortos, sperma, karpon poieō) put the good field in the first garden.

27And the slaves of the master of the house came near and said to him, 'Master, did you not sow good seed in your field? From where, then, does it have darnel?'

28And he said to them, 'An enemy person did this.' And the slaves say to him, 'Do you want us, then, to go off and collect them?'

29But he says, 'No — lest while collecting the darnel y'all uproot the wheat along with them.13

30Let both grow together until the harvest; and in the season of the harvest I will say to the reapers: Collect first the darnel and bind them into bundles for burning them up, but the wheat gather into my barn.'"14

Matt 3:12 — John the Immerser: "he will gather his wheat into the barn, but the chaff he will burn up." Four shared words (synagō, sitos, apothēkē, katakaiō): John's harvest, announced as imminent, is here deferred to the harvest at the end. Matt 6:26 shares the rest of the vocabulary — the birds "neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns."

Mustard and leaven · 13:31–33

31Another parable he set before them, saying: "The kingdom of the skies is like a grain of mustard, which a man took and sowed in his field —

32which is smaller than all the seeds, but when it has grown it is greater than the garden-plants and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the sky come and nest in its branches."15

"Nest," kataskēnoō, is built on skēnē, "tent" — the Greek Old Testament's word for the tabernacle.
Ps 104:12 (103:12 Greek) — "the birds of the sky will nest"; Dan 4:21 (Theodotion) — "in its branches nested the birds of the sky." Matthew's line is essentially the two joined: the tree of a great kingdom that shelters the nations.

33Another parable he spoke to them: "The kingdom of the skies is like leaven, which a woman took and hid in three seahs of flour, until the whole was leavened."16

Three seahs is somewhere around 25–40 litres of flour — bread for a hundred or more. She does not "mix" the leaven; she "hides" it.
Gen 18:6 — Abraham to Sarah at Mamre: "three measures (Hebrew: three seahs) of fine flour … make hidden-cakes" (enkryphias), bread for a divine visit. Exod 12:39 — at the exodus "they baked hidden-cakes, unleavened, for it was not leavened." The exodus bread was hidden-baked and not leavened; the kingdom's leaven is hidden and leavens everything.

Hidden since the foundation · 13:34–35

34All these things Jesus spoke in parables to the crowds, and without a parable he was speaking nothing to them —

A small chiasm in the Greek: spoke — in parables — without a parable — was speaking.

35so that what was spoken through the prophet might be filled full, saying: 'I will open my mouth in parables; I will pour out things hidden since the foundation.' (Ps 78:2)17

Greek (translit.)anoixō en parabolais to stoma mou, ereuxomai kekrymmena apo katabolēs
NASBI will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden since the foundation of the world.
Text-formHebrew Ps 78:2: "I will pour forth (abbi'ah) riddles (chidot) from of old (minni-qedem)." Greek Old Testament: "I will utter problems from the beginning." Matthew keeps the Greek first line, then chooses a gushing verb (ereugomai) closer to the Hebrew, makes the riddles "hidden things," and pushes "from of old" back to "from the foundation." Some manuscripts add "of the world"; some read "through Isaiah the prophet."

The darnel explained · 13:36–43

36Then, leaving the crowds, he went into the house. And his learners came near to him, saying, "Make clear to us the parable of the darnel of the field."18

37He answered and said, "The one sowing the good seed is the Son of Man;

38and the field is the world; and the good seed — these are the sons of the kingdom; and the darnel are the sons of the evil one;19

"Sons of" in Hebrew idiom means "belonging to, sharing the character of." At 8:12 "the sons of the kingdom" were the presumptive heirs thrown out; here the title names the good seed.

39and the enemy who sowed them is the slanderer; and the harvest is the completion of an age; and the reapers are angels.20

synteleia, "completion": syn "together" + telos "end, goal" — the point where things are brought together to their end.

40So just as the darnel is collected and burned up with fire, so it will be at the completion of the age.

41The Son of Man will send his angels, and they will collect out of his kingdom all the trip-traps and those doing lawlessness,21

Mal 4:1 (3:19 Greek) — "the day comes, burning like an oven, and all … who do lawless things will be straw … it will leave them neither root nor branch."

42and they will throw them into the furnace of fire; there will be the weeping and the grinding of the teeth.22

Dan 3:6 (Theodotion) — "he will be thrown into the furnace of fire, the burning one": the phrase of Nebuchadnezzar's furnace, where the faithful were thrown and walked unharmed. Here the same furnace receives the lawless.

43Then the righteous will blaze out like the sun in the kingdom of their Father. The one having ears — let him hear.

Dan 12:3 (Theodotion) — "those who understand will blaze out (hoi synientes eklampsousin) like the brightness of the firmament," at the resurrection of 12:2. Daniel's shining ones are "the understanding" — the verb that divided the soils.

Treasure, pearl, dragnet · 13:44–50

44The kingdom of the skies is like treasure hidden in the field, which a man found and hid; and from his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field.23

"Goes … sells … buys" are present tense inside a past-tense story — the storyteller's vivid present, speeding the action.
Matt 19:21–22 — "go, sell your possessions … and you will have treasure in the skies" (hypagō, pōleō, thēsauros); the rich young man "went away grieving." Joy and selling here, grief and refusal there.

45Again, the kingdom of the skies is like a merchant man seeking fine pearls;

46and having found one very precious pearl, he went off and has sold all that he had, and bought it.24

"Has sold" is perfect tense between two plain pasts: the sale is done, and it stays done.

47Again, the kingdom of the skies is like a dragnet thrown into the sea and gathering from every kind;25

Ezek 47:10 — fishers (halieis) spreading seines (sagēnai) on a sea healed by the river from God's house, its fish "very many." Hab 1:15 — the conqueror "gathered them in his seines" (synēgagen … en tais sagēnais). The net of restoration and the net of judgment, both behind one dragnet.

48which, when it was filled full, they hauled up onto the beach, and sitting down they collected the good into containers, but the rotten they threw outside.26

49So it will be at the completion of the age: the angels will go out and separate the evil from the midst of the righteous,

"From the midst of" (ek mesou) reverses 13:25's "in the midst of" (ana meson): sown into the middle, taken out of the middle.

50and they will throw them into the furnace of fire; there will be the weeping and the grinding of the teeth.

The scribe made a learner · 13:51–53

51"Have y'all understood all these things?" They say to him, "Yes."

52And he said to them, "For this reason every scribe made a learner for the kingdom of the skies is like a man, a master of the house, who throws out of his treasure-store new things and old."27

mathēteutheis: a passive participle — "having been made a learner" by someone else. Its first appearance in Matthew; its last is the commission, "make learners of all the nations" (28:19).
Matt 12:35 — "the good person throws out of the good treasure-store good things" (ek tou thēsaurou … ekballei); Matt 10:25 — the "master of the house" is Jesus himself, and "it is enough for the learner to become like his teacher"; Matt 28:19 — mathēteusate, "make learners."

53And it happened, when Jesus completed these parables, he moved on from there.28

Notes

  1. Parable (v. 3). The Greek Old Testament renders Hebrew mashal with parabolē in the large majority of cases. Mashal covers the proverbs of Solomon, the taunt against Babylon (Isa 14), Ezekiel's riddling allegories (his hearers call him "a maker of parables," Ezek 20:49), and Nathan's story that springs shut on David (2 Sam 12) — a story that looks like it is about someone else until the hearer finds himself inside it. Parabolē occurs twelve times in 13:3–53. It is a derivative of paraballō, "throw beside," from the same family as the throw thread, but it is kept as its own local root rather than tagged throw; flagged for a ruling. ↗
  2. The birds (v. 4). In Jubilees 11:11–24, a second-century BC retelling of Genesis, the demonic prince Mastema sends ravens and birds to devour the seed sown in the land, and the boy Abram drives them off. Birds as the devil's agents eating sown seed were therefore an image already current, which makes "the evil one comes and snatches" (13:19) less arbitrary than it may look. The birds return at 13:32, where most readers take them as the nations sheltering (Daniel 4, Ezekiel 17, 31) and a common dispensational reading takes them as sinister again. ↗
  3. Rock-like ground; rising (vv. 5–6). Petrōdēs, "rock-like," is built on petra, "rock" (the rock thread: the wise builder, 7:24; "on this rock," 16:18), but it is a different lexeme and is left untagged by ruling: a house wants rock under it; a seed wants depth. The verb anatellō links the scorching sun to Isaiah 9's light that "dawned" on Galilee (4:16) and to the Father who "makes his sun rise on evil and good" (5:45). Its compound exanatellō first appears in Genesis 2:9, where God makes every tree of Eden spring up; and Genesis 3:18 has the thorns "spring up" from the cursed ground — both words of 13:5–7 in one verse of the Fall. ↗
  4. A hundredfold (v. 8). A Roman agricultural writer of the first century BC gives normal grain yields of about ten- to fifteenfold and names Gadara, near the Sea of Galilee, as famous for a hundredfold. Spectacular, not fantastic. Mark has the numbers rising (thirty, sixty, a hundred); Matthew has them falling. No explanation of the reversal commands agreement. The fruit thread's related words akarpos, "fruitless" (v. 22), and karpophoreō, "bear fruit" (v. 23), are tagged fruit as transparent compounds of karpos.
  5. Secrets (v. 11). Mystērion is the Greek of Daniel's Aramaic raz: the dream-secret of the kingdoms and the stone that becomes a mountain, revealed by "a God in the sky who reveals mysteries" (Dan 2:28). At Qumran the Habakkuk commentary says God made known to the Teacher of Righteousness "all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets" (1QpHab 7:4–5). One reading, the dispensational one, takes these mysteries as truth never before revealed — an unforeseen interval between the Messiah's advents, a "mystery form" of the kingdom forced by Israel's rejection. Another takes them as the long-promised kingdom arriving in an unexpected, hidden shape. Daniel's usage leans toward "hidden, not unknown to God's plan"; 13:35 will call them "hidden since the foundation." Held open. ↗
  6. Having (v. 12). Echō, "have," runs through the discourse: the rocky ground "did not have" earth, depth, root (vv. 5–6); "the one having ears" (vv. 9, 43); whoever has, whoever does not have (v. 12); "he does not have root in himself" (v. 21); "from where does it have darnel?" (v. 27); the finder sells "all that he has" (v. 44), the merchant "all that he had" (v. 46). The proverb is about what one has as a receiver, and the discourse ends by defining having as letting go of all one has. The saying returns at 25:29, after the talents.
  7. Isaiah 6 in Matthew (vv. 14–15). Matthew reproduces the Greek Old Testament almost word for word, introduced by the rare anaplēroō, "filled up to the brim," in the present tense — being filled up now, in them. (Kept as its own local root, not the fulfill thread, by the compound-verb convention.) Three things the Greek did to the Hebrew: commands became descriptions; the people, not the prophet, shut their eyes; and God became the healer ("I will heal"). The heaviness root ties the verse to Pharaoh's heart in the plagues. Mark 4:12 instead ends "lest … it be forgiven them," matching the Aramaic synagogue paraphrase of Isaiah. Acts 28:26–27 quotes the identical Greek as the last Scripture in Acts; John 12:40 makes God the one who blinds; Paul fuses Isaiah 29:10 with Deuteronomy 29:4 (Rom 11:8) and speaks of a partial hardening "until the fullness of the nations comes in" (Rom 11:25). Isaiah's own chapter ends, in Hebrew, with "the holy seed is its stump" (6:13) — a line the Greek Old Testament lacks. Some scholars have suspected 13:14–15 is a later insertion, on style alone; no manuscript omits it. Epistrephō is rendered "turn back," keeping it distinct from metanoia, "turn," and letting the Hebrew shuv (return, including return from exile) stay audible — flagged as a new standing rendering. ↗ ↗
  8. Prophets and righteous ones (vv. 16–17). Luke has "prophets and kings" (Luke 10:24); "prophets and righteous" is Matthew's own pair (10:41; 23:29). The beatitude belongs to a recognizable form: the Psalms of Solomon, a first-century BC Jewish collection, twice bless "those born in those days, to see the good things" God will do (17:44; 18:6). Jesus tells his learners they are that generation. ↗
  9. The soils and the Shema (vv. 19–22). A Jewish-background reading maps the three failing soils onto the Shema, "love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might" (Deut 6:5), as the rabbis read it (Mishnah Berakhot 9:5): the heart (the word snatched "from his heart"), the soul read as martyrdom (persecution), and "might" (Hebrew me'od) read as wealth (the deceit of wealth). The Greek Old Testament has "power" for me'od, so the wealth reading is Hebrew and rabbinic; the Mishnah is later than Matthew, though this reading is widely thought older. Matthew's own Shema citation (22:37) is a second anchor. Harpazō, "snatch," links 11:12 (the violent snatch the kingdom), 12:29 (the strong man's goods snatched) and here. ↗
  10. For-a-season (v. 21). Outside the Synoptics, proskairos appears in 4 Maccabees 15, where the martyrs' mother refuses the tyrant's offer of "temporary" safety for her seven sons — Jewish martyr vocabulary for the soil that fails under persecution. Later reuse: 2 Cor 4:18; Heb 11:25. Readers have noticed that the rocky hearer's profile — immediately with joy, immediately tripped up — fits Peter's night of denial (26:31–35); Matthew does not draw the line, and it is left as a reader's observation.
  11. "Was likened" (v. 24). The aorist passive hōmoiōthē has been read, in a dispensational reading, as marking a change in the kingdom program after Israel's rejection. Matthew uses the same form to open the unforgiving slave (18:23) and the wedding banquet (22:2), where no program change is in view; it is more likely a stock opener, probably reflecting an Aramaic formula: "it is with the kingdom as with the following case."
  12. Darnel (v. 25). The Mishnah rules that "wheat and zunin are not diverse kinds with one another" (Kil'ayim 1:1), reflecting a belief, also found in Greek botany, that wheat could degenerate into darnel. Zizanion is probably a loan from that Semitic word. The servants' puzzlement assumes the field went bad; the master denies degeneration and names sabotage. Echthros, "enemy," first appeared in "love y'all's enemies" (5:43–44). ↗
  13. Do not uproot (v. 29). Uprooting is the divine and prophetic prerogative — Jeremiah is appointed "to uproot and to plant" (Jer 1:10), and at 15:13 "every plant my Father did not plant will be uprooted." A famous patristic reading takes the parable as forbidding the killing of heretics: to draw the sword on them would bring endless war, and many saints would fall with them — and some darnel may yet become wheat. The same tradition allowed that restraint short of death was not forbidden. In the fifth century the parable was turned against the Donatists' pure-church ideal; a dispensational reading holds that the field is not the church at all but the professing sphere of the kingdom, while still affirming church discipline. ↗
  14. "Collect first" (v. 30). Syllegō, the sorting verb, is rendered "collect" to keep it distinct from synagō, "gather" — flagged as a new standing rendering. The order "darnel first" is used in some dispensational arguments about the timing of the rapture relative to the second coming; on the parable's own terms it may simply be how a weed-choked field was harvested. Therismos, "harvest," and theristēs, "reaper," share one root and one tag.
  15. The mustard tree (v. 32). Mustard was proverbially the smallest seed a farmer sowed (cf. 17:20); black mustard can reach two or three metres — "becomes a tree" is the parable's deliberate exaggeration. Ezekiel 17:23 is theologically the richest background (God himself plants a tender shoot on Israel's mountain and every bird rests in its shade), though its Greek says "rest," not "nest." Kataskēnoō appears elsewhere in Matthew only at 8:20 — "the birds of the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head" — and in the Greek Old Testament it is God's own verb: "I will dwell in your midst … and many nations will flee to the LORD" (Zech 2:10–11). ↗
  16. Leaven (v. 33). Leaven was banned at Passover (Exod 12) and from altar grain offerings (Lev 2:11); Jesus will warn of "the leaven of the Pharisees" (16:6–12); Paul's "a little leaven leavens the whole lump" is negative (1 Cor 5:6; Gal 5:9). A dispensational reading takes the leaven here as corruption spreading through the professing kingdom. Against that: the parable is the twin of the mustard seed, and paired parables make the same point; and the one leavened offering the Torah commands is the harvest firstfruits of the Feast of Weeks — two loaves "baked leavened … firstfruits to the LORD" (Lev 23:17). Gideon's offering to the angel (Judg 6:19) and Hannah's at Shiloh (1 Sam 1:24) are an ephah, which is three seahs. ↗
  17. Psalm 78 (v. 35). The psalm's heading assigns it to Asaph, whom 2 Chronicles 29:30 calls "the seer." It retells Israel's history from the exodus so that the next generation will not be "a crooked and rebellious generation, who did not set their heart straight" (78:8), and it ends with God choosing David from the sheepfolds to shepherd Jacob (78:70–72). Ereugomai is the verb of Psalm 19:2 — "day to day pours out speech" — creation's voice. Katabolē, "foundation," literally "a throwing-down," shares its root with parabolē. The reading "through Isaiah the prophet" appears in the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus and some minuscules; a third-century pagan critic used it to charge the evangelist with ignorance, and a fourth-century Latin scholar reported that many copies of his day read it. A copyist is likelier to remove a wrong name than to insert one, which gives "Isaiah" some claim to be the earlier reading; most editions print "the prophet." ↗
  18. Into the house (v. 36). Diasapheō, "make thoroughly clear," echoes Deuteronomy 1:5 in the Greek Old Testament, where Moses "began to make this Law clear" beyond the Jordan — the learners ask Jesus to do what Moses did with Torah. What sets them apart from the crowd is not instant understanding but coming back to ask.
  19. The field is the world (vv. 38–41). Jesus says outright that the field is the world, not the church. A dispensational reading insists on this and distinguishes world, church and kingdom; an inaugurated-kingdom reading agrees and draws a mission horizon beyond Israel. Both then face verse 41: the lawless are collected "out of his kingdom," so the kingdom in some sense contains them now. Some readers distinguish the Son of Man's present, mixed kingdom (v. 41) from "the kingdom of their Father" at the end (v. 43).
  20. The completion of the age (v. 39). Synteleia tou aiōnos occurs at 13:39, 40, 49; 24:3 ("what will be the sign … of the completion of the age?"); and 28:20 ("I am with y'all all the days until the completion of the age"). A phrase born in a harvest parable becomes the horizon of the Emmanuel promise. Daniel's Greek has related phrases for "the time of the end" (12:4, 13). The root returns at 13:53: Jesus "completed" (etelesen) these parables. ↗
  21. Trip-traps and lawlessness (v. 41). "The trip-traps with the lawless" matches the Hebrew of Zephaniah 1:3 ("the stumbling blocks with the wicked"); the Greek Old Testament lacks the phrase, so any echo runs through the Hebrew. Anomia, "lawlessness," returns from "depart from me, y'all who work lawlessness" (7:23, from Ps 6:8) and comes back at 23:28 and 24:12 — in Matthew, the failure to do the Law as Jesus fulfills it (5:17–20).
  22. Weeping and grinding (v. 42). Matthew's signature refrain: 8:12; 13:42, 50; 22:13; 24:51; 25:30 (once in Luke, never in Mark). In the Psalms the wicked "grinds his teeth" in fury and frustration (Ps 112:10; 35:16; 37:12) — rage and loss together.
  23. Hidden treasure (v. 44). Burying valuables was common in a land often crossed by armies; rabbinic law had rules for found property (Mishnah, Bava Metzi'a 2), and the finder's quiet purchase has troubled readers — the parable uses one trait, total joyful commitment, and does not comment on the rest. Wisdom is sought "as hidden treasures" (Prov 2:4, with the verbs seek and treasure). Contrast Achan, whose stolen silver lay "hidden in the ground" in his tent (Josh 7:21) — treasure hidden and exposed versus treasure hidden and bought. Most readers take the finder as anyone who discovers the kingdom; a dispensational reading takes the finder as Christ buying the world at the cross to obtain a treasure identified as Jewish believers, with the pearl (from the sea) as Gentiles. The parallel with 19:21, where the one who sells all and gains treasure is plainly the would-be follower, weighs against that. ↗
  24. The pearl (v. 46). Margaritēs appears elsewhere in Matthew only at 7:6: "do not throw y'all's pearls before the pigs." The precious thing not to waste there is the precious thing everything is sold for here. Later reuse: the city's gates, each a single pearl (Rev 21:21).
  25. The dragnet (v. 47). Sagēnē, a weighted seine drawn in a wide arc and hauled ashore, occurs only here in the New Testament. The fish thread (halieus, "fisher," 4:19) is tagged on "dragnet" by ruling, though the verse contains no halieus — the scene is the fishers' work at its end, and the tag marks the payoff the thread was always heading toward. The thread's standing tension — fishing as judgment (Hab 1; Jer 16:16) and as restoration (Ezek 47) — is exactly the dragnet's: it gathers every kind, then sorts. Ezekiel 26:5, 14 (seines drying on ruined Tyre) sit on the judgment side. ↗
  26. Filled full; the beach (v. 48). Eplērōthē, "was filled full," is the formula-quotation verb of 13:35, tagged fulfill. Aigialos, "beach," occurs in Matthew only here and at 13:2, framing the discourse; the only other gospel use is John 21:4, where the risen Jesus stands on the beach while his learners haul a net. Sapros, "rotten, worthless," is the word for the rotten tree of 7:17–18 and 12:33.
  27. The scribe made a learner (v. 52). Oikodespotēs, "master of the house," links back to 13:27 and to 10:25, where the master of the house is Jesus — the learner has become like his master. "Throws out of his treasure-store" closely repeats 12:35; the storehouse is the heart (12:34). Thēsauros is rendered "treasure-store" here to keep the English root shared with "treasure" (v. 44). New before old echoes the lover's store in Song 7:13 — "new and old, my beloved, I have kept for you" — a shared image rather than shared vocabulary. Many readers have suspected the evangelist is sketching himself: a scribe made a learner, bringing out old texts and new teaching. Attractive and unprovable. ↗
  28. The formula (v. 53). The third of Matthew's five discourse endings (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1), and the only one that names its content as "parables." Metairō, "move on," occurs in the New Testament only here and at 19:1. The discourse about the completion of the age is itself "completed" (teleō).
